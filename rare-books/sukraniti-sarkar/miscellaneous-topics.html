    4.1 Characteristics of Friends¶

    1-2.1 Now 1 shall say briefly the chapter of miscellaneous topics. Now you will have to hear briefly of the characteristics of friends, etc.

    65-66. The separation of enemies is that which is due to making their friends powerless.

    67-69. A punishment applied to enemies is said to be the attack upon them, their wealth and grains by robbers, the aggression upon them with powerful force after noticing their weak points, and not ceasing from war when war has commenced.

    103.8 According to the dictates of Srwuri the execution of bad men is real ahimsa, i.e., mercy.

    104-105. One is deserted by good people and acquires sins by always not punishing those who ought to be punished, and punishing those who ought not, and by being a severe punisher.

    106-107. It has been said in the Sistras by sages that great virtue arises out of small charity because that is an encouragement to charity and the result of punishments is fear.

    108-169. Can virtue that is begotten of horse sacrifices come out of mere recital of hymns? So also can the virtue arising from mercy come out of punishinents?

    112-115.9 There was no punishment in the Satya Yuga or the Golden Age because the king then possessed virtue in its entirety. There was full punishment in the Tretd Yuga as the subjects had vice to quarter of its amount. In Dwdpara Yuga punishment to the extent of three quarters of its amount was necessary as virtue existed only to half its extent. In Kali Yuga punishment to half its extent is desirable as the subjects are poor and miserable through the king's wickedness.

    It thus appears that according to the political morality of the age of Sulraniti there is no horror of bloodshed. Sukriachiryya while advocating punishments and warfares cannot naturally shrink at the sight of blood. So he justifies capital punishments by reference to the Vedas.

    116-117.10 The king is the maker of the age as the promulgator of duties and sins. The faultsare to be ascribed neither to the age nor to the subjects but to the king.

    118-119. Men practise that by which the king is satisfied—why should they not follow his teachings through greed or fear ?

    120-121.11 Where the king is virtuous people are also virtuous, where the king is a sinner subjects are also vicious.

    122-123. Where the clouds do not pour rain in season there the lands are not productive, the commonwealth deteriorates, enemies are increased and wealth is destroyed.

    124-125. Even the king who is a drunkard is good but not he who is very angry and addicted to women. For the severe man irritates the subjects and the other destroys the castes.

    126. ‘The man who drinks wine is deprived of his intelligence and loses his business.

    127-129. Passion and anger are of the nature of liquor and greater intoxicants than wine, The king through excessive greed destroys the wealth and life of subjects, so the king should administer the State by giving up these three.

    130-131. The king should punish Lis own subjects by being mild internally but cruel externally, and should be severe in punishment towards those who are by nature evil-doers.

    132-133. The State is naturally ruined by informers who whisper into the king's ears ; so the king, even when informed by these, should carefully study affairs.

    134-135. The wise king notices his own faults as well as those of the subjects—and employs himself first, and then the servants.

    136-137. Offences are of four kinds—that through the body, that through speech, that through the mind, and that through association. Lach again is two-fold, committed voluntarily or involuntarily.

    138. Each again is two-fold —known and approved.

    139. [ach again is four-fold: temporary, constant, habitual, and natural.

    140-143.     144-146.12 The good man committing first offence deserves the following punishment. One should ask of him: “Is this yowr evil action ? Is it proper for you ?’ He deserves this sort of reproach twice or thrice.

    147-149. The good man committing second offence (of the second character) deserves the punishment for the first; and this intensified according to gradual increase in magnitude.

    4.2 Treasure¶
