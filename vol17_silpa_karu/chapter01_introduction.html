    Chapter 1: Śilpa and Kāru: Makers and Making in India¶

    From the bead-makers of Harappa to the Dhokra casters of Bastar and the silk weavers of Kāñcīpuram, the Indian subcontinent has one of the longest and best-documented histories of craft production in the world. This volume is about that history and about the knowledge it embodied: how materials were chosen and transformed, how techniques were organised and taught, how objects were traded, and how the people who made them were placed in society. It covers metals, textiles, dyeing and printing, pottery, wood, ivory and stone, bamboo and plant fibres, leather, jewellery, glass, lac and perfume, painting and the arts of the book, festival crafts, and the regional crafts of the present, and it ends with the guilds and craft knowledge, the colonial economy and the revival movements of the twentieth and twenty-first centuries.

    Two cautions are needed at the start. First, most craft knowledge in India was never written down. It was carried in hands, eyes and workshop routines, and passed from parent to child or master to apprentice. Texts in Sanskrit and other languages tell us a good deal about how crafts were classified, taxed and valued, and the technical treatises on architecture and image-making (śilpaśāstra) record proportional systems and procedures; but for most crafts the evidence is archaeological, inscriptional, commercial or ethnographic. Second, the continuity of Indian crafts is real but should not be exaggerated. Techniques such as lost-wax casting or mordant dyeing have very long histories in the subcontinent, but the communities, markets and meanings of craft changed constantly. Phrases such as “five thousand years of unbroken tradition” describe a hope rather than a documented lineage.

    1.1 The Vocabulary of Craft¶

    1.1.1 Śilpa¶

    The Sanskrit word śilpa means skill, art or craft, and also the work produced by skill. In the Vedic Brāhmaṇas the word already refers to works of art and ornament; in classical usage a śilpin is any skilled maker, from a sculptor to a weaver. The technical literature on building and image-making came to be called śilpaśāstra (“the science of craft”), and vāstuśāstra (“the science of building”); these two overlapping bodies of text are discussed below and in Volume 16 of this series.

    The best-known classification of the arts is the list of sixty-four kalās given in the Kāmasūtra (1.3) and explained by its commentator Yaśodhara (13th century). It mixes what we would call fine arts, crafts, accomplishments and games: singing, instrumental music and dance stand beside painting (ālekhya), needlework (sūcīvānakarma), carpentry (takṣaṇa), architecture (vāstuvidyā), the testing of silver (rūpyaparīkṣā), metallurgy (dhātuvāda) and the colouring of gems, alongside riddles, cock-fighting and the teaching of parrots. The list was meant to describe the accomplishments of a cultivated city-dweller (nāgaraka), not the work of professional artisans, and it should not be read as a technical curriculum. It shows, however, that a wide range of making skills was recognised as vidyā, a body of knowledge.

    1.1.2 Kāru¶

    The word kāru has an interesting history. In the Ṛgveda it usually means a singer or poet, the “maker” of hymns. In a well-known humorous hymn (RV 9.112) the poet lists the different aims of different people: the carpenter (takṣan) looks for what is broken, the physician for the injured, the priest for someone who presses soma; and the speaker says that he himself is a kāru, his father a physician, and his mother a grinder of grain (Jamison and Brereton 2014). The passage is valuable evidence that occupations were differentiated in Vedic society, and it suggests that they were not yet necessarily fixed by birth within a family. In later Sanskrit, kāru came to mean an artisan in general; the lexicons treat kāru and śilpin as near-synonyms, and the law-books speak of kārus and śilpins together.

    1.2 Artisans in Vedic Literature¶

    The earliest Indian texts already know a variety of craftsmen. The Ṛgveda mentions the carpenter and the chariot-maker, the smith (karmāra), and the weaving of cloth; it uses craft as a source of images for the poet’s own work, which is “fashioned” like a chariot or “woven” like a garment. A famous verse compares the creator Brahmaṇaspati to a smith who “blew together” the births of the gods like a karmāra at his bellows (RV 10.72.1–2). The hymns to Viśvakarman, “the maker of all” (RV 10.81–82), ask from what wood and what tree heaven and earth were hewn, and picture the one god who has eyes, faces, arms and feet on every side blowing heaven and earth together with his arms and wings (RV 10.81.3–4) (Jamison and Brereton 2014). These cosmological images are discussed in Chapter 2.

    The Yajurveda gives two remarkable lists. The Śatarudrīya litany to Rudra (Vājasaneyi Saṃhitā 16; Taittirīya Saṃhitā 4.5) pays homage, among many others, to carpenters and chariot-makers, potters and smiths. The list of victims for the symbolic “human sacrifice” (puruṣamedha) in book 30 of the Vājasaneyi Saṃhitā names dozens of occupations, among them the jeweller, the goldsmith, the arrow-maker, the bow-maker, the rope-maker, the dyer and the embroiderer; several of these, such as the dyer and the embroiderer, are women. The lists are ritual, not sociological, but they are the earliest Indian record of a highly specialised division of labour.

    Women’s craft work is visible in these early sources. Besides the dyer and the embroiderer of the puruṣamedha list, Vedic texts mention women weaving and stitching, and the image of the weaving woman is used of Night and Dawn, who weave the day. In the Arthaśāstra women spin yarn for the state (Chapter 4); in Buddhist texts, women prepare thread and cloth for the robes of monks and nuns. Throughout Indian history, much of the labour of craft, especially spinning, embroidery, basketry and the preparation of materials, has been done by women, though it has usually been less visible in the sources than the work of men.

    The status of the chariot-maker became a famous problem for ritual theory. Vedic texts allow the rathakāra to set up the sacred fires, in the rainy season. Since the chariot-maker was not one of the three “twice-born” classes, later Mīmāṃsā exegetes debated who exactly the rathakāra was and how he could have this right. From an early date, then, craftsmen occupied an uncertain place in the scheme of the four varṇas.

    1.3 Artisans in Buddhist, Jain and Tamil Sources¶

    The Buddhist canon in Pali, much of it composed in the last centuries BCE, gives a lively picture of the urban and village artisans of the Ganga valley. Its stories mention villages of smiths, carpenters and potters, heads (jeṭṭhaka) of groups of craftsmen, and the “eighteen guilds” (aṭṭhārasa seṇiyo) as a conventional total (Chapter 13). The Buddha’s last meal, according to the Mahāparinibbāna Sutta, was taken in the house of Cunda, a smith’s son (kammāraputta) of Pāvā, whom the Buddha took care to exonerate from any blame for his final illness. The monastic rules (Vinaya) regulate the cloth that monks may accept, listing six permitted materials (linen, cotton, silk, wool, hemp and mixed fibres), and give detailed instructions for cutting and sewing robes in a pattern of patches said to resemble the fields of Magadha (Horner 1951). Buddhist monasteries were also major patrons of builders, carvers and painters, as the rock-cut halls of western India and the paintings of Ajanta show.

    Jain texts approached craft from the point of view of non-violence. The lay vows of the Jain tradition forbid fifteen “occupations that bind karma” (karmādāna), including trades that involve fire and the destruction of living beings, such as charcoal-burning, clearing forests, digging and trading in lac, ivory, poisons and animal products. Such rules helped to steer Jain laymen away from many crafts and towards commerce, banking and the jewellery trade, and Jain merchants became important patrons of temple building, manuscript production and textile trade in western India (Jaini 1979).

    The earliest Tamil poetry, the Caṅkam anthologies of the early centuries CE, describes busy markets and harbours (Ramanujan 1985; Zvelebil 1973). The long poems Maturaikkāñci and Paṭṭiṉappālai picture the streets of Madurai and of the port of Pukār (Kāvirippūmpaṭṭiṉam), crowded with craftsmen, merchants and foreign traders, and Tamil poets often compare fine cloth to the cast-off skin of a snake or to steam or smoke. The fullest early literary picture of an Indian craft city is in the Tamil epic Cilappatikāram, probably composed in the middle of the first millennium CE. Its fifth canto describes the two quarters of Pukār during the festival of Indra: the houses of foreign traders and the warehouses by the sea, and streets in which particular trades lived and worked together, among them weavers of silk, wool and cotton, dealers in perfumes, gems and gold, and workers in metal and wood (Parthasarathy 1993). Its sequel, the Buddhist Maṇimēkalai, is set in the same city. These descriptions are literary, but they agree with archaeological evidence from sites such as Kodumanal, Arikamedu and Alagankulam for bead-making, iron-working and trade in this period.

    1.4 The Archaeological Record: Craft in the Indus Cities¶

    The earliest systematic evidence for specialised craft production in South Asia comes from the cities of the Indus or Harappan civilisation, whose mature phase is usually dated c. 2600–1900 BCE (Allchin and Allchin 1982; Kenoyer 1998). Excavations at Harappa, Mohenjo-daro, Chanhudaro, Dholavira and Lothal have revealed workshops and production debris for a wide range of crafts:

    • Shell working. Bangles and inlays were cut from the conch shell Turbinella pyrum, using bronze saws; the debris allows the whole sequence of production to be reconstructed.

    • Stone beads. Long carnelian beads were drilled with specially made stone drills, and carnelian was bleached and “etched” with white designs. Etched carnelian beads of Indus type have been found in Mesopotamia, including the royal tombs at Ur.

    • Faience and stoneware. Indus craftsmen made glazed faience beads, figurines and seals, and a very hard fired stoneware used for bangles. Faience was made earlier in Egypt and Mesopotamia; the Indus industry was a sophisticated regional tradition rather than the world’s first.

    • Metalwork. Copper and bronze were cast and hammered into tools, vessels and ornaments. The bronze “dancing girl” from Mohenjo-daro was cast by the lost-wax method.

    • Weights and seals. Standardised cubical stone weights and carved steatite seals point to an organised system of exchange and control.

    Kenoyer’s work at Harappa shows that many of these crafts were practised by specialists in particular parts of the city, and that some products, such as stoneware bangles and fine seals, were made under close control (Kenoyer 1997). The Indus evidence also includes some of the earliest cotton cloth and some of the earliest silk in the Old World (Chapter 4).

    Scholarly Note: Lothal: Dock or Reservoir?

    The large brick-lined basin at Lothal in Gujarat, about 214–219 metres long and 35–37 metres wide (published figures vary), was interpreted by its excavator S. R. Rao as a dockyard for ships coming from the Gulf of Khambhat. Some archaeologists regard the basin instead as a water reservoir or tank; its function — dock or reservoir — remains debated. A seal of Persian Gulf type found at Lothal and the many Indus objects found in the Gulf and Mesopotamia show that there was sea-borne trade, but “the world’s first dockyard” should be treated as a hypothesis, not a fact.

    The lost-wax casting of the Mohenjo-daro bronzes is often linked with the lost-wax casting still practised by Dhokra smiths in central and eastern India. The technique is the same in principle, but there is no documented chain of transmission over four thousand years, and lost-wax casting was practised in many parts of the ancient world. It is better to speak of a long history of the technique in the subcontinent than of an unbroken lineage of craftsmen.

    1.5 Texts on Craft¶

    1.5.1 The Arthaśāstra¶

    The Arthaśāstra attributed to Kauṭilya is a composite work, probably compiled between about 50 BCE and 300 CE (its attribution to Cāṇakya, minister of Candragupta Maurya c. 300 BCE, is traditional) (Olivelle 2013). Its second book describes the duties of a series of superintendents, several of whom control craft production: the superintendent of mines (2.12), who also runs metal workshops; the superintendent of gold (2.13) and the state goldsmith (2.14); the superintendent of forest produce (2.17); and the superintendent of yarn (2.23), who organises spinning by women and weaving in state workshops. Another chapter (2.11) describes the precious goods to be received into the treasury, including pearls, gems, diamonds, coral, sandalwood and fine textiles, with notes on their sources and qualities. Book 4 opens with a chapter on “keeping watch over artisans” (kārukarakṣaṇa), which regulates the dealings of weavers, washermen, goldsmiths and other craftsmen with their customers and fixes penalties for fraud (Kangle 1960–1965; Olivelle 2013). The text thus gives a precise picture of the state’s interest in craft: taxation, quality control, the prevention of fraud and the supply of the royal household and army.

    1.5.2 The Śilpaśāstra Literature¶

    The technical literature on architecture and image-making developed from the middle of the first millennium CE. Among the most important works are:

    Bṛhatsaṃhitā of Varāhamihira (6th century CE)
    An encyclopaedia of omens and natural knowledge with chapters on houses, temples, images, the selection of timber, gems, and perfumes (Bhat 1981–1982).
    Mayamata (c. 9th–12th century)
    A South Indian treatise on houses, temples, towns and images, important for its description of the master builder and his team; its editor and translator, Bruno Dagens, places it in the Tamil country between the ninth and the twelfth century (Dagens 1994).
    Mānasāra (medieval; date disputed)
    A South Indian treatise in seventy chapters on architecture and image-making, edited and translated by P. K. Acharya, who dated it to c. 500–700 CE; most later scholars place it after the Mayamata, though no firm date can be given (Acharya 1934).
    Samarāṅgaṇasūtradhāra (11th century)
    An architectural treatise attributed to King Bhoja of Dhārā.
    Mānasollāsa (c. 1131 CE)
    A royal encyclopaedia composed for the Cālukya king Someśvara III, in five sections of twenty chapters each, covering kingship, the royal household, pleasures and arts (Shrigondekar 1925–1961).
    Aparājitapṛcchā (12th–13th century)
    A western Indian treatise in the Māru-Gurjara tradition, attributed to Bhuvanadeva.
    Viśvakarmaprakāśa
    A popular text on house-building attributed to Viśvakarman, of uncertain date.
    Śilparatna of Śrīkumāra (16th century)
    A Kerala treatise on architecture, image-making and painting, relevant to the timber architecture of Kerala (Chapter 7).

    These texts were written by and for learned specialists, and how far working builders and sculptors used them is debated. Stella Kramrisch, whose The Hindu Temple (1946) remains the classic synthesis, emphasised that in these texts measurement, ritual and meaning are inseparable: the proportional grid of a temple is also a map of the cosmos (Kramrisch 1946). Her later essay on “Traditions of the Indian Craftsman” stresses the craftsman’s dependence on received forms and on the workshop rather than on books (Kramrisch 1958). Most crafts, such as weaving, dyeing, pottery and smithing, have no comparable Sanskrit literature at all.

    1.6 Craft in Indian Thought¶

    Craft supplied Indian philosophers with some of their most familiar examples. The Chāndogya Upaniṣad (6.1.4–6) explains how knowledge of a single underlying reality gives knowledge of all its forms by three craft analogies: by one lump of clay everything made of clay is known, the modification being “a name, a matter of words”, while “clay” alone is real; likewise with a copper (or gold) ornament and with an iron nail-cutter. The example of the pot and the clay became a standard illustration of the relation between cause and effect in Sāṃkhya and Vedānta. The Sāṃkhyakārikā (67) compares the liberated person who continues to live to a potter’s wheel that keeps turning after the potter has stopped. These are philosophical uses of craft, not statements about the status of craftsmen; but they show how familiar craft processes were to the thinkers who used them.

    The Bhagavad Gītā defines yoga as “skill in action” (yogaḥ karmasu kauśalam, BG 2.50). In context the phrase means the skill of acting without attachment to the results of action, not craftsmanship in the ordinary sense; but modern writers on Indian craft, from Coomaraswamy onwards, have often applied it to the artisan’s absorbed attention to work (Coomaraswamy 1909). The Gītā’s teaching that it is better to do one’s own duty (svadharma) badly than another’s well (BG 3.35), and its account of the duties of the four varṇas (BG 18.41–48), have a more ambiguous legacy: they could give dignity to every kind of work, but they were also read as sanctioning a social order in which occupation was fixed by birth.

    1.6.1 The Six Limbs of Painting¶

    The clearest piece of classical theory about the visual arts is the list of six “limbs” (ṣaḍaṅga) of painting quoted by Yaśodhara in his commentary on the Kāmasūtra’s list of arts: rūpabheda (the distinction of forms), pramāṇa (proportion), bhāva (expression of feeling), lāvaṇyayojana (the infusion of grace), sādṛśya (likeness) and varṇikābhaṅga (the handling of colour). The Viṣṇudharmottara Purāṇa, whose third book contains a substantial treatise on painting and image-making (the Citrasūtra), discusses similar qualities. These lists apply to painting and sculpture, but they suggest the standards by which skilled work in general was judged: correct proportion and form, expressive life, and beauty.

    1.7 Tools and Workshops¶

    Indian craftsmen worked mostly with simple tools, used with great skill. The carpenter’s adze, saw, chisel and bow-drill; the smith’s bellows of goatskin or bamboo, tongs, hammers and anvil; the potter’s wheel, paddle and anvil; the weaver’s loom, shuttle and reed; the bow-lathe, turned with one hand by a bow while the other hand guides the tool, used by turners and toy-makers; the goldsmith’s blowpipe, crucible and touchstone; the jeweller’s bow-drill and polishing wheel; the block-printer’s carved blocks and the painter’s brushes of squirrel hair: these were the basic equipment of Indian crafts. Many tools were made or maintained by the craftsmen themselves, and many were treated with respect and worshipped once a year (Chapter 2).

    Workshops were usually in or beside the home, and work often took place sitting on the ground, with the feet used as well as the hands to hold or operate tools. Western visitors were often struck by the contrast between the simplicity of the equipment and the refinement of the product. The contrast is misleading if it suggests that technique did not matter: the sophistication of Indian crafts lay less in machinery than in the organisation of processes, the knowledge of materials and the skill of hands trained from childhood.

    1.8 Artisans and Caste¶

    Any honest account of Indian crafts must deal with caste. For most of the last two thousand years, the great majority of crafts were practised by hereditary occupational groups (jāti), each associated with a particular occupation, marrying within itself, and ranked within a local hierarchy. Potters, smiths, carpenters, weavers, oil-pressers, washermen, barbers, leather-workers and many others formed distinct jātis, though the names, ranks and occupations of these groups varied greatly from region to region and changed over time (Bayly 1999).

    1.8.1 What the Law-Books Say¶

    The Dharmaśāstras both recognised artisans and ranked them low. Manu (c. 2nd century CE) allows the king to exact one day’s labour a month from artisans and craftsmen instead of taxes (Manu 7.138), and declares that “the hand of an artisan is always pure” while he is at work (Manu 5.129), a practical rule that allowed artisans’ products to be used by people of higher rank (Olivelle 2005). The same text includes smiths, goldsmiths, basket-makers, washermen and others among the people whose food a Brahmin should not accept (Manu 4.210–219), and in its account of the “mixed” castes (Manu 10) it assigns occupations such as leather-work, carpentry and fishing to groups born of unions between varṇas. The law-books also recognised the customs of guilds and occupational groups as binding on their members (Chapter 13).

    1.8.2 Untouchability and Craft¶

    The lowest place in this hierarchy was given to groups whose work involved death, dirt or animal remains: leather-workers and tanners, scavengers, sweepers, and in some regions weavers of coarse cloth, basket-makers and drummers. Communities such as the Chamārs and Mochīs of north India, the Mādigas of Andhra and Karnataka, the Cakkiliyars (Aruntatiyars) of Tamil Nadu and the Dhors and Chāmbhārs of Maharashtra were treated as untouchable, excluded from temples, wells and schools, and subjected to violence. B. R. Ambedkar’s Annihilation of Caste (1936) remains the most forceful critique of the idea that caste is merely a rational division of labour; in his words, the caste system “is not merely division of labour. It is also a division of labourers” (Ambedkar 1936). Chapter 13 treats untouchability, the historians’ critique of the “polluting occupation” stereotype and the artisans’ own responses more fully.

    1.8.3 Artisans’ Own Traditions¶

    Artisans did not simply accept the places assigned to them. The Viśvakarma communities of the South (the Kammāḷar of Tamil Nadu and Kerala, the Pāñcāla of Karnataka and Andhra), who comprise smiths, carpenters, bronze-casters, stone-carvers and goldsmiths, claim descent from the divine craftsman Viśvakarman and a Brahmanical status of their own, and have long disputed the precedence of Brahmins (Chapter 2) (Brouwer 1995). Potters in north India call themselves Prajāpati after the creator. Many of the most important poet-saints of the bhakti movements were artisans, from Kabīr the weaver to Raidās the leather-worker, and their songs speak with the authority of working people (Chapter 13). These traditions should be represented on their own terms, as part of India’s craft heritage.

    1.9 Art and Craft¶

    Modern English distinguishes “art”, made by individual artists for contemplation, from “craft”, made by artisans for use. The distinction took shape in Europe in the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries, and colonial institutions in India applied it, classifying textiles, metalwork and pottery as “industrial arts” or “art manufactures” and placing them below painting and sculpture (Chapter 14). Indian languages did not divide the field in the same way: śilpa covered the work of the temple sculptor and the weaver alike, and the sixty-four kalās mixed painting with needlework and carpentry. Ananda Coomaraswamy argued that in traditional societies there was no distinction between fine art and craft, and that every made thing was meant to be both useful and beautiful (Coomaraswamy 1934). The argument is attractive, but the absence of a single word for “fine art” did not mean the absence of hierarchy. Indian society ranked different kinds of makers very differently, and the sculptor who carved temple images or the goldsmith who served a king enjoyed a status very different from that of the basket-maker or the tanner.

    The present volume uses “craft” in a broad sense, for all skilled making by hand, and avoids treating any one craft as more “artistic” than another. Painting and sculpture as arts are treated in other volumes of this series; here they appear as crafts, with attention to materials, techniques and the people who practised them.

    1.10 Craft and Environment¶

    Every craft depended on local materials: clays, ores, fuels, fibres, dye plants, woods, grasses and water. Craft traditions were therefore closely adapted to their environments, and changes in land use, forest law, water supply and agriculture have often affected them more than changes in fashion. It is sometimes claimed that traditional crafts were inherently “sustainable”. Many were indeed economical in their use of energy and materials, reusing and recycling waste; but some, such as iron smelting and lime and brick burning, consumed large quantities of wood and charcoal, and others, such as tanning, dyeing and metalworking, produced pollution and exposed workers to harm. A realistic history of crafts must consider both their ecological wisdom and their costs.

    1.11 Sources for the History of Crafts¶

    Because craftspeople rarely wrote about their own work, the historian of Indian crafts must piece together evidence of many kinds. Archaeology provides excavated objects, workshops, kilns, slag heaps and debris, analysed by scientific methods and dated by radiocarbon; it supplies most of the evidence for the Indus period and much for later periods, including the zinc retorts of Zawar and the printed cottons of Fustat. Surviving objects (textiles, jewellery, metalwork and other things preserved in temples, palaces, families and museums, many of them now outside India) show what was made and for whom. Inscriptions name craftsmen and guilds as donors, list artisans’ rights and duties in temples, and record grants on copper plates.

    Texts in Sanskrit, Pali, Prakrit, Tamil, Persian and other languages describe, regulate, classify and praise crafts, though seldom from the craftsman’s point of view. Greek, Chinese, Arab, Persian and European travellers wrote about what they saw, and from the seventeenth century the vast archives of the European trading companies record prices, qualities, quantities and the organisation of production. Around 1900 district gazetteers, census reports, industrial surveys and provincial monographs on particular crafts described crafts and craft communities through the categories of colonial administration, and anthropologists have continued the work since. Last, the knowledge of present-day craftspeople can often explain techniques that texts and objects leave obscure, and archaeologists increasingly study living crafts, such as bead-making at Khambhat or village pottery, to interpret ancient evidence.

    Each kind of source has its biases: texts reflect the views of elites, company records the interests of merchants, colonial surveys the assumptions of administrators. The history of crafts is therefore always partly a reconstruction.

    1.12 Craft, Trade and the Wider World¶

    Indian craft products travelled very far. Carnelian beads of Indus type reached Mesopotamia in the third millennium BCE. The Periplus of the Erythraean Sea, a Greek merchant’s handbook of the mid-first century CE, lists Indian cotton cloth of many grades, including the “Gangetic” muslins of Bengal, and Indian iron and steel, among the exports of Indian ports (Casson 1989). Glass beads made at Arikamedu and other South Indian sites were traded across the Indian Ocean (Francis 2002). Block-printed Indian cottons of the tenth to fifteenth centuries have been excavated in large numbers in Egypt (Barnes 1997), and Indian textiles were the principal medium of exchange for spices in the Indonesian archipelago (Guy 1998). From the seventeenth century the European trading companies carried Indian cottons to Europe, West Africa and the Americas (Riello and Roy 2009; Riello 2013). Table 1.1 summarises these exports by period. These trades are surveyed in the chapters that follow and, for maritime commerce, in Volume 19.

    Table 1.1. Some Indian craft exports in long-distance trade (approximate periods)
    Period Products Destinations and evidence
    c. 2600–1900 BCE Carnelian beads, shell, other Indus goods Mesopotamia and the Gulf (excavated objects, cuneiform texts on Meluhha)
    1st century CE Cotton cloth, muslins, iron and steel, gems, pepper Red Sea and Roman Egypt (Periplus)
    c. 2nd century BCE onwards Glass beads Southeast Asia and East Africa (excavated beads)
    10th–15th centuries Block-printed and resist-dyed cottons (mainly Gujarat) Egypt and the Red Sea (Fustat, Quseir al-Qadim)
    14th–18th centuries Paṭolā, printed and painted cottons Southeast Asia (heirloom textiles in Indonesia)
    17th–18th centuries Calicoes, chintz, muslins Europe, West Africa, the Americas (company records)

    How large was this craft economy? Economic historians agree that before the nineteenth century South Asia was one of the world’s great manufacturing regions, and that its share of world manufacturing fell drastically in the colonial period; but the figures usually quoted are rough estimates whose basis is discussed in Chapter 14.

    1.13 An Outline History¶

    The chapters that follow are organised by material and theme. A brief chronological outline may help the reader to place them.

    Before c. 1900 BCE
    Neolithic and Chalcolithic villages of Baluchistan and the Indus region (Mehrgarh) develop pottery, copper working, cotton and bead-making; the mature Harappan cities (c. 2600–1900 BCE) support highly specialised crafts and long-distance trade.
    c. 1900–600 BCE
    Regional Chalcolithic and early Iron Age cultures; Vedic texts describe carpenters, chariot-makers, smiths and weavers; iron comes into common use.
    c. 600 BCE–300 CE
    The second urbanisation of the Ganga valley and the Deccan; coinage; guilds; Buddhist and Jain monasteries as patrons; the Arthaśāstra; Indian Ocean trade with the Roman world, Southeast Asia and East Africa; crucible steel and glass beads in the south.
    c. 300–1200
    Gupta and post-Gupta kingdoms; the growth of temple architecture and image-making; the śilpaśāstras; land grants on copper plates; regional kingdoms in the south (Pallava, Cola) and east (Pāla); the beginnings of zinc distillation; Indian textiles traded to Egypt and Southeast Asia.
    c. 1200–1550
    The Delhi Sultanate and regional sultanates introduce or spread paper, the spinning wheel, new weaving and dyeing techniques, glazed tiles and Persian design; Gujarat and the Coromandel coast dominate Indian Ocean textile trade; Vijayanagara in the south.
    c. 1550–1750
    The Mughal empire and Rajput, Deccani and southern courts; imperial workshops; carpets, jewellery and painting at their height; European companies begin large-scale export of Indian textiles.
    c. 1750–1947
    The East India Company and the British Raj; coercion of weavers; competition from British factory goods; decline and adaptation of crafts; colonial collecting and art schools; Svadeśī and khādī.
    After 1947
    State support for handicrafts and handlooms; the craft revival; design schools; Geographical Indications; globalisation and new markets.

    1.14 The Structure of This Volume¶

    The book moves from ideas about making to materials, then to places, and finally to the institutions and history that shaped the makers’ lives. Chapter 2 follows Viśvakarman, the divine craftsman, from the Vedic hymns to the festival of Viśvakarma Pūjā and the artisan communities who claim him as ancestor. Chapters 3 to 11 are organised by material and process. Metals come first (Chapter 3): bronze-casting and the image-maker’s workshop, iron and the Delhi pillar, crucible steel, zinc, gold and silver, Bidrī ware and brass. Two chapters on textiles follow, the first on fibres, looms, the regional woven traditions, embroidery, garments and their makers (Chapter 4), the second on dyeing, printing, the chintz trade and Indian cloth in Asia, Africa and the Atlantic world (Chapter 5). Pottery and terracotta (Chapter 6) are followed by the hard materials that are cut and carved, wood, ivory and stone, including the makers of musical instruments (Chapter 7), and by bamboo, cane and the other plant fibres (Chapter 8). Leather has a chapter of its own (Chapter 9), because its makers were placed at the bottom of the caste order and its history shows most clearly the theme of this book. Chapter 10 treats the crafts of adornment: jewellery and gems, beads, glass, lac and perfume. Chapter 11 treats painting as a craft together with the arts of the book, and the crafts of festivals, toys, games and masks.

    Chapter 12 surveys living crafts region by region, with a brief look at Sri Lanka, Nepal, Bangladesh and Pakistan, and its closing table serves as a finding aid to the rest of the book. The last three chapters are analytical. Chapter 13 examines guilds, caste, the organisation of production, the nature of craft knowledge and the notations artisans devised, the relation between craft and science, and the work of women and children. Chapter 14 treats crafts in the colonial economy, including the debate on “deindustrialisation”, the myth of the muslin weavers’ thumbs and the making of khādī, and Chapter 15 the revival, protection and present condition of the crafts, including the design schools. Line diagrams of the main processes (the loom, resist-dyeing, the chintz sequence, lost-wax casting, crucible steel and zinc, the potter’s wheel and firing, and tanning) accompany the chapters that describe them. Photographs show representative objects, from a Harappan storage jar and a Cola bronze to a Kashmir shawl and a box spinning wheel, and some of the monuments discussed; their sources are listed under Image Credits at the end of the volume.
