    Chapter 2: Viśvakarman: The Divine Craftsman and His Communities¶

    In many workshops of India, from engineering works in Howrah to goldsmiths’ shops in Kerala, an image or print of Viśvakarman, the divine craftsman, receives worship once a year, and tools and machines are garlanded in his honour. Viśvakarman (in modern Indian languages usually Viśvakarmā) is the patron deity of craftsmen across much of India, and the ancestor claimed by several large artisan communities. This chapter follows the figure from the Vedic hymns, where “Viśvakarman” is a name for the creator, through the epics and Purāṇas, where he is the architect of the gods, to the religious and social life of the communities who claim him as their own.

    2.1 Viśvakarman and Tvaṣṭṛ in the Veda¶

    2.1.1 The Viśvakarman Hymns¶

    The name Viśvakarman means “maker of all” or “whose work is everything”. In the Ṛgveda it is used as an epithet of Indra and the Sun, and in two late hymns of the tenth book (RV 10.81 and 10.82) it names the creator himself. These hymns are among the earliest Indian speculations on the origin of the world, and they think about creation in the language of craft (Jamison and Brereton 2014):

    Ṛgveda 10.81.3

    viśvataścakṣur uta viśvatomukho
    viśvatobāhur uta viśvataspāt
    sam bāhubhyāṃ dhamati sam patatrair
    dyāvābhūmī janayan deva ekaḥ

    “With eyes on every side and faces on every side, with arms on every side and feet on every side, the one god, generating heaven and earth, blows them together with his arms and with his wings.”

    The verb sam dhamati, “blows together”, is the smith’s word for working the bellows (the same verb is used of the smith in RV 10.72.2), and the “wings” may be the fans used to blow a fire. The next verse asks a carpenter’s question:

    Ṛgveda 10.81.4

    kiṃ svid vanaṃ ka u sa vṛkṣa āsa
    yato dyāvāpṛthivī niṣṭatakṣuḥ

    “What was the wood, and what the tree, from which they fashioned heaven and earth?”

    The verb takṣ-, “to fashion, to hew”, is the root of takṣan, the carpenter. Creation is imagined as the work of a smith and a carpenter; the questions are left open, as befits the speculative hymns of the late Ṛgveda.

    2.1.2 Tvaṣṭṛ and the Ṛbhus¶

    In the Ṛgveda the craftsman of the gods par excellence is Tvaṣṭṛ, “the fashioner”. He forges Indra’s mace, the vajra (RV 1.32.2), and he is the god who shapes forms (rūpa) in the womb; a hymn for conception asks that Tvaṣṭṛ “fashion the forms” of the child (RV 10.184.1). Beside him stand the three Ṛbhus, divine artisans who became immortal through their skill: they renewed their aged parents, fashioned a chariot for the Aśvins and a cow that gave milk, and made four cups out of Tvaṣṭṛ’s single cup (RV 1.161 and the Ṛbhu hymns of the fourth book). In later tradition Tvaṣṭṛ and Viśvakarman are often identified.

    The Ṛbhus are especially interesting as divine artisans who were not born gods but earned immortality and a share in the sacrifice through their craftsmanship. They are called “skilful” and “good workers”, and their achievements are all craft achievements: the chariot, the cup, the cow, the renewal of their parents. In the ritual, they receive their share of soma at the third pressing of the day. Some scholars have seen in them a reflection of the honour in which skilled craftsmen were held in early Vedic society; others emphasise that they remain, in the hymns, figures of slightly lower rank than the great gods, admitted to the sacrifice only late (Jamison and Brereton 2014). Either way, the Vedic poets of the Ṛbhu hymns treated the making of things, like the making of hymns, as an activity of the highest significance.

    2.2 The Architect of the Gods¶

    In the epics and Purāṇas Viśvakarman becomes the architect and engineer of the gods. According to the Viṣṇu Purāṇa (1.15.118–119) he is the son of Prabhāsa, the eighth of the Vasus, and of Yogasiddhā, the sister of Bṛhaspati (Mani 1975). His daughter Saṃjñā married the Sun; unable to bear her husband’s brilliance, she fled, and Viśvakarman, according to the same Purāṇa (3.2.2–12), trimmed the Sun on his lathe and from the filings made the discus of Viṣṇu, the trident of Śiva and other divine weapons.

    The works attributed to Viśvakarman in the epics and Purāṇas include the city of Laṅkā, the aerial chariot Puṣpaka, and Kṛṣṇa’s city of Dvārakā; in some versions he fashions Indra’s thunderbolt from the bones of the sage Dadhīca. In the Rāmāyaṇa the bridge to Laṅkā is built by the monkey Nala, “son of Viśvakarman”. In the Mahābhārata the wonderful assembly-hall of the Pāṇḍavas, with its floors that look like water and its pools that look like floors, is built not by Viśvakarman but by Maya, the architect of the Dānavas. These stories express admiration for skilled building on a heroic scale; they are not, as is sometimes claimed, coded records of ancient aircraft or other lost technologies.

    In Pali literature the divine craftsman appears as Vissakamma, a god (devaputta) who builds palaces and other works for Sakka (Indra), the king of the gods, and who in some stories descends to earth to build for kings and holy men. Jain narratives describe the gods building the assembly-hall (samavasaraṇa) in which a Tīrthaṅkara preaches after attaining omniscience, a wonderful circular structure of walls, gateways and terraces that is often represented in Jain painting and sculpture. With the spread of Indian religions, the divine craftsman travelled to Southeast Asia: in Thailand, Phra Witsanukam (Viśvakarman) is revered as the patron of craftsmen and builders.

    2.2.1 The Person of the Site¶

    The Bṛhatsaṃhitā tells how the vāstupuruṣa came to lie beneath every building. A formless being, blocking heaven and earth, was seized and pressed face down to the ground by the gods, who remained seated on its limbs; Brahmā placed it there as the “person of the site”, to be honoured whenever a building is laid out (Bhat 1981–1982). Each god presides over the square of the grid on which he sits, and the builder must avoid placing pillars or walls on the vulnerable points (marman) of the body. The myth links the measuring and dividing of the ground, the first task of the builder, with the cosmic ordering of chaos by the gods.

    2.2.2 The Craftsman’s Vow¶

    The śilpaśāstras present the work of the image-maker as a ritual discipline. The craftsman was to begin work on an auspicious day, purify himself, worship the deity of the image and his own tools, and visualise the form described in the meditation verses (dhyāna-śloka) of the deity before carving or modelling it. These prescriptions are ideals stated in learned texts; they are also, in modified form, part of the living practice of many sthapati families, who worship their tools and begin major work with rituals.

    2.3 Viśvakarman in the Śilpaśāstras¶

    The treatises on architecture and image-making present themselves as revealed by divine or semi-divine authors: the Mayamata by Maya, other texts by Viśvakarman, Kāśyapa or Agastya. In this tradition Viśvakarman is the archetypal sthapati, and craft knowledge descends from him.

    2.3.1 The Master Builder and His Team¶

    The Mayamata and the Mānasāra describe the qualifications of the master builder and a team of four craftsmen (Dagens 1994; Acharya 1934):

    Sthapati
    The master builder, who must know the treatises, be skilled in all the crafts and of good character, and who directs the whole work.
    Sūtragrāhin
    “He who holds the cord”, the surveyor and draughtsman, often the son or pupil of the sthapati.
    Takṣaka
    The carver, who cuts wood or stone to size.
    Vardhakin
    The joiner or assembler, who fits together what the carver has cut.

    The sthapati was also a ritual figure: the building of a temple or the making of an image was punctuated by rites, and the completed image received its life through the rite of “establishing the breath” (prāṇapratiṣṭhā), performed by priests (Kramrisch 1946).

    2.3.2 Measure and Proportion¶

    The śilpaśāstras describe images in terms of a module, the tāla (roughly the length of the face), and classify figures by systems of ten, nine, eight or fewer tālas; the highest gods are typically made in the ten-tāla system. Temples and houses are laid out on grids of squares (pada) governed by the vāstupuruṣamaṇḍala, the diagram of the “person of the site”. The Bṛhatsaṃhitā describes a grid of eighty-one squares with forty-five deities, Brahmā at the centre and the head of the vāstupuruṣa in the north-east (Bhat 1981–1982). These systems are discussed more fully in Volume 16.

    Scholarly Note: Vāstu Today

    Vāstu is today the subject of a large popular literature that presents it as a science of “energies” and makes claims about magnetism, health and prosperity. Such claims have no basis in the classical texts, which are concerned with ritual order, auspiciousness and proportion, nor in modern science. The modern uses and reinventions of vāstu are studied by Chakrabarti (1998); the planning of eighteenth-century Jaipur, often cited as a vāstu city, is examined by Sachdev and Tillotson (2002).

    2.3.3 Texts in the Workshop: Odisha¶

    In at least one region we can see how śilpa texts were used by working craftsmen. In Odisha, families of hereditary stone-carvers kept palm-leaf manuscripts of architectural texts and drawings, which they consulted and copied. The Swiss artist and scholar Alice Boner, working with the Sanskrit scholar Sadāśiva Rath Śarmā, edited and translated one such text, the Śilpa Prakāśa attributed to Rāmacandra Kaulācāra, a manual of temple architecture and ornament in the Odishan tradition; its editors argued for an early medieval date, which others think too early (Boner and Rath Śarmā 1966). Its detailed instructions for laying out temple walls and for drawing figures and ornaments show a close connection between text and practice, and its vocabulary is still partly understood by Odishan carvers. Elsewhere, the relation between the Sanskrit treatises and the knowledge of working builders was looser: builders learned by apprenticeship, and the texts were often known to them through oral tradition and local manuals in regional languages.

    2.3.4 Maya, Architect of the Asuras¶

    Viśvakarman has a counterpart in Maya, the architect of the demons (asuras or dānavas). In the Mahābhārata Maya, saved by Arjuna from the burning of the Khāṇḍava forest, builds the wonderful assembly-hall of the Pāṇḍavas in gratitude; in the Purāṇas he builds the three flying cities of the demons, which Śiva destroys with a single arrow. South Indian tradition made Maya the author of the Mayamata and of other works on architecture and astronomy, and the Sūryasiddhānta, the most important Indian astronomical text, is presented as revealed by the Sun to an Asura named Maya. Maya thus represents a rival, non-Vedic source of craft knowledge, honoured even though he belongs to the enemies of the gods.

    2.4 Viśvakarma Pūjā¶

    The festival of Viśvakarma Pūjā is celebrated in eastern India (Bengal, Odisha, Bihar, Jharkhand, Assam and Tripura) on the day of the Sun’s entry into Virgo (Kanyā saṅkrānti), at the end of the Bengali month of Bhādra, which usually falls on 16, 17 or 18 September. In parts of north India Viśvakarma is worshipped on the day after Dīpāvalī. In south India tools are worshipped on the ninth day of Navarātri (Āyudha Pūjā, Mahānavamī), when craftsmen, students and soldiers honour the instruments of their work.

    On Viśvakarma Pūjā, workshops, factories and garages close; machines and tools are cleaned and decorated; and an image of the god is installed. In Bengal the image is typically a youthful, moustached, four-armed figure riding an elephant, holding tools such as a hammer and a measuring instrument. In much of India the god is shown as an older, bearded figure seated on a goose (haṃsa). Among south Indian Viśvakarma communities a five-faced form, representing the five crafts, is common. The festival has adapted readily to industrial work: it is observed in railway workshops, steel plants, shipyards and among taxi and auto-rickshaw drivers, who decorate their vehicles.

    2.4.1 Āyudha Pūjā¶

    In south India and Maharashtra, the worship of tools and weapons (āyudha pūjā) on the ninth day of Navarātri plays a role similar to that of Viśvakarma Pūjā in the east. Soldiers worship their weapons, farmers their ploughs, craftsmen their tools, students their books and musicians their instruments; today vehicles, computers and machines are cleaned, marked with sandal paste and vermilion, and garlanded. The custom is connected with the worship of the goddess Durgā, who in the myth received her weapons from the gods, and with the epic story in which the Pāṇḍavas recover the weapons they had hidden in a tree during their year in disguise. The festival expresses, in a form shared by many social groups, the idea that the instruments of work deserve respect.

    2.5 The Viśvakarma Communities¶

    2.5.1 Five Crafts, Five Sons¶

    Several large artisan communities claim descent from Viśvakarman. In south India the five crafts of iron, wood, brass and bronze, stone and gold are practised by the communities known in Tamil as Kammāḷar, in Kerala as Viśvakarma or by the names of the individual crafts, in Karnataka as Pāñcāla (“of the five”) and in Andhra as Viśvabrāhmaṇa. Their traditions hold that Viśvakarman had five sons, each the ancestor of one craft (Table 2.1).

    Table 2.1. The five crafts of the south Indian Viśvakarma communities, as described in community tradition
    Ancestor Craft Tamil and Malayalam names
    Manu iron (smiths) Kollaṉ
    Maya wood (carpenters) Taccaṉ, Āśāri
    Tvaṣṭṛ brass, copper, bronze Kaṉṉāṉ; Mūśāri (Kerala)
    Śilpī stone (masons, sculptors) Kal-taccaṉ, Kallāśāri
    Viśvajña (Daivajña) gold, silver (goldsmiths) Taṭṭāṉ

    In north India there is no single equivalent, but smiths (Lohār), carpenters (Baṛhaī, Khātī), goldsmiths (Sunār) and others have increasingly adopted the name Viśvakarma (often spelled Vishwakarma) as a common caste or community name in the nineteenth and twentieth centuries, and Viśvakarma associations are active in community and political life.

    2.5.2 Claims to Status¶

    The south Indian artisans’ claim to be “Viśvabrāhmaṇa”, Brahmins of Viśvakarman, is old. They had their own priests, wore the sacred thread, and in some places refused to accept the ritual precedence of Brahmins. Medieval Tamil society was divided into “right-hand” (valaṅkai) and “left-hand” (iṭaṅkai) caste groupings; the Kammāḷar belonged to the left hand, and disputes between the two divisions over processional and ritual privileges were frequent from the Cola period into the nineteenth century (Bayly 1999). Under British rule such disputes came before the courts, and censuses that ranked castes provoked petitions from Viśvakarma associations for recognition as Brahmins. Jan Brouwer’s study of the Viśvakarma artisans of Karnataka shows how their own texts, myths and rituals articulate an artisan view of the world, in which making is the primary sacred act and the craftsman, not the priest, stands closest to the creator (Brouwer 1995).

    These claims are best understood on their own terms: as an artisan tradition asserting the dignity and sanctity of skilled work, in a society whose dominant texts ranked artisans low (Chapter 1). They were also, however, claims within the caste system rather than against it; Viśvakarma communities themselves observed rules of purity and distance towards groups they considered lower.

    2.5.3 Builders and Their Names¶

    Indian temples are usually remembered by the names of their royal patrons, but inscriptions sometimes name the builders. The great Rājarājeśvara (Bṛhadīśvara) temple at Thanjavur, completed c. 1010 CE, records the name of its chief architect, Kuñcaramallaṉ Rājarāja Peruntaccaṉ (in Sanskritised form Kuñjaramalla). At Pattadakal an eighth-century inscription names the architect of the Virūpākṣa temple. The Hoysaḷa temples of Karnataka (12th–13th centuries) are notable for the many sculptors’ signatures carved on the images, among them Mallitamma and Dāsoja. Such evidence makes it possible to glimpse individual craftsmen behind monuments usually credited to kings.

    Temples were also permanent employers of craftsmen. The inscriptions of Rājarāja I at Thanjavur list the hundreds of people attached to the temple, among them carpenters, goldsmiths, braziers and potters as well as priests, musicians, dancers and accountants, with the shares of land or grain allotted to each. Temple endowments thus maintained families of artisans over generations, and the great temple towns of south India, such as Madurai, Kumbakonam and Kāñcīpuram, became centres of bronze-casting, weaving, jewellery and other crafts serving both the temple and its pilgrims.

    2.5.4 Viśvakarma in Modern Public Life¶

    In the twentieth century, Viśvakarma associations campaigned for education, for the reform of community customs and for representation in politics, and many artisan communities have been classified as “Other Backward Classes” for the purposes of reservation in education and government employment. The festival of Viśvakarma has become an occasion for public celebration of labour and skill, and in 2023 the name was given to a national scheme for traditional artisans (Chapter 15). The name has thus moved from Vedic cosmology through artisan genealogy to labour politics and welfare policy.

    Modern Relevance: The Sthapatis Today

    The tradition of the hereditary sthapati continues, above all in Tamil Nadu. Bronze-casting families at Swamimalai near Kumbakonam make images by the lost-wax method for temples in India and abroad. The government college of architecture and sculpture at Mamallapuram, founded in the 1950s, trains young sculptors in the traditional canons; its long-serving principal was V. Gaṇapati Sthapati (1927–2011), whose works include the statue of Tiruvaḷḷuvar at Kanyakumari. Hindu temples built in North America, Europe and elsewhere often employ sthapatis and craftsmen from south India.
