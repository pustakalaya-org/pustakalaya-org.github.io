    Preface¶

    This is Volume 17 of the series Bhāratīya Jñāna Paramparā: Śilpa–Kāru: Crafts and Textiles. It is about the knowledge of those who made things in the Indian subcontinent: smiths and bronze-casters, spinners, weavers, dyers and printers, tailors, potters, carpenters and carvers, jewellers, glass- and lac-workers, painters, embroiderers, instrument-makers and basket-makers. It follows their work from the craft quarters of the Indus cities to the workshops, cooperatives and design studios of the present, and it asks how that knowledge was organised, transmitted, valued and, at times, devalued.

    Primary texts are quoted only where the wording and the reference could be confirmed, and figures are given only where a source can be named; where the evidence is uncertain, the text says so. Popular myths, such as the story that the East India Company cut off the thumbs of Bengal’s muslin weavers, are discussed and corrected rather than repeated. Dates are given as the range accepted in current scholarship, with traditional dates labelled as such.

    Two themes run through the book. The first is the sheer range and sophistication of Indian craft knowledge, much of it never written down, which is documented here from archaeology, inscriptions, Sanskrit, Pali, Tamil and Persian texts, the records of European trading companies and modern ethnography. The second is the social setting of that knowledge. Most crafts were practised by hereditary occupational communities (jāti), within a hierarchy that ranked many artisans low and treated some, above all leather-workers, as untouchable. The book tries to give both themes their due: to describe the artisans’ achievements and their own traditions, such as those of the Viśvakarma communities, without romanticising the system of caste in which they worked. Leather, the clearest case of the second theme, therefore has a chapter of its own.

    Chapters on materials and processes are followed by a regional survey and by three chapters on guilds and caste, the colonial economy and the present; schematic line diagrams show the main processes, and photographs show representative objects, monuments and tools.

    References to the standard studies (among others those of Stella Kramrisch, Ananda Coomaraswamy, John Irwin, Rosemary Crill, Ruth Barnes, Giorgio Riello, Prasannan Parthasarathi, Tirthankar Roy, Sharada Srinivasan and Paul Craddock) are given in the text and collected in the select bibliography. A glossary of the principal terms is provided at the end. Metallurgy and chemistry are treated more fully in Volume 14, architecture and sculpture in Volume 16, and trade in Volume 19 of this series.

    No survey of this size can be complete, and specialists will find much to add and correct. Readers should treat it as an introduction, and turn to the works cited for the evidence itself.

    Nikhil Kumar Rajput
    Ramanujan College, University of Delhi
    Guru Pūrṇimā, 2026

    A Note on Transliteration and Names¶

    Sanskrit, Pali and Prakrit words are given in the International Alphabet of Sanskrit Transliteration (IAST): śilpa, kāru, Arthaśāstra. Tamil words follow the conventions of the Tamil Lexicon (Madras), which marks the letters peculiar to Tamil: Kammāḷar, Paṭṭiṉappālai. Words from Hindi, Urdu, Persian, Bengali, Gujarati, Marathi, Assamese and other modern languages are given with long vowels and retroflex consonants marked, in the form most used for the craft concerned (bāndhaṇī, kānthā, paṭolā, mugā); Persian and Arabic terms of the court workshops are given in a simplified scholarly form (kārkhāna, nastaʿlīq). Where a craft term has a regional variant, the variant is noted at its first appearance, as with Gujarati ajrakh and Sindhi ajrak; the glossary marks the language of each term.

    Modern place-names are given in their current official or customary English spelling without diacritics (Varanasi, Kolkata, Kumbakonam), except for a few places, such as Kāñcīpuram, whose classical names recur in the history of a craft; older forms (Banaras, Calcutta, Madras, Cawnpore) are used where they are the names of the period under discussion. The names of registered Geographical Indications, firms, institutions and laws are given exactly as registered, even where they differ from the spelling used elsewhere (“Kancheepuram silk”, “Muga silk of Assam”).
