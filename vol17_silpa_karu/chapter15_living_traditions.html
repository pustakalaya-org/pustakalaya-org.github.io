    Chapter 15: Living Crafts: Revival, Protection and the Present¶

    India still has one of the largest populations of craftspeople and handloom weavers in the world. Their crafts survive in very different conditions: some prosper in national and international markets, some are sustained by government and voluntary support, and many are declining as younger people leave for other work. This chapter surveys the institutions and movements of the craft revival since independence, the design schools, the legal protection of craft names through Geographical Indications, the markets and enterprises that sell crafts today, and the problems that remain.

    15.1 The Craft Revival after Independence¶

    15.1.1 Kamaladevi Chattopadhyay and the Handicrafts Board¶

    The central figure of the post-independence craft revival was Kamaladevi Chattopadhyay (1903–1988), a nationalist, socialist and feminist who had worked in the freedom movement and for refugee resettlement after Partition. As the first chair of the All India Handicrafts Board (1952), she directed surveys of crafts across the country, established emporia and marketing organisations, and promoted the recognition of master craftspeople; she was also a founder of the Crafts Council of India (1964) and a leading figure in the World Crafts Council. Her books, such as Handicrafts of India, describe crafts region by region (Chattopadhyay 1975), and her memoir Inner Recesses, Outer Spaces (1986) records her work (Chattopadhyay 1986). Pupul Jayakar, who organised exhibitions of Indian textiles and crafts in India and abroad, from the Museum of Modern Art exhibition of 1955–56 (Jayakar and Irwin 1956) to the Festivals of India of the 1980s, was another influential figure.

    Kamaladevi’s work for crafts began with the refugees of Partition. The Indian Co-operative Union, which she founded in 1948, helped to resettle refugees from the North-West Frontier and western Punjab in a co-operative township at Faridabad, near Delhi. To sell the work of refugee and other craftspeople it opened a cottage industries emporium in New Delhi, which the government later took over as the Central Cottage Industries Emporium. At the Handicrafts Board she argued that the artisan should be treated as a creator and not as a source of cheap labour, and that craftspeople should be organised in co-operatives they controlled themselves. She travelled widely to find crafts on the edge of extinction, and many of the makers honoured by the early national awards were people she had sought out. Her approach also had limits that later writers have noted. By presenting crafts above all as national heritage, the programmes she inspired could cast artisans as custodians of tradition rather than as workers with interests of their own (Chapter 13). She received the Ramon Magsaysay Award in 1966.

    15.1.2 Institutions¶

    The state created a network of institutions for crafts and handlooms:

    • the All India Handicrafts Board (1952), later the office of the Development Commissioner (Handicrafts), and the All India Handloom Board;

    • the Khadi and Village Industries Commission (1957), for khādī and village industries;

    • state handicrafts and handloom development corporations and cooperative societies, with their emporia;

    • the Crafts Museum in New Delhi, founded in the 1950s and now the National Crafts Museum and Hastkala Academy;

    • the Weavers’ Service Centres, set up from the 1950s to provide designs and technical support to handloom weavers;

    • the National Institute of Design, Ahmedabad (1961), and later craft-design schools (Section 15.8);

    • national awards for master craftspersons (from the 1960s) and, from 2002, the Shilp Guru awards.

    These institutions sustained many crafts through difficult decades. They have also been criticised for bureaucracy, for favouring cooperatives dominated by traders, and for promoting products designed for urban tastes rather than for the needs of artisans themselves.

    15.1.3 Voluntary Organisations and Enterprises¶

    From the 1970s, voluntary organisations, designers and private enterprises became increasingly important. The Self-Employed Women’s Association (SEWA), founded by Ela Bhatt in Ahmedabad in 1972, organised home-based women workers, including embroiderers and other craftswomen, and helped them to market their work. Dastkar, founded in Delhi in 1981, has worked with craft groups across India and organised craft bazaars that bring artisans directly to urban buyers. Fabindia, founded in 1960 as an exporter of handloom textiles, became a large retailer of hand-made and hand-printed goods, and from the 2000s experimented with companies in which artisan suppliers hold shares. Anokhi, founded in Jaipur in 1970, worked with block-printers in Sanganer and Bagru and established a Museum of Hand Printing at Amber. In Kutch, organisations such as Khamir, founded after the 2001 earthquake, and Kala Raksha (Section 15.8) have worked with embroiderers, weavers and printers.

    15.2 Artisans and New Technology¶

    Artisans have never been simply “traditional”; they have adopted new tools, materials and designs whenever these served them. In the early twentieth century handloom weavers took up the fly-shuttle, which allowed one weaver to weave wider cloth faster, and the Jacquard attachment; colonial and princely governments and missionaries promoted improved looms. Weavers adopted mill-spun yarn, artificial silk and synthetic dyes. After independence, many weavers moved from handlooms to power looms, especially in towns such as Bhiwandi and Malegaon in Maharashtra and Surat in Gujarat, which became huge centres of power-loom weaving. Potters have adopted electric wheels and kilns, metalworkers electric blowers and lathes, embroiderers sewing machines, and printers screen-printing.

    These changes raise difficult questions. When does a craft cease to be “hand-made”? Does technology that increases productivity help artisans, or does it hand the market to those with capital? Policy has often tried to protect a sharp boundary between handloom and power loom, while many weavers have moved across it in search of a living, and some of the most admired “traditional” textiles of the twentieth century were woven with tools, such as the fly-shuttle and the Jacquard, that had once been new.

    15.3 Handloom Policy¶

    Handloom weaving remains the largest single craft occupation in India. Since independence the state has tried to protect it from the competition of mills and power looms through cooperatives, subsidies on yarn, rebates on sales, and the reservation of certain products for the handloom sector under the Handlooms (Reservation of Articles for Production) Act of 1985. Enforcement of the reservation has been weak, and power looms producing imitations of handloom saris and fabrics have taken much of the market. Labelling schemes, such as the Handloom Mark (2006) and the India Handloom brand (2015), certify that products are genuinely hand-woven. Since 2015, 7 August, the anniversary of the launch of the Svadeśī movement in 1905, has been observed as National Handloom Day.

    State governments have also promoted crafts through programmes such as Uttar Pradesh’s “One District One Product” scheme (2018), which identifies a characteristic product of each district, many of them crafts, for support with credit, training and marketing; and in 2023 the central government launched the PM Vishwakarma scheme of credit, training and toolkits for artisans in eighteen traditional trades (Chapter 2). Such schemes are recent, and their effects remain to be assessed.

    Policy for handicrafts and handlooms has often swung between two views: that crafts are a heritage to be preserved, and that they are an economic sector to be modernised. Artisans themselves have generally wanted both: respect for their skills and traditions, and access to credit, raw materials, markets, education, social security and new technology on their own terms.

    15.4 Craft and Education¶

    Gandhi’s scheme of “basic education” (Nayī Tālīm), adopted at a conference at Wardha in 1937, proposed that children should learn through productive work, especially spinning and weaving, and that schools should become partly self-supporting through the sale of their products. The scheme was adopted by several provinces and, after independence, became the model for “basic schools”, but it was gradually abandoned in favour of conventional academic schooling. Its central idea, that manual skill is a form of knowledge to be valued in education, has been revived in recent discussions of school curricula and vocational training.

    Artisan families themselves now face a choice between transmitting the craft to their children and giving them a formal education that may lead them away from it. Many craft organisations run schools, scholarships and training programmes, and some, such as the design schools for working artisans described below, try to combine the two.

    15.5 Museums¶

    Museums have played an important part in the documentation and revival of Indian crafts. The Victoria and Albert Museum in London holds one of the largest collections of Indian textiles and decorative arts, formed largely in the colonial period; its 2015 exhibition The Fabric of India surveyed the whole history of Indian textiles (Crill 2015). In India, the Calico Museum of Textiles in Ahmedabad, founded in 1949 by Gautam and Gira Sarabhai, holds outstanding collections of historic textiles and has published a series of scholarly catalogues (Irwin and Hall 1971, 1973); the National Crafts Museum in Delhi combines collections with demonstrations by living craftspeople; and many state and private museums, such as the Anokhi Museum of Hand Printing at Amber, document particular crafts. Museums face the question of how to present craft objects: as anonymous “ethnographic” specimens, as works of art, or as the products of named individuals and communities with their own histories.

    15.6 Geographical Indications¶

    The Geographical Indications of Goods (Registration and Protection) Act was passed in 1999 and came into force in 2003. A Geographical Indication (GI) is a name identifying goods as originating in a particular place, where a given quality, reputation or other characteristic of the goods is essentially attributable to that origin. Registration prevents others from using the name for goods that do not come from the registered area or meet its specification. The first Indian GI was Darjeeling tea (2004); since then several hundred GIs have been registered, a large proportion of them handicrafts and handloom products.

    Among the craft and handloom GIs are Pochampally ikat, Kancheepuram silk, Banaras brocades and saris, Chanderi fabric, Muga silk of Assam, Kashmir pashmina and the Kani shawl, Phulkari, Lucknow chikan, Madhubani paintings, Thanjavur paintings, Orissa paṭṭacitra, Bidrī ware, Channapatna toys, Kondapalli toys, Āṟanmuḷa mirrors, Kannauj perfume, the block prints of Sanganer, Bagru and Bagh, and Kolhapuri chappals.

    Scholarly Note: What GIs Can and Cannot Do

    A GI protects a name, not a technique or a design, and not the livelihood of the makers. It can prevent machine-made saris from Surat from being sold as “Kancheepuram silk”, and it can help to build a reputation for quality. But enforcement depends on producers’ organisations with the resources to take action; a registered GI may be held by an association dominated by traders rather than artisans; and a GI cannot by itself address the low earnings, poor working conditions and social stigma that lead young people to leave crafts. Its value depends on what producers and their organisations do with it.

    15.7 Markets Today¶

    Indian crafts reach their buyers through many channels: local markets and fairs, government emporia and craft bazaars (such as the Dilli Haat in Delhi, opened in 1994, and the Shilpgram centres), private shops and brands, exporters, and increasingly online platforms and social media, which allow some artisans to sell directly to customers. Craft tourism has grown in places such as Kutch, Raghurajpur, Pochampally and Swamimalai; Pochampally was named one of the “Best Tourism Villages” of the UN World Tourism Organization in 2021. Jaipur (2015) and Srinagar (2021) have been designated UNESCO Creative Cities of Crafts and Folk Art.

    The structure of the market often works against the artisan. Between the maker and the final buyer stand contractors, traders, exporters and retailers, each taking a share; artisans who lack working capital must accept advances from traders who then fix the price. Hand production cannot compete with machines on price, and where buyers do not value the difference between hand-made and machine-made goods, artisans are squeezed. Power looms imitating handloom designs have taken much of the market for handloom saris, despite legal reservations for handloom.

    15.7.1 Who Buys Crafts?¶

    The buyers of Indian crafts have changed several times. Village households once bought pots, cloth and tools from local artisans; courts and temples commissioned luxury goods; foreign merchants bought for export. Today, much of the market for handmade goods is among the urban middle classes in India and among buyers abroad, including the Indian diaspora, who buy crafts as expressions of taste, identity and support for artisans. Handloom saris, khādī and regional textiles are worn by many Indians as a statement of national or regional identity, and politicians often wear them in public. This market values “authenticity” and “tradition”, but it also demands novelty, consistency and convenience; artisans must negotiate between these expectations.

    15.8 Design Schools and the Crafts¶

    Since independence, the professional designer has become one of the main intermediaries between India’s craftspeople and their markets. The institutions that trained designers grew out of a long argument about the relation between art, craft and industry, an argument that began in the colonial art schools (Chapter 14) and at Santiniketan (Chapter 11).

    15.8.1 The Bauhaus in Calcutta¶

    In December 1922 the Indian Society of Oriental Art in Calcutta exhibited works by artists of the Bauhaus, the German school of art, architecture and design founded at Weimar in 1919. Among them were Paul Klee, Wassily Kandinsky, Lyonel Feininger and Johannes Itten, and their works were hung alongside paintings of the Bengal school. The exhibition was arranged through Stella Kramrisch, who had come to India at Rabindranath Tagore’s invitation. Partha Mitter treats it as the moment at which Indian artists first met the European avant-garde in their own city (Mitter 2007). The Bauhaus programme called for artists and craftsmen to work together in the workshop, with no barrier of rank between them. This had obvious affinities with Tagore’s experiments at Santiniketan and Sriniketan. At the time, however, its influence on Indian crafts was slight. Its lasting influence came forty years later, through design education.

    15.8.2 The Eameses’ India Report¶

    In 1958, at the invitation of the Government of India and with the support of the Ford Foundation, the American designers Charles and Ray Eames spent several months travelling in India. Pupul Jayakar had a large part in bringing them. Their India Report, submitted in April 1958, argued that India faced rapid change, in which traditional ways of making and modern industrial production would collide. It called for a new kind of designer who understood both, and it recommended an institute for training, research and service in design (Eames and Eames 1958). The report’s famous praise of the humble brass loṭā as a model of good design was not nostalgia. The Eameses used the vessel to show how a form refined by generations of makers and users can answer many demands at once, of material, use, cost and beauty. They asked designers to approach every new product with the same seriousness.

    15.8.3 The National Institute of Design¶

    The National Institute of Design was set up in Ahmedabad in 1961 as an autonomous institution. Gira Sarabhai and Gautam Sarabhai, of the family that had founded the Calico Museum (Section 15.5), were its guiding figures, and the Ford Foundation gave support. Its early teaching drew on European modernist design education, including the foundation course of the Bauhaus and the methods of the Ulm School of Design, and on visiting teachers from Europe, America and Japan. It also sent its students to study crafts. The documentation of a craft became a regular exercise in Indian design education: recording, in a village or workshop, the materials, tools, processes, products, makers and markets of a living craft. Such studies, accumulated over decades, form an important archive of crafts as they were practised in the second half of the twentieth century.

    In January 1979 the Institute hosted an international meeting, held by the United Nations Industrial Development Organization and the International Council of Societies of Industrial Design, which adopted the Ahmedabad Declaration on Industrial Design for Development. The Declaration called on designers in developing countries to serve basic needs and to build on local resources and skills rather than imitate the products of the industrial West. A National Institute of Design Act of 2014 declared the Institute an institution of national importance, and further campuses have since been opened in other states.

    15.8.4 Designers in the Handloom Sector¶

    The Weavers’ Service Centres of the All India Handloom Board (Section 15.1) employed artists and designers to create new designs for handloom weavers and to record old ones. The painter K. G. Subramanyan, later a leading teacher at Baroda and Santiniketan, worked as a designer for the Board’s Bombay centre around 1960. In the 1980s the Weavers’ Service Centres, under the direction of Martand Singh, prepared a series of exhibitions of handwoven textiles called Vishwakarma. Weavers re-created historical designs and techniques from museum pieces and old samples for these shows, which were seen in India and at the Festivals of India abroad. These projects showed that skills thought to be lost could often be recovered when weavers were given the time, materials and payment to do so.

    15.8.5 Crafts on Show: The Festivals of India¶

    In the 1980s the Festivals of India, a series of cultural festivals organised by the Indian government under the direction of Pupul Jayakar, presented Indian crafts abroad on an unprecedented scale. They began in Britain in 1982 and continued in the United States and France in the mid-1980s and later in the Soviet Union, Japan and other countries. Besides exhibitions of sculpture and painting, they brought living craftspeople and performers to work in public. The exhibition Aditi, designed by Rajeev Sethi and first shown in London in 1982, followed the cycle of life, from birth and childhood to marriage, through objects, with potters, painters, puppeteers and other makers working in the galleries. Other exhibitions paired international designers with Indian craftspeople to make new objects. The festivals opened export markets and made some master craftspeople known by name. They also revived an old problem. Saloni Mathur has described how, at the Colonial and Indian Exhibition in London in 1886, Indian artisans were brought to work before visitors as living exhibits of a timeless India (Mathur 2007). Critics of the 1980s festivals asked whether showing artisans at work, however respectfully, still presented them as specimens of “tradition” rather than as contemporary makers.

    15.8.6 Surveys and Documentation¶

    Designers were not the only documenters. The Census of India of 1961 included a series of surveys of handicrafts, published as monographs on particular crafts and villages, which recorded processes, tools, communities and incomes across the country. The art magazine Marg, founded in Bombay in 1946 by the writer Mulk Raj Anand, published special numbers on regional crafts and textiles that brought them to a wide educated readership. Together with the catalogues of the Calico Museum, the reports of the handicrafts and handloom boards and the work of design students, these surveys make the crafts of the mid-twentieth century unusually well recorded.

    15.8.7 New Schools¶

    From the 1980s the number of design schools grew. The National Institute of Fashion Technology was founded in New Delhi in 1986, and the Indian Institute of Crafts and Design in Jaipur followed in the 1990s. A different model put the artisan in the classroom. The design school opened by Kala Raksha in Kutch in 2005, led by Judy Frater, taught working artisans from embroidering, weaving, printing and other communities to design new work within their own traditions and to understand the markets they sold to. Frater later founded Somaiya Kala Vidya on the same principles. Its graduates are recognised as designers in their own right, not as executors of other people’s designs.

    15.8.8 Design for Whom?¶

    Collaboration between designers and artisans has been one of the main strategies of the revival. At its best it brings new products and markets to artisans while respecting their skills and their own design traditions; at its worst it treats them as anonymous labour executing the designs of others. The design schools have also changed how craft knowledge is judged. Ethnographic studies, such as Soumhya Venkatesan’s work on the Pattamadai mat-weavers (Chapter 13), show how the views of designers, officials and buyers about what counts as good craft can override the makers’ own (Venkatesan 2009). The language of “design intervention”, common in development programmes, assumes that a craft needs correcting from outside. Many designers and institutions now prefer to speak of collaboration, to name the artisans they work with, and to share authorship and income. The ideal set out in the India Report and the Ahmedabad Declaration, of design that starts from the knowledge already present in a community, is easier to state than to carry out.

    Some artisans have become well-known artists in their own right. Mithilā painters such as Sita Devi, Ganga Devi and Dulari Devi, the Warli painter Jivya Soma Mashe, the Gond painter Jangarh Singh Shyam and the rogan painter Abdul Gafur Khatri (Chapter 12) have been recognised with national awards and international exhibitions. Their success has raised its own disputes: whether a painting made for the market by a named artist is still “folk” or “tribal” art, who may use a community’s styles and motifs, and how much of the price paid in a city gallery returns to the village. Jangarh Singh Shyam’s early death in Japan in 2001, where he had gone to paint for a museum, became for many critics a symbol of the inequalities of that market.

    15.9 Fair Trade and Certification¶

    Since the 1970s, “alternative trade” organisations in Europe and North America have bought Indian crafts directly from producer groups on terms intended to give artisans a fair price, advance payment and long-term relationships. Indian organisations have joined international fair-trade networks, and certification schemes, such as those against child labour in carpet-weaving (Chapter 4) and the Handloom Mark, try to assure buyers of the conditions under which goods are made. Such schemes have helped some producer groups, but they reach only a small part of the craft sector, and certification adds costs that small producers find hard to bear.

    15.10 Shocks and Resilience¶

    Craft economies are vulnerable to sudden shocks. The Gujarat earthquake of 2001 destroyed homes and workshops across Kutch; craft organisations and communities rebuilt, and the earthquake led to new initiatives such as the village of Ajrakhpur and the organisation Khamir. The lockdowns imposed during the COVID-19 pandemic in 2020 closed markets and fairs, cut off orders from exporters and left many artisans without income; online sales and relief from craft organisations helped some, but many artisans turned to other work. Craft communities have survived many such crises, but livelihoods that depend on long chains of intermediaries and on distant markets are the first to fail when those chains break.

    15.11 Problems That Remain¶

    15.11.1 Livelihoods and Status¶

    The most serious problem is that many craftspeople earn too little to live decently from their work. Surveys of handloom weavers and handicraft artisans conducted by the Government of India have repeatedly found low household incomes and high levels of debt. Young people from artisan families often prefer other occupations, both because they pay better and because craft work still carries the low status that caste attached to manual work. Any revival that does not improve artisans’ incomes and social standing will not last.

    15.11.2 A Note on Numbers¶

    Figures for the number of artisans in India, the value of craft production and exports, or the number of Geographical Indications are often quoted, but they should be used with care. The most systematic counts are the All India Handloom Censuses conducted for the Ministry of Textiles, the most recent of them in 2019–20, and the surveys of handicraft artisans of the office of the Development Commissioner (Handicrafts). The handloom censuses count households and persons engaged in weaving and in “allied” work such as winding, warping and sizing, and distinguish full-time from part-time workers, so that totals change greatly according to which categories are added together; successive censuses have recorded a declining number of handloom workers, a large majority of them women. Handicraft surveys use different definitions again, and many artisans work part-time or seasonally, combine craft with agriculture or wage labour, or are counted as “helpers” rather than workers. This volume has therefore avoided precise statistics where reliable, sourced figures could not be given. Readers who need numbers should consult the census reports themselves and note how each figure was obtained.

    15.11.3 Raw Materials¶

    Crafts depend on materials whose supply is often uncertain: the indigenous “desi” cottons suited to hand-spinning, now a small fraction of India’s cotton area, which since 2002 has been dominated by genetically modified Bt hybrids; the host trees of tasar and mugā silk; natural dye plants; sandalwood and other carving woods (Chapter 7); clay from village commons; and bamboo and cane from forests governed by laws that have not always recognised the rights of forest communities.

    15.11.4 Knowledge and Its Ownership¶

    Traditional designs and techniques are easily copied by industrial producers in India and abroad, and existing intellectual property law protects them only partly. GIs protect names; design registration protects individual new designs; copyright protects individual works. Collective, inherited knowledge falls between these categories. Documentation by museums, design schools and craft organisations helps preserve knowledge of techniques and designs, but documentation cannot replace living transmission from one practitioner to another.

    15.11.5 Health and Safety¶

    Many crafts carry occupational hazards: silicosis among stone-carvers and agate-workers, lung disease among glass and metal workers, injuries and eye strain among weavers and embroiderers, exposure to toxic dyes, mordants and metals among dyers, printers and jewellers. Child labour, once widespread in carpet weaving, glass-bangle making and other crafts, has been reduced but not eliminated. A craft revival that ignores these issues romanticises hardship.

    15.11.6 Women Artisans¶

    Women make up a large part of the craft workforce, especially in embroidery, weaving in the North-East, basketry, pottery preparation, bead-work and the processing of fibres; yet they are often recorded as “helpers” rather than artisans, paid less, and excluded from the control of markets and from organisations dominated by men. Many of the most successful craft initiatives of recent decades, such as SEWA, the embroidery collectives of Kutch and Bihar and many self-help groups, have been organised by and for women. Their experience shows that income from craft can increase women’s independence and standing in their families, but also that the double burden of household and craft work can be heavy (Wilkinson-Weber 1999; Chakravarti 2003).

    15.11.7 Caste and Dignity¶

    Many of the communities that practise crafts, especially those working with leather, bamboo, fibre and clay, are Dalit, Ādivāsī or “backward” castes, and the stigma attached to manual work and to their identities has not disappeared. Revival efforts that present crafts as timeless “heritage” can ignore this history, or even reinforce it by expecting artisans to remain in their hereditary occupations. Respect for craft must include the freedom of artisans’ children to choose other work, and the dignity of those who choose to stay.

    15.12 Conclusion¶

    The crafts described in this volume embody one of the great bodies of practical knowledge in human history: the knowledge of how to transform cotton into muslin, iron into crucible steel, ore into zinc, clay into a temple’s terracotta skin, and threads into paṭolā whose patterns appear only when warp and weft meet. That knowledge was created and carried mostly by people whom the dominant texts ranked low and whose names were rarely recorded. To value Indian craft knowledge truly is to value its makers: to record their achievements accurately, without myth or exaggeration; to recognise the injustices of caste and colonial exploitation that shaped their lives; and to ensure that those who practise crafts today can do so with dignity and a fair reward.

    For crafts region by region, Ranjan and Ranjan (2007); for the work of the post-independence revival, Chattopadhyay (1975); for the making of the idea of Indian craft, McGowan (2009); for the Fabric of India exhibition of 2015, Crill (2015); for the origins of the National Institute of Design, Eames and Eames (1958); for the Bauhaus exhibition in Calcutta, Mitter (2007).
