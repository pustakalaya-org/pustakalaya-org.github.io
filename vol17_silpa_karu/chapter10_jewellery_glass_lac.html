    Chapter 10: Ābharaṇa: Jewellery, Gems, Glass, Lac and Perfume¶

    Jewellery in India has never been only adornment. It is a store of wealth, especially for women, for whom it has often been the most secure form of property; a marker of status, caste, region and marriage; an offering to deities; and a vehicle of belief about protection and good fortune. India was also, for two thousand years, a major source of precious stones, above all diamonds, and its lapidaries and gem-merchants developed a specialised literature on the testing of gems. This chapter is about the crafts of adornment. It surveys the history of ornament, the goldsmith’s techniques, the regional traditions, and the gem trade and its literature, and then the humbler materials from which most Indians’ ornaments were made: beads of stone, shell and glass, and bangles of glass and lac. It ends with perfume, which the texts treat, like jewellery, as part of the adornment of the person. Glass, lac and perfume also demanded a practical knowledge of materials transformed by heat, solution and distillation; the chemical tradition of rasaśāstra that theorised such transformations is treated in Volume 14.

    10.1 Ornament from the Indus to the Mughals¶

    The Indus cities produced gold and silver ornaments, bangles of shell, faience and stoneware, and beads of carnelian, agate, lapis lazuli and steatite; the long, perfectly drilled carnelian beads of Harappa and Chanhudaro are among the most skilful stone work of the ancient world (Kenoyer 1998). Early historic sculpture, from Bharhut and Sanchi to Mathurā and Amarāvatī, shows men and women wearing heavy necklaces, ear ornaments, armlets, girdles and anklets, many of which can be matched with surviving pieces, such as the gold jewellery with granulation and inset stones excavated at Taxila (Marshall 1951).

    Sanskrit literature has a rich vocabulary of ornaments, which lexicons such as the Amarakośa list in detail: the necklace (hāra, of many strings and types), the armlet (keyūra, aṅgada), the bracelet (valaya, kaṅkaṇa), the ear-ring (kuṇḍala, karṇikā), the crown or diadem (kirīṭa, mukuṭa), the girdle with tinkling bells (mekhalā, kāñcī, raśanā), and the anklet (nūpura, mañjīra). Poets describe the sound of anklets and girdle-bells as part of a woman’s beauty, and sculptors carved these ornaments in great detail on images of gods, goddesses and yakṣīs. Iconographic texts prescribe the ornaments of each deity, and temple images are dressed and adorned with real jewellery for worship and festivals.

    Under the Mughals and the Rajput courts, jewellery became an art of extraordinary richness, combining Indian forms and gem-setting with Persian and Central Asian taste: jewelled turban ornaments (sarpech), pendants, rings and daggers set with diamonds, rubies, emeralds and spinels, often with enamel on the reverse. Jewels were exchanged as gifts and tribute and were a central part of royal treasuries.

    10.2 The Goldsmith¶

    The goldsmith (suvarṇakāra, Hindi Sunār, Tamil Taṭṭāṉ) appears in the Arthaśāstra as a figure the state must watch closely: the state goldsmith works in a guarded workshop, receives gold by weight, and is fined heavily for substitution or adulteration (2.13–14) (Kangle 1960–1965). The goldsmith was ranked low in the Dharmaśāstras, in part, perhaps, because of this association with deceit. In south India goldsmiths are one of the five Viśvakarma crafts (Chapter 2).

    10.2.1 Techniques¶

    Filigree
    Fine twisted wire bent into patterns and soldered into openwork. Cuttack in Odisha (tārakasī) and Karimnagar in Telangana are known for silver filigree.
    Granulation
    Tiny spheres of gold fused to a surface; known from Taxila and from early historic south India.
    Repoussé and chasing
    Sheet metal shaped by hammering from behind and refined from the front, the main technique of temple jewellery and of much tribal silver.
    Kundan
    The setting of stones in a bed of lac within a gold frame, using strips of highly refined gold foil (kundan) pressed and burnished around each stone. The technique allows irregular, uncut or flat stones to be set closely together.
    Mīnākārī
    Enamelling (from Persian mīnā): coloured glass powders fused into recesses engraved in gold or silver. Jaipur is famous for enamel on gold, often on the backs of kundan pieces; Varanasi for pink enamel (gulābī mīnākārī); Lucknow, Delhi and Nathdwara for other styles. The Jaipur tradition is said to have been introduced by craftsmen brought from Lahore in the late sixteenth century.
    Thewā
    A speciality of Pratapgarh in Rajasthan, in which a sheet of gold, cut and engraved with fine figurative scenes, is fused onto coloured glass.

    10.2.2 Gold and Silver Thread¶

    Gold and silver thread (zarī, kalābattū) for weaving and embroidery is itself the product of a specialised metal craft. Silver is drawn through a series of progressively smaller holes in a draw-plate to make very fine wire, which is flattened between rollers and wound round a core of silk or cotton thread; for gold thread the silver wire is gilded. Surat in Gujarat has long been the main centre of the industry, with Varanasi, Agra and other weaving towns. Flattened wire (bādlā) and sequins are also used in embroidery. Since the twentieth century, much “zarī” has been made from copper or from metallised plastic film rather than silver.

    10.2.3 Temple Jewellery¶

    In south India heavy gold jewellery is made for temple images, and in the twentieth century this “temple jewellery” style, in gilt silver or imitation materials, was adopted for the costumes of Bharatanāṭyam and other classical dancers. Nagercoil in Kanyakumari district is a well-known centre of temple-style jewellery. The jewels of major temples, given by kings and devotees over centuries, form some of the largest collections of historic jewellery in India.

    10.2.4 Assaying and Hallmarking¶

    Goldsmiths and their customers have always needed ways to judge the purity of gold. The touchstone, on which the streak left by a gold object is compared with the streaks of test-needles of known fineness, is described in the Arthaśāstra and was used by Indian goldsmiths and money-changers until modern times (Chapter 3). Gold was refined by cementation and cupellation, and its fineness expressed in units of varṇa or, later, in carats. Since 2021 hallmarking by assaying centres recognised by the Bureau of Indian Standards has been compulsory for most gold jewellery sold in India, replacing the customer’s trust in the individual goldsmith with a system of public certification.

    10.2.5 Goldsmiths in Modern India¶

    The modern state has shaped the goldsmith’s trade as directly as the Arthaśāstra’s superintendent once did. To curb gold smuggling and reduce private demand for gold, the Gold Control Rules of 1963 at first prohibited the making of new ornaments in gold finer than 14 carats. Customers were used to jewellery of about 22 carats and would not buy the lower standard, and the rule caused severe distress among goldsmiths, many of whom lost their work. It was soon relaxed. The Gold (Control) Act of 1968 then required goldsmiths and dealers to be licensed and to keep accounts of the gold they held. The Act was repealed in 1990, and the import and trade of gold were liberalised in the following years.

    The organisation of the craft has also changed. In the cities the old relation between a family goldsmith and his clients has largely given way to retail jewellers, from small shops to national chains, who sell finished jewellery and employ or subcontract the craftsmen who make it. Much of the hand work in the jewellery workshops of Mumbai, Delhi, Chennai and other cities is done by migrant artisans (kārīgar) from West Bengal. They work in small rented rooms for piece rates, often far from their families. Machines now make chains, and many standard pieces are cast in multiples from wax models produced in rubber moulds or designed on computer and printed. Mandatory hallmarking has further moved the guarantee of quality from the goldsmith to the institution. The goldsmith’s skill remains essential for setting, enamelling, filigree and repair, but the craftsman is often hidden behind the shop.

    10.3 Regional and Tribal Jewellery¶

    Every region and community has its own ornaments, and many ornaments mark marital status. In much of north India married women wear toe-rings (bichhiyā), glass or lac bangles and vermilion in the parting of the hair; in Maharashtra the black-beaded maṅgalsūtra; in Tamil Nadu and Kerala the tāli tied at marriage; in Bengal the conch-shell and red lac bangles (śaṅkhā and palā). The “sixteen adornments” (solah śṛṅgār) of a bride are a conventional north Indian list.

    Pastoral and Ādivāsī communities wear heavy silver ornaments, which serve as savings and as markers of identity: the necklaces, anklets and armlets of Rabārī and other pastoral women in Gujarat and Rajasthan, the silver of the Lambāṇī (Banjārā), and the bead, brass and shell ornaments of the Naga peoples, among whom ornaments once signified rank and achievements in war and feasting. Dhokra brass ornaments are cast by the lost-wax method in central and eastern India.

    Under classical Hindu law, a woman’s own property (strīdhana) included the gifts she received at marriage, especially ornaments; in a society in which women rarely inherited land, jewellery was often the main property a woman could call her own.

    10.3.1 Ornament and the Life-Cycle¶

    Ornaments follow a person through life, and their making is tied to the household rites that mark its stages. The piercing of a child’s ears (karṇavedha) is counted among the life-cycle rites (saṃskāra) in several lists, and small children are given anklets with bells, black threads and amulets against the evil eye. At marriage a bride receives ornaments from both families, and in many communities the set she wears identifies her region, caste and religion as clearly as her dress. Widowhood was marked by their removal. In many Hindu communities a widow’s bangles were broken and her nose- and toe-rings taken off, a practice that expressed in metal and glass the loss of status that the law-books and custom imposed on widows. Some ornaments have their own history: the nose-ring, for example, is rarely if ever shown in early Indian sculpture and became common only in the medieval period, after which it was adopted so widely that it came to be thought of as an ancient Indian ornament.

    Village and small-town jewellery is also money. Silver anklets, necklaces and armlets are valued largely by weight; they are pawned with moneylenders in bad years, and melted and remade by the goldsmith when fashions change or a daughter must be provided for. The customer pays for the metal, and separately for the workmanship and for the metal lost in working, and disputes over weight and purity were a constant feature of the relation between goldsmith and client, the background to the suspicion of goldsmiths in the Arthaśāstra and the law-books. Because old pieces were so often melted down, few village ornaments survive from before the nineteenth century, and their history must be reconstructed from sculpture, painting and the forms still made. Oppi Untracht’s survey, based on years of fieldwork, records the forms, names and techniques of the jewellery of hundreds of communities, much of it made by village smiths with simple tools (Untracht 1997).

    10.4 Gems and Their Literature¶

    10.4.1 The Sources¶

    Ancient and medieval India was a major source of precious stones. Diamonds came from the alluvial deposits of the Krishna and Godavari valleys, from the Golconda region, and from Panna in central India; pearls from the Gulf of Mannar between India and Sri Lanka; beryl and other gems from south India. Rubies, sapphires and spinels were imported from Sri Lanka, Burma and Badakhshan, and emeralds, in the Mughal period, from Colombia via Europe. The Periplus lists pearls, transparent stones, diamonds and sapphires among the exports of south Indian ports (Casson 1989).

    10.4.2 Texts on Gems¶

    The testing of gems (ratnaparīkṣā) was a recognised field of knowledge; “the testing of silver” and “knowledge of the colouring of gems and of their mines” are among the sixty-four arts of the Kāmasūtra (1.3.15; Chapter 1). The Arthaśāstra’s chapter on the examination of precious articles for the treasury (2.11) describes pearls, gems, diamonds and coral, their sources, and the qualities of good and faulty stones: a good gem, it says, should be well formed, clear, smooth, heavy and lustrous, with radiance within (Kangle 1960–1965). The Bṛhatsaṃhitā (6th century) has chapters on the testing of gems, including diamonds, pearls, rubies and emeralds (Bhat 1981–1982). Separate lapidary treatises, such as the Ratnaparīkṣā attributed to Buddhabhaṭṭa and the Agastimata, were edited and translated into French by Louis Finot (Finot 1896); the Garuḍa Purāṇa includes a series of chapters on gems drawn from this literature. The Ratnaparīkṣā of Ṭhakkura Pherū (1315), a Jain official at the court of the Delhi sultan ʿAlāʾ al-Dīn Khaljī, describes the gems he saw in the royal treasury, with their weights and prices, from the point of view of a working assayer.

    These texts combine practical observation (colour, transparency, hardness, weight, flaws) with beliefs about the good and bad fortune brought by particular stones. They should be read as records of medieval connoisseurship and belief, not as gemmology in the modern sense.

    10.4.3 The Nine Gems¶

    In later astrological tradition, each of the nine “planets” (navagraha) was associated with a gem: the Sun with ruby, the Moon with pearl, Mars with coral, Mercury with emerald, Jupiter with yellow sapphire or topaz, Venus with diamond, Saturn with blue sapphire, and the lunar nodes Rāhu and Ketu with hessonite (gomeda) and cat’s-eye (vaiḍūrya). A ring or pendant with all nine stones (navaratna) became a popular auspicious ornament.

    A Note on Gem “Therapy”

    The belief that wearing particular gems can change a person’s fortune or health is a matter of faith and astrological tradition. There is no scientific evidence that gems have such effects, and they should never be used in place of medical treatment. Preparations of gems and metals taken internally as medicines can be harmful (see Chapter 3).

    10.5 Diamonds¶

    Until diamonds were found in Brazil in the 1720s, India was the principal source of diamonds known to Europe and western Asia. The French jeweller Jean-Baptiste Tavernier, who visited the diamond mines of the Golconda kingdom, including Kollur on the Krishna, in the mid-seventeenth century, described thousands of labourers digging and washing the gravels, the merchants who bought the stones, and the Indian method of polishing diamonds on wheels with diamond powder and oil. He noted that Indian cutters preferred to preserve weight rather than maximise brilliance (Tavernier 1889).

    Many famous diamonds came from India. The Koh-i-Noor, whose early history is largely legend, passed through Mughal, Persian, Afghan and Sikh hands before it was taken by the East India Company after the annexation of the Punjab in 1849 and presented to Queen Victoria; it was recut in London in 1852 and is now in the British Crown Jewels (Dalrymple and Anand 2017). The large blue diamond that Tavernier sold to Louis XIV is the ancestor of the Hope diamond, now in the Smithsonian Institution. The Regent diamond, found in the Golconda region about 1698, is in the Louvre, and the Daryā-i Nūr, taken from Delhi by Nadir Shah in 1739, is among the Iranian crown jewels.

    Diamond cutting today is concentrated in Surat in Gujarat, where an industry that grew from the 1960s and 1970s cuts and polishes a large share of the world’s smaller diamonds. It employs hundreds of thousands of workers, many of them migrants from Saurashtra, in conditions that range from modern factories to small workshops.

    10.6 Beads, Shell and Pearls¶

    10.6.1 Stone Beads of Khambhat¶

    The cutting and drilling of hard stones such as carnelian, agate and jasper into beads is one of the oldest continuous crafts of western India. The Indus bead-makers worked carnelian from the Rajpipla hills of Gujarat; in the medieval and early modern periods, Khambhat (Cambay) on the Gulf of Khambhat became the great centre of agate and carnelian working, exporting beads across the Indian Ocean to East Africa, Arabia and Southeast Asia. The stones are heated to deepen their colour, chipped into shape, ground, drilled and polished. Studies of present-day bead-makers at Khambhat have helped archaeologists understand ancient techniques (Kenoyer 1998). The work is dangerous: workers who breathe the fine dust produced by grinding are at high risk of silicosis.

    10.6.2 Conch Shell¶

    The conch shell (śaṅkha), sacred to Viṣṇu and blown in temples, was cut into bangles and ornaments from Indus times onwards. In Bengal, the white shell bangles (śāṅkhā) worn by married Hindu women, together with red lac or coral bangles, are made by the Śāṅkhārī community, especially in Bishnupur and Dhaka, from shells of Turbinella pyrum fished in the Gulf of Mannar.

    10.6.3 Pearls¶

    The pearl fisheries of the Gulf of Mannar, between south India and Sri Lanka, were famous in antiquity; the Periplus and Tamil literature mention them, and the Pāṇḍya kings controlled them. Divers of the Paravar community dived for oysters in the seasonal fisheries, under the control first of local rulers and then of the Portuguese, the Dutch and the British. Pearls were also brought from the Persian Gulf. The Paravars converted to Catholicism in the sixteenth century, partly to gain Portuguese protection; in their history craft, caste, religion and empire on the Indian coast cannot be separated.

    10.7 Glass¶

    10.7.1 Beginnings¶

    The Indus craftsmen made faience and a glassy stoneware, but true glass appears in South Asia only in the first millennium BCE. Glass beads and bangles are found at sites of the Painted Grey Ware and Northern Black Polished Ware cultures in the Ganga valley, and become common in the early historic period. Claims for glass in Harappan contexts are uncertain and most refer to faience. The Sanskrit word for glass is kāca; “glass gems” (kācamaṇi) were a byword for the imitation of the precious.

    10.7.2 Beads and Bangles¶

    From the late centuries BCE, South Indian sites such as Arikamedu near Pondicherry produced small drawn glass beads in great quantities. These “Indo-Pacific” beads, made by drawing a tube of glass and cutting it into short lengths, were traded around the Indian Ocean from East Africa to Southeast Asia for some two thousand years (Francis 2002). Alok Kanungo’s ethnoarchaeological study of present-day glass-bead makers in India illustrates techniques that help archaeologists interpret ancient workshops (Kanungo 2004). Excavations at Taxila produced glass beads and bangles, and imported Roman glass vessels (Marshall 1951).

    Glass bangles are worn by married women across much of north and central India. Their manufacture is concentrated today at Firozabad in Uttar Pradesh. Glass is melted in furnaces, gathered and drawn onto a rotating cylinder to form a continuous coil; the coil is cut into rings, whose ends are then joined in a flame, often by home-based workers, before the bangles are decorated. The industry has a long record of hazardous working conditions and child labour, which reforms and enforcement have reduced but not eliminated.

    Firozabad’s industry grew in the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries (Chapter 14). It replaced imported glass bangles, and it was helped in the Svadeśī years by the call to buy Indian goods. Its furnaces burned coal for most of the twentieth century. Firozabad lies within the Taj Trapezium Zone, the area around the Taj Mahal in which the Supreme Court of India, from the mid-1990s, ordered polluting industries to switch to cleaner fuels in order to protect the monument. The glass furnaces were therefore required to move to natural gas, which changed their technology and costs and drove some small units out of business. Bangles are not only made but also sold by specialists. In north India the Manihār or Cūṛīhār communities, mostly Muslim, have traditionally sold glass and lac bangles and fitted them on customers’ wrists, a service of some intimacy that brought men of these communities into the women’s quarters of households of every caste and religion.

    10.7.3 Glass Vessels¶

    Glass vessels were less common in India than in western Asia, and much fine glass was imported, from the Roman world in antiquity and from Iran, Venice and later Bohemia and Britain in the early modern period. Indian glassmakers did, however, make bottles, flasks, bowls, lamps and huqqa bases, some decorated with enamel and gilding, in the Mughal and later periods; Gujarat and the courts of north India were among the centres. Glass was also used for inlay in palace walls, for bangles and beads, and, from the nineteenth century, for the reverse paintings on glass made in Thanjavur, Gujarat and elsewhere.

    10.7.4 Mirrors¶

    At Āṟanmuḷa in Kerala, a few families of craftsmen make metal mirrors (āṟanmuḷa kaṇṇāṭi) from a high-tin bronze, cast and then polished to a reflective surface. The mirror is one of the eight auspicious objects of Kerala ritual and is a registered Geographical Indication. Mughal and Rajput palaces used small pieces of convex mirror glass in plaster for the decoration of “mirror halls” (śīśmahal), and small mirrors are sewn into the embroidery of Gujarat and Rajasthan.

    10.8 Lac¶

    10.8.1 The Material¶

    Lac (lākṣā) is a resin secreted by the lac insect, Kerria lacca, which lives on the branches of trees such as palāśa (Butea monosperma), kusum (Schleichera oleosa) and ber (Ziziphus). The encrusted twigs (“stick-lac”) are harvested, crushed and washed to give “seed-lac”, which is melted and stretched into thin sheets that break into flakes: shellac. The red dye washed out of the raw lac was used for dyeing silk and wool and as a cosmetic. Lac softens with heat and hardens on cooling, and it is used as an adhesive, a varnish, a sealing wax and a moulding material. The word “lac” comes through Persian from Sanskrit lākṣā; “shellac” refers to the thin flakes.

    India has long been one of the world’s main producers of lac. Much of it is cultivated, by inoculating host trees with brood-lac, by Ādivāsī communities in Jharkhand, Chhattisgarh, Odisha and neighbouring states. The Indian Lac Research Institute was founded at Namkum near Ranchi in 1924, and its successor institution continues research on lac and other natural resins. Shellac had a large industrial market in the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, as a varnish, as an electrical insulator and as the material of gramophone records, until it was replaced by synthetic plastics; it is still used as a food glaze and a coating for tablets.

    The Mahābhārata tells how Duryodhana tried to burn the Pāṇḍavas in a house of lac (lākṣāgṛha) built to catch fire easily; they escaped through a tunnel.

    10.8.2 Lac Crafts¶

    Lac is used for bangles, especially in Rajasthan, Hyderabad and Bihar, made by the Lakherā and Maṇihār communities: coloured lac is softened over a fire, rolled round a core, shaped to the size of the wearer’s wrist and decorated with glass stones, mirror pieces and foil. Lac is also used to colour turned wooden toys and objects (Chapter 7), to fill the backs of hollow gold ornaments and as the bed in which kundan stones are set (Section 10.2), and as sealing wax.

    10.9 Perfume¶

    Scent completed the adornment of the body. Perfumery (gandhayukti, “the combination of scents”) was one of the arts of the cultivated person and the subject of chapters in the Bṛhatsaṃhitā and other texts (Bhat 1981–1982). Indian perfumes were made from sandalwood, aloeswood (agaru), musk, camphor, saffron, vetiver, flowers and resins, and used in worship, in the bath and on the body. James McHugh’s study of smell in Indian religion and culture surveys this literature (McHugh 2012). F. R. Allchin argued that terracotta apparatus from early historic sites in Gandhāra and the Ganga valley may have been used for distillation, perhaps of alcoholic spirits (Allchin 1979); the interpretation is debated.

    The perfumers (gandhī, aṭṭār) of Kannauj in Uttar Pradesh continue to make ittar by a distillation method known as deg-bhapkā. Flowers or other materials are heated with water in a copper still (deg); the vapour passes through a bamboo pipe into a copper receiver (bhapkā) standing in cold water and containing sandalwood oil, which absorbs the fragrance. Kannauj attars include rose (gulāb), jasmine, screwpine (keoṛā), vetiver (khas), henna blossom (hinā) and miṭṭī, made by distilling baked earth to capture the smell of the first rain. Kannauj perfume is a registered Geographical Indication, but the industry has declined under competition from synthetic fragrances and the scarcity of sandalwood oil.

    Further Reading¶

    For the lapidary texts, Finot (1896); for Tavernier’s account of the mines and diamond trade, Tavernier (1889); for the Koh-i-Noor, Dalrymple and Anand (2017); for regional jewellery, Untracht (1997) and Ranjan and Ranjan (2007); for the 1956 exhibition of Indian textiles and ornaments, Jayakar and Irwin (1956); for beads, Francis (2002) and Kanungo (2004); for perfume and smell, McHugh (2012); for natural products of commercial importance in the early twentieth century, including lac, Watt (1908).
