    Chapter 13: Śreṇī and Jāti: Guilds, Caste and Craft Knowledge¶

    How was craft knowledge organised, protected and handed down in India? Two institutions stand out in the sources: the guild (śreṇī), an association of people following the same trade, which is prominent in texts and inscriptions of the early historic period; and the hereditary occupational community (jāti), within which, for most of the last two millennia, most crafts were learned and practised. This chapter takes these in turn. It then looks at the ways in which production was organised (in the household, under village patrons, in court and temple workshops and for merchants), at the kind of knowledge that craftspeople held and the means they devised to record and teach it, at the relation between that knowledge and science, and finally at the work of women and children.

    13.1 Guilds in Ancient India¶

    13.1.1 The Evidence of the Texts¶

    The word śreṇī (Pali seṇi) means a row or series, and hence a group of people of the same occupation. Pali texts speak of eighteen guilds as a conventional number, and the Jātaka stories mention heads of craft groups, such as the chief of the carpenters or of the smiths (jeṭṭhaka), and villages of potters, smiths and carpenters. The great merchants (seṭṭhi, Sanskrit śreṣṭhin) of the Buddhist texts, such as Anāthapiṇḍika of Sāvatthī, who bought the Jeta grove for the Buddha by covering its ground with coins (a scene carved at Bharhut), were wealthy householders and financiers rather than leaders of craft guilds (Chakravarti 2002).

    The Dharmaśāstras recognise the customs of occupational groups as law for their members. Gautama allows farmers, traders, herdsmen, moneylenders and artisans to settle matters according to the rules of their own groups (Gautama 11.21); Manu (8.41) tells the king to establish the law after examining the laws of castes, regions, guilds (śreṇīdharma) and families (Olivelle 2005); and the Yājñavalkya and Nārada law-books deal with breaches of the agreements made by guilds and other corporate bodies. The Arthaśāstra lists “guild troops” (śreṇībala) among the kinds of armed forces available to a king (9.2), regulates the conduct of artisans and their dealings with customers (4.1), and treats guilds as bodies to be watched and taxed (Kangle 1960–1965; Olivelle 2013).

    13.1.2 The Evidence of Inscriptions¶

    Inscriptions give a more concrete picture (Chakravarti 2002).

    • Sanchi (late 1st century BCE or early 1st century CE). The ivory-carvers of Vidiśā record a carving on the southern gateway of the great stūpa as their work (Chapter 7).

    • Nasik (1st or 2nd century CE). An inscription of Uṣavadāta, son-in-law of the Western Kṣatrapa ruler Nahapāna (whose date is disputed), records that he invested sums of money with two weavers’ guilds (kolika-nikāya) at Govardhana, at fixed monthly rates of interest, so that the interest would provide robes and other necessities for the monks of the cave monastery. Another Nasik inscription, probably of the third century, records similar deposits with guilds of potters, of oil-pressers and of makers of water-machines. Guilds thus acted as trustees and bankers for perpetual endowments.

    • Indor (465 CE). A copper-plate of the reign of Skandagupta records an endowment deposited with the guild of oil-men (tailika-śreṇī) of Indrapura, who undertook to supply oil for a lamp in a temple of the Sun in perpetuity (Fleet 1888).

    • Mandasor (436 and 473 CE). The best-known guild inscription, composed by the poet Vatsabhaṭṭi, tells how a guild of silk-weavers (paṭṭavāya) migrated from Lāṭa (southern Gujarat) to Daśapura (Mandasor), built a temple of the Sun in 436 CE and repaired it in 473 CE. It describes how some members of the guild took up other occupations, becoming archers, story-tellers, astrologers and men of religion, while remaining members of the group (Fleet 1888).

    In south India, inscriptions of the Cola period and later record powerful merchant associations, such as the “Five Hundred” (Aiññūṟṟuvar), and associations of weavers and artisans that made donations to temples and negotiated privileges with kings and temples (Chakravarti 2002).

    13.1.3 Artisans, Kings and Temples in the South¶

    Medieval south Indian inscriptions show artisans as taxpayers, donors and bargainers. Cola and later inscriptions mention taxes on looms and on other craft activities. Others record grants of honours to particular artisan groups, which could include the right to blow conches at weddings and funerals, to wear sandals, to plaster the walls of their houses or to build houses of more than one storey. Such privileges were symbols of status, and their grant or denial was a matter of intense concern. Vijaya Ramaswamy’s study of weavers in medieval south India traces how weaving communities such as the Kaikkōḷar and the Sāliyar became temple donors and trustees and, by the Vijayanagara period, acted as corporate bodies, migrating when conditions were unfavourable and negotiating terms with temples and rulers (Ramaswamy 1985; Mines 1984). The silk-weavers of Madurai and other Tamil towns known as Saurāṣṭras (Paṭṭunūlkārar) speak an Indo-Aryan language related to Gujarati and trace their origin to migrations from Gujarat, probably in the later medieval period. They too moved across India in search of patrons and markets, as the Mandasor silk-weavers had done a thousand years earlier.

    13.1.4 What Guilds Did¶

    From these sources we can infer that guilds regulated the work and conduct of their members, settled disputes among them, accepted deposits and endowments, made donations as corporate bodies, and in some cases maintained armed men. It is not clear how far they controlled apprenticeship or standards of workmanship, and it would be misleading to equate them simply with the craft guilds of medieval Europe or with modern trade unions. D. D. Kosambi saw the guilds as an important feature of an economy based on commodity production and trade in the early historic period, and linked their decline in the post-Gupta centuries to the growth of a more localised, village-based economy (Kosambi 1956); the timing and extent of that decline are debated.

    13.2 Craft and Caste¶

    13.2.1 Hereditary Occupation¶

    By the late first millennium CE most crafts were practised by hereditary jātis, and the relation between jāti and occupation became a central feature of Indian society. A child born into a potter’s or weaver’s family learned the craft at home, married within the community, and was expected to follow the family occupation. This system ensured the transmission of skills, and it gave each community a recognised place and, often, a monopoly of its trade in a locality. It also confined people to occupations by birth, and ranked those occupations in a hierarchy of purity and status.

    The relationship between jāti and occupation was never complete or unchanging. Many members of artisan castes have always worked as cultivators or labourers; occupations changed with new technologies and markets; and some communities, such as the Mandasor silk-weavers or the Kaikkōḷar weavers of Tamil Nadu, combined weaving with trade, soldiering or other work (Mines 1984; Bayly 1999).

    13.2.2 Purity, Pollution and Untouchability¶

    Crafts were ranked in part by notions of purity and pollution. The Brahmanical law-books declared some artisans’ food unacceptable to Brahmins (Chapter 1), and treated contact with dead animals, blood, hair and excrement as polluting. Leather-working, skinning dead animals, scavenging and sweeping were assigned to the lowest groups, who were treated as untouchable. In many regions some weaving, basket-making and drumming castes were also untouchable.

    Untouchability meant more than low status. Untouchable artisans were excluded from temples, wells and schools, forced to live apart, and subjected to humiliating rules and violence. The leather-working castes, whose history is the clearest example, are the subject of Chapter 9, which also discusses Ramnarayan Rawat’s critique of the “leather-worker” stereotype and the protests that followed the Una flogging of 2016.

    Scholarly Note: Ambedkar on Caste and Occupation

    B. R. Ambedkar argued in Annihilation of Caste (1936) that defenders of caste confused it with the division of labour. The caste system, he wrote, is not merely a division of labour but a division of labourers into watertight compartments, graded one above the other; it assigns occupations by birth rather than by capacity, prevents people from changing occupations when technology and markets change, and so produces unemployment and degrades work. He drew particular attention to the way caste made certain kinds of work, such as scavenging, degrading by association (Ambedkar 1936). Uma Chakravarti has shown how control of women’s sexuality and marriage was central to the reproduction of caste, and therefore of hereditary occupation (Chakravarti 2003).

    13.2.3 Caste in the Colonial Archive¶

    From the mid-nineteenth century, British administrators set out to describe and classify the castes of India. District gazetteers, ethnographic surveys and the decennial censuses recorded each caste with a “traditional occupation”; the census of 1901, under H. H. Risley, attempted to rank castes in order of social precedence. These exercises turned fluid, locally variable relationships into fixed categories and encouraged castes to organise themselves to claim higher places, often by asserting a link with a prestigious occupation or origin (Bayly 1999). Artisan castes petitioned to be recorded as Brahmins (Viśvakarma), Kṣatriyas or Vaiśyas, took new names (Prajāpati for potters, Anṣārī for Muslim weavers), and founded caste associations and journals. The colonial archive is therefore both a valuable source on crafts around 1900 and an active force that shaped the identities it recorded.

    13.2.4 Artisan Voices and Movements¶

    Artisans and other working people also produced their own critiques of caste and their own visions of dignity. The bhakti poet-saints from artisan and untouchable castes, Kabīr the Julāhā weaver, Raidās (Ravidās) the Chamār leather-worker, Nāmdev the tailor and calico-printer, Gorā the potter, Senā the barber and Cokhāmeḷā the Mahār, used the imagery of their crafts and insisted that devotion, not birth, made a person worthy (Hawley and Juergensmeyer 1988). Raidās’s vision of “Begumpura”, the city without sorrow, imagines a society without caste or tax.

    Several religious movements drew their following largely from artisan and labouring castes. The followers of Kabīr formed the Kabīr Panth, with monastic centres in Banaras and Chhattisgarh; the devotees of Raidās built temples and communities in Punjab and Uttar Pradesh, and in the twentieth century the Ad Dharm and later Ravidassia movements asserted a separate religious identity for Punjabi Dalits. In Chhattisgarh, the Satnāmī movement founded by Guru Ghāsīdās in the early nineteenth century rejected caste and idol worship and attracted many Chamārs. In modern times anti-caste thinkers from Jotirao Phule to Ambedkar have drawn on these traditions (Omvedt 2008). The Viśvakarma communities, with their own monasteries (maṭhas), priests and claim to sacred descent (Chapter 2), represent another form of artisan self-assertion, made from within the caste order rather than against it.

    Weaving communities show how far an occupation’s meaning could be contested. In Gujarat the Vaṇkars (formerly called Dheḍs) wove coarse cotton cloth for villages and were treated as untouchable; in Maharashtra many Mahārs did the same. As mill cloth undermined coarse weaving in the nineteenth and twentieth centuries, many left the craft for labour in towns, the army and the mills. The Mahār community became the core of Ambedkar’s movement, and in 1956 many Mahārs converted with him to Buddhism, rejecting the caste order that had defined their occupations. The Muslim weavers of north India, by contrast, kept the loom but rejected the name: the Momin Anṣārī movement replaced the derogatory “Julāhā” with a claim of honourable descent (Chapter 4). Leaving a craft and staying in it on new terms were both ways of refusing the place that caste assigned.

    13.3 Households, Patrons and Markets¶

    13.3.1 The Household Workshop¶

    Most crafts were practised in the household. The family was the unit of production, and children learned by watching and helping: winding bobbins, preparing clay, grinding pigments, carrying materials. As they grew older they took on more demanding tasks under the eye of a parent or an older relative, and eventually worked independently. In some crafts, such as sculpture, bronze-casting and the finer kinds of weaving and painting, a skilled master might take pupils from outside the family; the relationship between master (ustād, guru) and pupil (shāgird, śiṣya) could be close and long. Families and communities also guarded their techniques. Recipes for dyes, alloys and glazes were treated as family property and disclosed reluctantly to outsiders. This secrecy protected livelihoods, but it also meant that knowledge died with families who abandoned the craft.

    13.3.2 The Jajmānī System¶

    In many villages of north India, craftsmen and service castes (carpenters, smiths, potters, barbers, washermen and others) served a set of landowning households in return for annual payments in grain at harvest and gifts on ceremonial occasions. In Maharashtra the comparable village servants were the balutedārs, among them the carpenter, the smith, the potter and the leather-worker (Chapter 9). The American missionary William Wiser, who studied a village near Mainpuri in the 1920s, described these relationships as the “Hindu jajmani system” (from yajamāna, the patron of a sacrifice), a system of mutual obligations that bound castes together in a hereditary and reciprocal division of labour (Wiser 1936).

    Later anthropologists and historians have criticised this picture. They have pointed out that the relationships were highly unequal, that patrons could withhold payment and service castes had little recourse, that much village craft was in fact paid for in cash or kind by the piece, and that the “system” was neither as ancient, as uniform nor as harmonious as Wiser suggested. C. J. Fuller argued that the very idea of a jajmānī “system” was largely a construction of observers (Fuller 1989). What is clear is that in many villages artisans’ livelihoods depended on customary relationships with dominant landowners, and that these relationships declined in the twentieth century with the spread of markets, factory goods and new opportunities outside the village.

    13.3.3 Courts, Temples and Workshops¶

    Alongside household production and guild organisation, kings, temples and great households maintained their own workshops. The Arthaśāstra’s state workshops for weaving, metals and gold (Chapter 1) represent an ideal of royal control. Medieval south Indian temples employed carpenters, smiths, goldsmiths and potters, paid with shares of land and produce (Chapter 2). The Mughal emperors maintained a large establishment of imperial workshops (kārkhānas), described in Abu’l Fazl’s Āʾīn-i Akbarī, which produced textiles, carpets, arms, jewellery, books and paintings for the court and for gifts (Abu’l Fazl 1873). Bernier, who described their halls in the Delhi fort (Chapter 4), also thought that the craftsmen’s skill was wasted because they were poorly paid and had little security (Bernier 1891). Regional courts, from the Rajput states to Thanjavur and Mysore, had their own workshops, and the patronage of such courts shaped styles of painting, jewellery and textiles. Court workshops brought together craftsmen of different origins, including immigrants from Iran and Central Asia, and fostered exchanges of technique and design. When courts declined, as in the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries, their craftsmen lost their patrons and either dispersed to other courts or turned to the market.

    13.3.4 The Mahājans of Gujarat¶

    In the towns of Gujarat, associations called mahājan survived into the nineteenth and twentieth centuries. Each trade or craft, such as cloth merchants, potters, carpenters or weavers, might have its own mahājan, which met under elected or hereditary heads, regulated the conduct of members, fixed holidays on which work was forbidden, settled disputes, imposed fines and supported charities such as animal shelters (pāñjrāpoḷ). Associations of the great merchants, often Jain and Vaiṣṇava Baniyas, could close the whole market of a city to put pressure on the authorities. Observers around 1900 compared these bodies with the guilds of ancient India and medieval Europe. Whatever the justice of the comparison, they are evidence that corporate organisation among merchants and artisans continued in some regions long after the period of the ancient śreṇīs.

    13.3.5 Merchants and Artisans¶

    From at least the early modern period, much craft production for markets was organised by merchants, who advanced money or raw materials to artisans and bought their products. This “putting-out” or advance system gave merchants great power over artisans, but it also allowed artisans to reach distant markets. Weavers in eighteenth-century south India and Bengal negotiated with merchants and with the European companies, and could, in favourable circumstances, refuse advances or move to another territory (Parthasarathi 2001). In north Indian towns, merchant and artisan communities formed associations and neighbourhood organisations that regulated trade and supported religious and charitable institutions (Bayly 1983). For the late colonial and early independent decades, Douglas Haynes has shown how the weavers and merchants of the small towns of western India built a dense world of workshops, credit and petty capital, the beginnings of what is now called the informal economy (Haynes 2012). The colonial transformation of these relationships is the subject of the next chapter.

    13.4 Knowledge Without Books¶

    Why did so few Indian craftsmen write about their crafts? Part of the answer lies in literacy: until the twentieth century most artisans could not read Sanskrit or Persian, the languages of learned writing, and many could not read at all. Part lies in the nature of craft knowledge, much of which is embodied and tacit, learned by doing rather than by reading. Part lies in the social order. Learned treatises were written by and for literate elites, who classified and regulated crafts but rarely practised them. Sheldon Pollock has argued that Sanskrit intellectual culture held that every practice (prayoga) should be governed by a prior body of theory (śāstra), so that theory was held to come first even in fields, such as erotics, where practice obviously did (Pollock 1985). In such a culture the knowledge of texts was readily ranked above the knowledge of hands. Where artisans had access to writing, as among the Odishan stone-carvers or the Kashmiri shawl-weavers with their coded tālīm, they used it for their own purposes.

    The chemist and philosopher Michael Polanyi argued that much human knowledge, from riding a bicycle to recognising a face or judging the quality of a material, is tacit, held in the body and in trained perception rather than in explicit rules (Polanyi 1958). Craft knowledge is largely of this kind. A dyer judges the readiness of an indigo vat by its colour, smell and froth; a smith judges the temperature of iron by its colour in the forge; a potter knows by touch whether clay is ready to throw. Kramrisch emphasised the craftsman’s dependence on a tradition of forms received and re-made in each generation (Kramrisch 1958). Such knowledge is acquired through long practice under the guidance of someone who already has it, and it is easily lost when the chain of transmission is broken.

    Tacit does not mean unsystematic. Craftspeople experiment, compare results, adjust recipes and pass on rules of thumb; they classify materials by their working properties and have names for qualities and defects that outsiders do not notice. The double-ikat paṭolā, whose pattern depends on the precise calculation of thousands of resist-tied sections of warp and weft, or the distillation of zinc at Zawar, which required the control of a process that cannot be observed directly, show that craft knowledge could be precise, cumulative and inventive. Most craft traditions, however, lacked two things: a body of theory linking their procedures to explanations of nature, and institutions such as printing, scientific societies and laboratories through which such explanations could be tested and communicated beyond the workshop. The chief exception was rasaśāstra, which joined laboratory practice in metals and minerals to an elaborate theory, though for medical and alchemical ends rather than for the crafts (Section 13.6 and Volume 14). The absence of craft texts is therefore better understood as a different way of holding knowledge than as a sign that Indian crafts were “unscientific”: knowledge was kept in workshop routines, family recipes, oral formulas, pattern-books and drawings, and the trained perceptions of experienced workers, and it was vulnerable because it depended on the continuity of families and markets.

    13.5 Learning a Craft: Apprenticeship, Pattern and Notation¶

    Craft knowledge was not carried only in hands and memories. Artisans also devised their own means of storing and passing on designs: coded scripts, pattern harnesses, wooden blocks, pounced tracings, counted formulas and memorised verses. These devices, and the forms of apprenticeship in which they were used, organised craft knowledge beyond the single household.

    13.5.1 Master and Apprentice¶

    In the urban workshop crafts of north India, many of them practised by Muslim artisans, the relation between master (ustād) and apprentice (shāgird) had a vocabulary and an etiquette shared with music and wrestling (Volumes 9 and 20). An apprentice was often a relative or a neighbour’s son, received little or no pay while learning, and rose over several years from preparatory work to the loom, the embroidery frame or the engraver’s bench. The master’s name served as a credential, and a craftsman might be known as the pupil of a named master long after he had left the workshop. Apprenticeship could shade into dependence: where a master-weaver or merchant had advanced money to a family, the boy who learned in his workshop might be bound to work there until the debt was cleared. The hereditary sthapati lineages of the south and the stone-carvers of Odisha (Chapter 2) could take pupils from outside the family into a line of instruction.

    13.5.2 The Kashmir Tālīm¶

    One of the most elaborate artisans’ notations in India is the tālīm of the Kashmir shawl and carpet. In the production of the woven kānī shawl (Chapter 12), the design was drawn by a pattern-drawer (naqqāsh), a colour specialist (tarah-guru) fixed its colours, and a writer (tālīm-guru) translated the drawing into a coded script. The script specified, for each line of weaving, the colours to be used and the number of warp threads over which each was to be woven. The weavers followed the tālīm rather than the drawing, and the master of the loom read it out to them as they worked. The system separated design from execution: a design could be copied, altered, sent from one workshop to another, and woven by men who had never seen the drawing. It also created a hierarchy within the craft, in which the designer and the writer of the tālīm were well paid and the weavers, many of them bound to their employers by debt and heavily taxed by the state, were poor (Irwin 1973; Ames 1986).

    Kashmiri carpet-weavers use the same method. The carpet tālīm, written on strips of paper in symbols for colours and numbers of knots, is chanted line by line by the head weaver, and the weavers repeat their part as they tie the knots. A carpet of great complexity can thus be woven by people who never see its design as a picture. The tālīm is a notation devised and owned by artisans, outside the learned scripts of Sanskrit and Persian; like the syllables of the drummer (Volume 9), it is precise because the work required precision.

    13.5.3 The Pattern Harness and the Punched Card¶

    On the drawloom (Section 4.4) the design was stored not on paper but in the loom itself. A pattern-maker (naqshaband) worked from a design drawn on squared paper, in which each square stood for a group of warp threads and a pick of weft, and tied the pattern into the harness of cords, a task that could take weeks for a complex border. The finished naqsha could be taken off one loom, stored, lent or sold, and tied onto another: it was an object that embodied a design. When the Jacquard mechanism was adopted in Banaras and other centres in the early twentieth century, the same squared designs were transferred to sets of punched cards cut by card-punchers. Since the 1990s, designs have increasingly been prepared on computers, which print the cards or drive electronic Jacquards directly. Each of these changes has moved control of the design further from the weaver at the loom, towards the pattern-maker, the card-puncher, the computer designer and the merchant who commissions the work.

    13.5.4 Blocks, Tracings and Samples¶

    Other crafts stored designs in their tools. A printer’s blocks are the pattern-book of the workshop: the set of blocks for a design may be kept for generations, and a design can be reproduced as long as its blocks survive (Chapter 5). Painters kept pounced tracings (charbā), through which compositions passed from father to son (Chapter 11). Designs also arrived from outside. From the seventeenth century the European companies sent patterns and samples to the Coromandel painters and printers to copy, and the Indian workshops adapted them to their own techniques (Crill 2008). In the opposite direction, John Forbes Watson’s The Textile Manufactures and the Costumes of the People of India (1866) was accompanied by sets of bound volumes of actual Indian cloth samples, distributed in Britain and India so that manufacturers could study, and imitate, the cloths that Indian buyers wanted (Watson 1866).

    13.5.5 Counting and Memory¶

    In women’s counted-thread embroidery the pattern is held as a count, not as a drawing. The kasūti of the Dharwad region of Karnataka (Chapter 12) is worked in four stitches: the double running stitch gavanti, the zigzag running stitch murgi, the darning stitch negi and the cross-stitch menthi. Its motifs of temple towers, chariots, lamps and birds are built up by counting the threads of the ground cloth, so that the double running stitch looks the same on both sides. The sūf embroiderers of Kutch likewise compose each motif by counting, planning its geometry in their heads as they work from the back of the cloth (Frater 1995). Such patterns are learned by sitting beside an experienced embroiderer and by copying older pieces, and they are remembered as sequences of counts and movements rather than as pictures. The same is true of the kōlam designs drawn on thresholds in Tamil Nadu, which girls learn from their mothers as rules for drawing lines around a grid of dots.

    Image-makers learned iconography from meditation verses (dhyāna), which describe the posture, attributes, ornaments and colour of each form of a deity, and from proportional rules counted in tāla units (Chapter 2). Measuring cords and sticks marked with the units carried the system from the text into the workshop. The verse was memorised, and the cord and stick did the counting, so that a sculptor could reproduce a canonical form without consulting a manuscript.

    13.5.6 From Workshop to Institution¶

    Since independence, the state and voluntary bodies have tried to reproduce apprenticeship in institutional form. They have set up training centres attached to the handicrafts and handloom boards, schemes that pay master craftspeople to teach pupils from outside their families, and schools such as the college of sculpture at Mamallapuram (Chapter 2). Soumhya Venkatesan’s study of the Labbai Muslim mat-weavers of Pattamadai in Tamil Nadu (Chapter 8) shows how such programmes, and the language of national heritage that comes with them, change what counts as craft knowledge and who is recognised as holding it. The weavers had to meet the expectations of officials, designers and buyers as well as those of their own families (Venkatesan 2009). Opening a craft to learners outside the hereditary community also raises a further question, taken up in Chapter 15: whether skills once protected by birth can be taught to anyone without undermining the livelihoods of those who hold them.

    13.6 Craft and Science¶

    Was Indian craft knowledge a form of science? Colonial writers sometimes contrasted the “empirical” skill of the Indian craftsman with the “scientific” knowledge of the European, as if the craftsman merely repeated what his ancestors had done. Some modern writers go to the opposite extreme, claiming that ancient Indian craftsmen possessed secret sciences that anticipated modern physics or nanotechnology. Neither view does justice to the evidence. Indian learned traditions had their own theories of matter, in Sāṃkhya, Vaiśeṣika, Jain and Buddhist philosophy, in Āyurveda and in rasaśāstra (Volumes 3, 13 and 14), but these were developed largely by and for learned specialists, and their connection with the practical knowledge of dyers, smiths and potters was usually loose. The history of Indian metallurgy and chemistry is told in Volume 14; this section looks only at the meeting of crafts with mechanical learning, with European science and with modern archaeometry.

    13.6.1 Machines in the Texts¶

    Sanskrit literature knows the skilled maker of mechanical devices (yantra). The eleventh-century architectural treatise Samarāṅgaṇasūtradhāra, attributed to King Bhoja of Dhārā, has a chapter on yantras describing, in general terms, mechanical figures that move, birds that fly or sing, devices that pour water and fountains for pleasure gardens. Story literature tells of wonderful automata: in the Pañcatantra, a weaver in love with a princess has his friend, a carpenter, make him a wooden Garuḍa that can fly, and he visits her in the guise of Viṣṇu; in Buddhist and Jain stories, craftsmen make mechanical dolls and flying contraptions. Such descriptions show admiration for the ingenuity of craftsmen and a lively interest in mechanical marvels, especially for courtly entertainment. They are not technical specifications, and attempts to read them as evidence of ancient aircraft or advanced machinery misunderstand their genre (Chapter 2). The practical devices of the crafts and the fields are better documented: water-lifting wheels, including the geared “Persian wheel” with a chain of pots, which spread in north India from about the thirteenth century, oil presses, sugar-cane mills, cotton gins, spinning wheels, looms and lathes. Irfan Habib’s survey of the period from about 650 to 1750 brings the evidence for these together (Habib 2008).

    13.6.2 European Observers¶

    From the seventeenth century, Europeans in India observed and recorded Indian techniques that they wished to understand, imitate or exploit, and some of their accounts are among the best evidence we have for Indian crafts before the colonial transformation. The records of chintz-making at Pondicherry made for French readers by Beaulieu and Cœurdoux are described in Chapter 5; European printers learned mordant printing partly from such accounts and partly from Armenian and other intermediaries (Irwin and Brett 1970; Riello 2013).

    In the 1790s, Helenus Scott, a physician in Bombay, sent samples of Indian “wootz” steel to Sir Joseph Banks, President of the Royal Society. The chemist George Pearson analysed them and reported to the Society in 1795 on this remarkable steel and its properties (Pearson 1795). Francis Buchanan, travelling through Mysore and Malabar for the East India Company in 1800–01, described iron smelting and crucible steel-making in detail (Buchanan 1807). In 1819–22 Michael Faraday, working with the cutler James Stodart at the Royal Institution, analysed wootz and experimented with alloys of steel in an attempt to reproduce its qualities; this work was a step towards the scientific study of alloy steels (Srinivasan and Ranganathan 2004).

    In 1971 the Gandhian historian Dharampal published a collection of eighteenth-century European accounts of Indian science and technology, including descriptions of iron smelting, the manufacture of ice at Allahabad by evaporation on cold nights, the drill plough, and inoculation against smallpox in Bengal (Dharampal 1971). The collection did much to correct the colonial picture of Indian technical backwardness, though it should be read alongside the Indian sources and the critical scholarship of later historians. Kapil Raj has argued more generally that modern science was not simply carried from Europe to India, but was in important respects made through encounters in which Indian practitioners, informants and intermediaries took an active part (Raj 2007). The same is true of crafts: Europeans who wrote about Indian dyeing, steel or textiles depended on the craftsmen who showed them their work, and whose names they seldom recorded.

    13.6.3 Modern Study and Its Uses¶

    Since the mid-twentieth century, scientists in India and abroad have studied historical Indian materials with the methods of archaeometry: chemical and isotopic analysis, metallography, electron microscopy and radiocarbon dating. The results described in earlier chapters, from the dating of Indian textiles found in Egypt and Indonesia to the reconstruction of zinc distillation at Zawar and of crucible steel, have often been won with the help of craftspeople, as when bladesmiths, bead-makers or dyers have reproduced historical materials for scientists. Conservation science is another meeting-point: laboratories such as the National Research Laboratory for Conservation of Cultural Property, founded at Lucknow in 1976, study textiles, paintings, metals and manuscripts and often consult the people who still make such objects. The earlier, nationalist history of Indian science and chemistry, begun by P. C. Ray and Brajendranath Seal, is discussed in Volume 14.

    Careful study of this kind is also the best answer to the myths that gather around Indian crafts: claims that the Delhi pillar is “rust-free” or that wootz was deliberate “nanotechnology” (Chapter 3), that Dhaka muslin had thread-counts beyond any modern cloth (Chapter 4), that vāstu encodes magnetic “energies” (Chapter 2), or that the British cut off the weavers’ thumbs (Chapter 14). The real achievements are remarkable enough, and exaggeration only obscures them. Craft knowledge also has present uses that deserve testing rather than assumption. Natural dyeing and handloom weaving are models of low-energy production, and building techniques such as kāṭh-kuṇī are studied for their behaviour in earthquakes; but traditional techniques have their own costs, from the pollution of dye-works and tanneries to the silicosis of stone-carvers. The relationship worth building is one in which craftspeople are treated as holders of knowledge and partners in research, not merely as informants, and share in its benefits.

    13.7 Women’s and Children’s Work¶

    Women’s labour has been essential to almost every Indian craft, but it has often been invisible. In many crafts work was divided by gender: men threw pots on the wheel, women prepared clay and painted; men wove, women spun, wound and sized the yarn; men worked the forge, women worked the bellows. Some crafts, such as embroidery, quilting, floor- and wall-painting, and many forms of basketry and weaving in the North-East, were largely women’s work, done for the household and for gifts and dowries before they entered the market. State employment of women spinners is as old as the Arthaśāstra (Chapter 4).

    These divisions were customary, not natural. In much of India women were kept from particular tools, the potter’s wheel, the loom of the professional weaver, the smith’s anvil, the sculptor’s chisel, and the exclusion was often explained in terms of ritual purity or of the sacredness of the tool, which was worshipped at festivals such as Viśvakarma Pūjā (Chapter 2). Yet the same tasks were women’s work elsewhere: in Assam, Manipur and the hill societies of the North-East the loom belongs to women (Chapter 4), among the Hira of Assam women build pots by hand (Chapter 6), and in Kashmir it is men who embroider (Chapter 12). Within a single craft, too, the stages were divided. In bāndhaṇī, the thousands of tiny knots are tied by women and girls at home, and the dyeing is done by men in the workshop; in many weaving households women wound, sized and warped the yarn that men wove. The stages done by women were usually those done at home and paid by the piece, and they were the ones least often recorded, credited or named.

    Women also carried much of the knowledge of transmission. Girls learned embroidery, floor-painting and household weaving from their mothers and grandmothers, often by working on the textiles of their own dowries, so that learning a craft and preparing for marriage were the same process. Such knowledge passed outside the guild, the workshop and the caste council, through which men’s craft knowledge was organised, and it left few written traces. It is known mostly from the objects themselves and from modern ethnography (Frater 1995). When women’s crafts became commercial, women were frequently paid least, as in the home-based chikan embroidery of Lucknow (Chapter 4). Organisations such as the Self-Employed Women’s Association (SEWA), founded in Ahmedabad in 1972, have organised home-based women workers, including embroiderers, to improve their bargaining position (Chapter 15).

    In household production, children worked from an early age, and their labour was part of the way skills were transmitted. In the commercial craft industries of the nineteenth and twentieth centuries, however, the employment of children often had little to do with learning a family craft. Children were employed in large numbers in carpet-weaving, where their small fingers were valued for knotting; in glass-bangle making at Firozabad; in match and firework factories; in gem-polishing and in brassware workshops, often in hazardous conditions and sometimes in bondage to employers who had advanced money to their parents. Campaigns by Indian and international organisations from the 1980s, laws such as the Child Labour (Prohibition and Regulation) Act of 1986 and its later amendments, labelling schemes and the expansion of schooling have reduced child labour in these industries, but not eliminated it. Any account of Indian crafts that celebrates the skill of child artisans without considering their conditions repeats the romanticism this volume tries to avoid.
