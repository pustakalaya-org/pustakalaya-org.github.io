    A Guide to Further Reading¶

    The short notes at the end of each chapter list the main sources for that chapter. This guide is selective and evaluative: it suggests where a reader should begin, what each work does best, and where its limits lie. Full details are given in the Select Bibliography.

    Where to Begin¶

    For what is made where today, the indispensable work is Handmade in India, edited by M. P. and Aditi Ranjan (Ranjan and Ranjan 2007). It is a regional encyclopaedia, richly photographed and organised state by state, with descriptions of processes and products; it has little history and almost no social analysis, and is best used beside a historical account such as this one. For textiles as objects, Rosemary Crill’s catalogue The Fabric of India (Crill 2015) is the best single illustrated introduction, and Gillow and Barnard (1991) is a reliable practical survey of techniques. Kamaladevi Chattopadhyay’s Handicrafts of India (Chattopadhyay 1975) belongs to the revival it describes and should be read as a document of that movement as well as a survey.

    On the idea of the Indian craftsman, the classic statements are Coomaraswamy’s The Indian Craftsman (Coomaraswamy 1909) and Kramrisch’s essay on the traditions of the Indian craftsman (Kramrisch 1958). Both are eloquent and both largely leave caste aside. George Birdwood’s The Industrial Arts of India (Birdwood 1880) is a Victorian source, valuable for what it records and for its attitudes. Abigail McGowan’s Crafting the Nation in Colonial India (McGowan 2009) is the best modern account of how the category of “Indian crafts” itself was made, and is the natural corrective to all three.

    Early History and Texts¶

    Kenoyer (1998) is the clearest introduction to Indus crafts by the archaeologist who has studied them most closely; Allchin and Allchin (1982) remains a sound general archaeology. For guilds and trade in early India, Chakravarti (2002) is concise and reliable, and Kosambi (1956) is still stimulating, though its model of post-Gupta decline is debated. The translations of the Arthaśāstra by Olivelle (2013) (with up-to-date notes and dating) and Kangle (1960–1965) (with fuller commentary), of Manu by Olivelle (2005), of the Periplus by Casson (1989) and of the Bṛhatsaṃhitā by Bhat (1981–1982) are the primary texts most often cited here. For Tamil literature, Ramanujan (1985) gives a selection of Caṅkam poetry in fine translations and Parthasarathy (1993) a complete translation of the Cilappatikāram. Irfan Habib’s Technology in Medieval India (Habib 2008) is a compact, well-documented account of the period from about 650 to 1750, the one most thinly covered in general surveys.

    Metals¶

    Metallurgy is treated in detail in Volume 14. Srinivasan and Ranganathan (2004) and Bronson (1986) are the best starting points on crucible steel, Balasubramaniam (2002) on the Delhi pillar and Craddock (2017) on zinc, lead and silver; all are written by specialists who also correct popular exaggerations. Verrier Elwin’s The Agaria (Elwin 1942) is a classic ethnography of iron-smelters made just before the craft disappeared.

    Textiles and Dress¶

    For the global trade, Riello (2013) is the most important recent synthesis, arguing that Indian cottons shaped European industrialisation; Parthasarathi (2011) makes a related case on comparative grounds, and Beckert (2014) sets both in a world history of cotton. The essays in Riello and Roy (2009) and Riello and Parthasarathi (2009) are uneven but cover markets from Japan to West Africa. Gittinger (1982) and Irwin and Brett (1970) remain the best accounts of the dyer’s technique; Guy (1998) and Barnes (2005) are essential for the Indian Ocean, and Barnes (1997) for the finds from Egypt. On weavers and the Company, Hossain (1988) and Parthasarathi (2001) are detailed and complementary; on medieval south Indian weavers, Ramaswamy (1985). Tirthankar Roy’s Artisans and Industrialization (Roy 1993) and Traditional Industry (Roy 1999) are the indispensable revisionist accounts of handloom weaving and other industries under colonial rule. For Africa, Machado (2014) and Prestholdt (2008) write from the side of the consumers. On dress, Goswamy (1993) is the best guide to surviving garments, Tarlo (1996) the best study of what clothing meant to those who wore it, and Cohn (1996) the classic essay on dress and colonial rule.

    Other Crafts¶

    On jewellery, Untracht (1997) is encyclopaedic and based on long fieldwork. On instruments and their makers, Deva (1978) describes the instruments, and Krishna (2020) shows, for the mṛdaṅgam, how much the makers’ history has been left out. On leather, there is still no full history; Roy (1999) covers the colonial industry, Lynch (1969) the shoe-makers of Agra and Rawat (2011) the history of the “leather-working” caste label. On painting as a craft, Goswamy (1997) reconstructs a family of hill painters with exceptional care, and Mitter (1994) traces the split between artist and craftsman in colonial India. Losty (1982) is a well-illustrated introduction to the arts of the book, and Topsfield (2006) to board and card games.

    Caste, Society and Economy¶

    Bayly (1999) is the best general history of caste in the eighteenth to twentieth centuries. Ambedkar’s Annihilation of Caste (Ambedkar 1936) is short and should be read whole; Chakravarti (2003) explains why caste and gender cannot be studied apart, and Omvedt (2008) and Zelliot (1992) trace the traditions of anti-caste thought and movement. On artisan communities, Brouwer (1995) presents a Viśvakarma world-view on its own terms and Mines (1984) a weaving caste’s long history. For the jajmānī debate, read Wiser (1936) with the critique in Fuller (1989). On the colonial economy, Dutt (1902) and Naoroji (1901) state the nationalist case, Bagchi (1976) and Morris (1963) the two sides of the deindustrialisation debate, and Haynes (2012) carries the story of artisans and merchants into the twentieth century. On Svadeśī and khādī, Sarkar (1973), Bayly (1986) and Trivedi (2007).

    Craft Knowledge and Design¶

    On tacit knowledge, Polanyi (1958) is the philosophical source; on the place of theory and practice in Sanskrit learning, Pollock (1985). Dharampal (1971) collects European accounts of Indian technology and should be read critically, Raj (2007) for the circulation of knowledge, and Venkatesan (2009) for what development programmes do to craft knowledge. On design and the crafts, the Eameses’ India Report (Eames and Eames 1958) is short and still worth reading, and Mitter (2007) covers the Bauhaus exhibition of 1922.
