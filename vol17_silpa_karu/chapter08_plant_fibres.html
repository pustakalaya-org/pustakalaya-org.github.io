    Chapter 8: Veṇu and Tṛṇa: Bamboo, Cane and Plant Fibres¶

    Mats, baskets, ropes, fans, umbrellas and containers made from bamboo, cane, grasses, reeds, palm leaves and other plant fibres are among the most widespread and least studied of Indian crafts. They are cheap, perishable and usually made for daily use, often by women and by communities of low status, and they rarely appear in museums or texts. Yet they are technically sophisticated, closely adapted to local environments and essential to rural life. This chapter surveys the materials, the main products (mats, baskets, rope, coir and jute, leaf and pith work, fans and umbrellas) and the people who make them. The spinning and weaving of fibres into cloth are treated in Chapter 4, and the palm leaves, barks and papers used for writing in Chapter 11.

    8.1 Bamboo and Cane¶

    Bamboo (veṇu, vaṃśa) and cane (rattan) are the most versatile plant materials of India, especially in the North-East and the forested regions of central and southern India. They are used for houses, bridges, fences and furniture, for baskets, mats, fish-traps and storage containers, for musical instruments such as flutes and the plucked zither, and for tools and utensils, including water-carriers made from bamboo internodes. Basket-makers form distinct, often low-ranked, castes in many regions (Basor, Dom, Medara, Kaikāḍī), and Manu lists the basket-maker among those whose food a Brahmin should not eat (Chapter 1).

    The skills begin with the material. Bamboo is cut in the dry season, when it is least attacked by borers, and is often soaked in water for weeks, or stored above the hearth where smoke and heat season it and keep insects away. A culm is split with a heavy knife into strips and then into thin, flexible splints, which are scraped to an even thickness; the hard, glossy outer layer is used for fine and durable work, the softer inner layers for coarser things. Cane, the stem of climbing palms of the genus Calamus, is stripped of its spiny sheath and either used whole, for frames and bindings, or split into strands for weaving. From these materials makers produce plain, twilled and open hexagonal weaves, coiled work and lashed frames, choosing the weave for strength, flexibility or ventilation: open weaves for fish-traps and poultry cages, tight twills for grain baskets that are sometimes plastered with mud or dung.

    In the North-East, bamboo and cane work reaches great refinement: the finely woven cane baskets and hats of the Nagas, the Adis and other peoples of Arunachal Pradesh, the cane furniture of Tripura and Assam, and the Assamese jāpi, a conical sun- and rain-hat of split bamboo and palm leaves, which has become a symbol of Assamese identity (Chapter 12 and Volume 23). In the hills, bamboo and cane are also materials of construction. Houses on bamboo stilts, with floors and walls of split and woven bamboo, are general, and the Adi and other peoples of Arunachal Pradesh built suspension bridges of cane across the rivers of the eastern Himalaya, with a walkway hung from cane cables and enclosed in a tube of cane hoops. Such bridges, and the village houses, were built and repaired by collective labour, and the knowledge of where to find suitable canes, when to cut them and how to join them was held by the community rather than by a caste of specialists. In this the North-East differs from much of India, where basket-making was the hereditary work of particular, often low-ranked, castes.

    8.2 Grasses, Palms and Bast Fibres¶

    The raw materials of the fibre crafts are as varied as India’s environments:

    • Grasses and sedges, such as muñja and kāśa (species of Saccharum), kuśa (Desmostachya bipinnata), the korai sedge of Tamil Nadu, sabai grass of eastern India, sikkī grass of Bihar, and reeds of many kinds.

    • Palm leaves and fibres, from the palmyra, date, coconut and other palms, used for mats, baskets, fans, umbrellas, thatch and writing material.

    • Bast fibres, from the stems of jute, hemp, flax, ramie and other plants, used for rope, sacking and coarse cloth.

    • Coir, the fibre of the coconut husk, used for rope, matting and brushes.

    • Leaves and sheaths, such as the leaves of the sal and palāśa trees, stitched into plates and cups, and banana fibre and sheath.

    Many of these plants have ritual as well as practical uses. Kuśa grass is spread around the Vedic fire and used to make purificatory rings; the sacred thread of the twice-born and the girdle of the Vedic student were made of specified grasses and fibres, such as muñja. Leaf plates made of sal or palāśa leaves are used at feasts and in rituals because they are new and are thrown away after use.

    8.3 Mats¶

    Mats were the basic furniture of most Indian homes, used for sitting, sleeping, drying grain and praying, and several regional traditions are renowned for their fineness. The most celebrated are the mats of Pattamadai in Tirunelveli district of Tamil Nadu, woven by Labbai Muslim families from split and dyed korai sedge; the finest are soft enough to be folded like cloth. Soumhya Venkatesan’s study of the Pattamadai weavers shows how state training schemes and the language of national heritage have reshaped what counts as skill in the craft and who is recognised as holding it (Venkatesan 2009) (Chapter 13).

    In Bengal, the madur reed of Medinipur is woven into madurkāṭhi mats on a simple loom with a warp of cotton or jute. The “cool mats” (śītalpāṭī) of Cooch Behar, Assam and the Sylhet region are woven from split strips of the stem of a marsh plant (Schumannianthus dichotomus); they are smooth and cool to sleep on, and their weaving in Sylhet has been inscribed by UNESCO (Chapter 12). In Manipur the water reed called kauna is made into mats, cushions and baskets, and in coastal regions throughout the south and east palm-leaf mats are woven, often by women. Mats were also made in prisons and by refugees, and many mat-weaving traditions were adapted for urban and export markets in the twentieth century; several have been registered as Geographical Indications.

    8.4 Baskets and Coiled Work¶

    Baskets are made by plaiting, twining, wickerwork and coiling. Plaited baskets of bamboo and palm leaf are the most common in India. Coiled basketry, in which a bundle of grass is wound round with a binding strip and stitched into a spiral, is used for fine containers and decorative objects. In the wet lands of north Bihar, women of the Mithilā region dye the golden sikkī grass in bright colours and coil it into boxes, baskets, toys and figures, which were traditionally part of a bride’s dowry. In Odisha, Jharkhand and West Bengal, and especially in Mayurbhanj, Ādivāsī and other communities twist sabai grass into rope and coil it into baskets, mats and bags. Around Prayagraj (Allahabad), women make coiled baskets with a core of kāśa grass bound with strips of muñja (moonj).

    8.5 Rope and Cordage¶

    Rope was needed everywhere: for wells, carts, boats, buildings, beds (the woven cot, chārpāī, has a frame strung with rope or tape) and animals. The rope-maker (rajjusarja) appears in the Yajurveda’s list of occupations (Chapter 1). Ropes were made from muñja and other grasses, hemp, jute, coir and the fibres of trees and creepers, by twisting yarns into strands and strands into rope, often with simple wheels or hooks. The sewn boats of the Indian Ocean depended on coir cordage (Chapter 7).

    8.6 Coir¶

    Coir is the coarse, water-resistant fibre of the husk of the coconut. In Kerala, coconut husks were traditionally soaked (“retted”) for months in the backwaters and lagoons until the fibre could be beaten out by hand with wooden mallets, a task done mainly by women of the Īḻava and other communities. The fibre was spun by hand into yarn on simple wheels, and the yarn woven into mats and matting or twisted into rope. Coir has been exported since at least the medieval period, when Arab writers noted its use for sewing ships, and in the nineteenth century European firms established coir factories at Alappuzha (Alleppey), which became the centre of the industry. The Coir Board was established under the Coir Industry Act of 1953.

    The coir industry has long been associated with poor working conditions, especially for the women who ret and beat the husks, and it was one of the bases of the labour movement in Kerala. The retting of husks in backwaters also pollutes the water. Mechanisation of defibring and spinning has reduced the drudgery of the work but also the number of jobs.

    8.7 Jute¶

    Jute, a bast fibre from plants of the genus Corchorus, was grown in Bengal and spun and woven by hand into coarse cloth, sacking and rope for local use. In the nineteenth century, after the fibre was successfully spun by machine in Dundee in Scotland, jute became a great industrial commodity. The first jute mill near Calcutta was set up at Rishra in 1855, and by the early twentieth century the mills along the Hughli were the largest jute industry in the world, producing sacking for the world’s grain, cotton and sugar trade. The cultivation of jute by peasants in eastern Bengal, and its processing in Calcutta’s mills, belong to the history of colonial industry rather than of crafts. Since the late twentieth century, however, hand-made jute bags, mats and decorative goods have been revived as crafts in Bengal as an alternative to plastic.

    8.8 Leaf, Sheath and Pith¶

    Other plant materials are used for specialised crafts:

    • Leaf plates and cups (pattal, dona) of sal, palāśa, banana or areca sheath, stitched with thin twigs or pressed in moulds; their production employs many forest-dwelling women and is promoted as an alternative to disposable plastic.

    • Sola pith (śolā), the light white pith of a marsh plant (Aeschynomene), carved by the Mālākār community of Bengal into the elaborate crowns worn by brides and grooms, the decorations of the images of Durgā, and models of temples and figures. Sola pith was also used for the sun-helmets (“sola topi”) of the colonial period.

    • Palm-leaf craft, from fans and umbrellas to toys and boxes; the preparation of palm leaves for writing is described in Chapter 11.

    • Banana fibre, extracted from the stem and used for rope, mats and, in recent years, for fabrics and paper.

    8.9 Fans and Umbrellas¶

    Hand-fans (pankhā) of palm leaf, bamboo, grass, cloth and peacock feathers were essential in the Indian heat, from the simple palm-leaf fan of the village to the embroidered and beaded fans of courts and the large ceremonial fans and fly-whisks (cāmara) that were emblems of royalty and divinity, carried beside kings and images of gods. The large cloth fan suspended from the ceiling and pulled by a servant, the pankhā of colonial bungalows, required the constant labour of “punkah-wallahs”. Umbrellas of palm leaf, bamboo and cloth were made in many regions; the parasol (chatra) was a symbol of sovereignty in Indian kingship and Buddhist art, and ceremonial umbrellas are still made for temple processions, as in the appliqué umbrellas of Pipli in Odisha (Chapter 12) and the decorated umbrellas displayed on elephants at the Pūram festival of Thrissur in Kerala.

    8.10 The Makers of Fibre Crafts¶

    The fibre crafts are mostly practised by women and by communities of low status or Ādivāsī origin: basket-makers such as the Basor, Turi, Dom and Medara, mat-weavers, rope-makers and leaf-plate makers. Their products are cheap, their incomes low, and their skills seldom recognised as “craft” by buyers or officials. Many depend on access to common lands, wetlands and forests for their raw materials, and have suffered as these lands have been enclosed, drained or protected. The same fibre crafts are now promoted as sustainable alternatives to plastic, and a few, such as sikkī work and Pattamadai mats, have found high-value urban markets. Whether the makers gain from that interest depends less on the materials than on who controls the trade (Chapter 15).

    Further Reading¶

    For a regional encyclopaedia of contemporary crafts, including bamboo, cane, grass and fibre work, Ranjan and Ranjan (2007); for a classic survey, Chattopadhyay (1975); for the Pattamadai mat-weavers, Venkatesan (2009); for the commercial products of plant fibres in the early twentieth century, Watt (1908).
