    Chapter 40: Phrasal verbs¶

    Phrasal verbs — two words, a new meaning

    A phrasal verb is a verb + a small word (up, off, out, after…) that together make a completely new meaning. Give = to hand over. Up = in the upward direction. But give up = to stop trying. This is why you can know every word and still not understand English — and it is why learning phrasal verbs is one of the most rewarding things you can do for your vocabulary. Native speakers use them more than big words.

    In one line

    Remember a phrasal verb as one word — do not try to work out the meaning by adding up the parts. Don't think of "look after" as look + later; just remember it as "take care of".

    40.1 The personality of the small word¶

    Every particle (up, off, out…) carries a feeling that shows up in almost every phrasal verb it is part of. Once you understand that feeling, you can start guessing new phrasal verbs on your own.

    40.2 Separable vs inseparable — the real rule¶

    This part causes the most mistakes. The question is: does the object (the thing the action is done to) come before the particle or after it?

    Three kinds of phrasal verbs

    A. Separable — the object can go in the middle or after:
    Turn down the offer ✓ · Turn the offer down ✓

    B. Inseparable — the object always comes after the particle:
    Look after the baby ✓ · Look the baby after ✗

    C. No object — nothing follows:
    Please carry on. · Don't give up.

    The firm pronoun rule — never forget it

    With a separable phrasal verb, when the object is it, him, her, them, us, me, it must go in the middle. There is no choice here.
    ✓ Turn it down. · ✗ Turn down it.
    ✓ Pick her up at six. · ✗ Pick up her at six.
    ✓ Put it off till Monday. · ✗ Put off it till Monday. A pronoun is short and light — it cannot trail behind, so it sits in the middle.

    One more easy clue: if a phrasal verb is already followed by a preposition (get along with, run out of), it is always inseparable.

    40.3 By group — eleven essential phrasal verbs¶

    A. With people — relationships and care¶

    Phrasal verb Meaning Type
    look after take care of Inseparable
    get along (with) have a good relationship Inseparable
    bring up raise a child; or mention a topic Separable
    • Can you look after my bag for ten minutes? = Can you watch my bag for ten minutes?
    • I get along with almost everyone on the team. = I have a good relationship with almost everyone on the team.
    • She brought up the budget issue at the end of the call. = She mentioned the budget issue at the end of the call.
    • He was brought up by his grandparents in Nagpur. = His grandparents raised him in Nagpur.

    An interview line

    "I get along well with people from different teams." Sounds more natural, and more honest, than "I am a good team player".

    B. Starting, continuing and finishing¶

    Phrasal verb Meaning Type
    give up stop trying; quit (a habit) Separable / no object
    carry on continue No object (or "with")
    run out of have none left (stock) Inseparable
    • Don't give up now — you're two days from finishing. = Don't stop trying now — only two days are left.
    • He gave up sugar in January and hasn't looked back. = He quit sugar in January and has never gone back to it.
    • Sorry to interrupt — please carry on. = Sorry for interrupting — please continue.
    • We've run out of milk again. = We have no milk left, again.

    run out of — two mistakes

    • Wrong: The milk run out.
    • Right: We've run out of milk. / The milk has run out.

    • Why: When you say what is missing, "of" is needed. Without "of", only the thing itself can be the subject.

    • Wrong: I am running out from time.

    • Right: I'm running out of time.
    • Why: Always "out of" — never "out from".

    C. Decisions and work — office phrasal verbs¶

    Phrasal verb Meaning Type
    put off postpone, delay Separable
    turn down refuse (an offer, a request); lower the volume Separable
    sort out solve, fix, organise Separable
    • Let's put the review off until Thursday. = Let's postpone the review until Thursday.
    • She turned down the offer — the pay was good but the city wasn't. = She refused the offer — the salary was good, but she did not like the city.
    • Give me an hour and I'll sort it out. = Give me an hour and I will fix everything.

    'I'll sort it out' — pure gold

    When something goes wrong at the office, these three words build more trust than anything else. You do not need to say more.

    D. Chance and learning¶

    Phrasal verb Meaning Type
    come across find by chance; or give an impression Inseparable
    pick up lift; collect someone; learn casually Separable
    • I came across an old photo of us while clearing my desk. = I found an old photo of us by chance while clearing my desk.
    • He comes across as shy, but he isn't. = He seems shy, but he is not.
    • Can you pick the kids up at four? = Can you collect the kids at four?
    • She picked up Tamil just by living there. = She learned Tamil simply by living there.

    'comes across as' — very useful

    A gentle way to give an opinion about someone: "The email comes across as a bit harsh." You are not saying it is bad — only that it seems that way. At work, that difference matters.

    40.4 At a glance — the full list¶

    Phrasal verb Meaning Separable? With a pronoun
    give up stop trying / quit Yes give it up
    look after take care of No look after her
    pick up lift / collect / learn Yes pick it up
    put off postpone Yes put it off
    turn down refuse / lower the volume Yes turn it down
    come across find by chance / seem No come across it
    carry on continue No object carry on
    run out of have none left No run out of it
    sort out solve Yes sort it out
    get along (with) have a good relationship No get along with him
    bring up raise / mention Yes bring it up

    40.5 The most common mistakes¶

    Pay attention

    • Wrong: Please turn down it, the music is too loud.
    • Right: Please turn it down, the music is too loud.

    • Why: With a separable phrasal verb, a pronoun (it, him, her, them) must go in the middle.

    • Wrong: She will look the baby after.

    • Right: She will look after the baby.

    • Why: Look after is inseparable — the object never goes in the middle, even a pronoun: look after her.

    • Wrong: I gave up from the exam.

    • Right: I gave up on the exam.

    • Why: To lose hope in something = give up on. There is no "give up from".

    • Wrong: We are running out from seats.

    • Right: We are running out of seats.

    • Why: Always "out of". Remember the pair as if it were one word.

    • Wrong: Let's prepone the meeting to Monday.

    • Right: Let's move the meeting up to Monday.

    • Why: "Prepone" is only understood in India. To make something earlier = move up / bring forward. To make it later = put off / push back.

    • Wrong: I get along my manager very well.

    • Right: I get along with my manager very well.

    • Why: With a person, "with" is needed. Without "with", it means "I am managing".

    • Wrong: He came across very rude.

    • Right: He came across as very rude.
    • Why: When the meaning is "seemed", "as" is needed. Without "as", it means "met by chance".

    40.6 A short conversation — seven phrasal verbs together¶

    Read it aloud twice

    A: We've run out of chairs, and I've still got forty forms to sort out.
    B: Don't give up — I'll pick some up from the store room. Should we put the briefing off by an hour?
    A: Good idea. Can you bring that up with Meera? She gets along with the facilities team.
    B: Done. You carry on with the forms.

    40.7 Practice — Phrasal verbs¶

    Exercise 1

    The music is too loud. Which sentence is correct?

    • Please turn it down.
    • Please turn down it.
    • Please turn down.
    • Please it turn down.

    Hint: Where does the pronoun go?

    Answer

    Please turn it down.

    Turn down is separable, and the pronoun (it) must go in the middle. 'Turn down it' is never correct.

    Exercise 2

    Which phrasal verb is inseparable — the object never goes in the middle?

    • look after
    • put off
    • sort out
    • bring up
    Answer

    look after

    Look after is inseparable: look after her ✓, look her after ✗. The other three are separable.

    Exercise 3

    What does 'He comes across as shy' mean?

    • He seems shy
    • He meets shy people
    • He has become shy
    • He is not shy
    Answer

    He seems shy

    'Come across as' = seem, give an impression. Note that 'as' is needed.

    Exercise 4

    You want to make a meeting earlier, not later. What do you say?

    • Let's move the meeting up.
    • Let's prepone the meeting.
    • Let's put the meeting off.
    • Let's turn the meeting down.

    Hint: 'Prepone' is not understood outside India.

    Answer

    Let's move the meeting up.

    Making it earlier = move up / bring forward. Put off means the opposite (postpone), and turn down means refuse.

    Exercise 5

    We've run ___ of printer paper again.

    Hint: This pair never changes.

    Answer

    out

    Always 'run out of' — 'run out from', or just 'run out' + a thing, is wrong.

    Exercise 6

    I ___ along with everyone on my team.

    Hint: Have a good relationship.

    Answer

    get

    Get along with = have a good relationship. With a person, 'with' is needed.

    Exercise 7

    She ___ up the budget problem right at the end of the call.

    Hint: Mention a topic.

    Answer

    brought

    Bring up has two meanings: raise a child, and mention a topic. Here it is the second one.

    Exercise 8

    The word order in this sentence is wrong — tap the wrong word.

    She will look the baby after this evening.

    Answer

    Wrong word: after.

    Corrected: She will look after the baby this evening.

    Look after is inseparable — the object comes after the particle, never in the middle.

    Exercise 9

    One word is wrong — find it.

    I finally gave up from trying to fix the printer.

    Answer

    Wrong word: from.

    Corrected: I finally gave up on trying to fix the printer.

    To lose hope = give up on. There is no 'give up from' in English.

    Exercise 10

    Match each phrasal verb with its meaning.

    A B
    put off solve
    turn down find by chance
    sort out postpone
    come across refuse
    Answer

    put off — postpone; turn down — refuse; sort out — solve; come across — find by chance

    All four are used at the office every day. Note: the first three are separable, come across is not.

    Exercise 11

    Match each particle with its core feeling.

    A B
    up continue
    off moving together
    on completely, finished
    along apart, moving away
    Answer

    up — completely, finished; off — apart, moving away; on — continue; along — moving together

    Once you understand the feeling of the particle, half the meaning of a new phrasal verb guesses itself.

    Exercise 12

    Build the sentence — watch the pronoun.

    Words: hour / it / an / out / sort / Give / I / and / me / will

    Answer

    Give me an hour and I will sort it out

    'Sort it out' — the pronoun goes in the middle. 'Sort out it' is wrong.

    Exercise 13

    Build the sentence.

    Words: four / you / Can / pick / kids / at / up / the

    Answer

    Can you pick the kids up at four

    With a separable phrasal verb, a noun can go in the middle or after: 'pick up the kids' is also correct.

    Exercise 14

    Say this line. The three words of 'sort it out' join together and flow as one.

    Answer

    Say: Don't worry I will sort it out

    This is the most trusted line when something goes wrong at the office. When speaking, 'sort-it-out' comes out like a single wave.

    What to do next

    Don't memorise all eleven phrasal verbs at once. Pick just three today — sort out, get along with, put off — and use them in sentences from your real life (your work, your people, your names). The sentence that is yours is the one you remember.
