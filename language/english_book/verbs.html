    Chapter 10: Verbs¶

    Verb — the heart of the sentence

    A verb is a word that shows an action, an occurrence or a state. If you could fix only one thing in all of English grammar, fix the verb — because tense, voice, agreement and making questions all depend on it. This chapter is a little long, but after it many other chapters will start to feel easy.

    In one line

    A verb shows an action, a state, an occurrence or possession. A verb can show something being done (action), a condition (state), something that happens (occurrence), or having something (possession). Without a verb, a sentence is not complete.

    • She writes a letter. Action — something is being done: she writes a letter.
    • He is happy. State — no action, just a condition: he feels happy.
    • It rained yesterday. Occurrence — something happened, nobody did it: there was rain yesterday.
    • They have a car. Possession — someone owns something: the car belongs to them.

    10.1 Types of verb — the whole tree¶

    Verbs can be divided in several ways, and these divisions overlap. One verb can be a "main verb", "transitive" and "irregular" all at once — like write. So don't think of the tree as a competition; think of it as four separate questions.

    10.2 Main verb and helping verb¶

    In a sentence, the real meaning comes from the main verb. The small verbs that come with it — is, am, are, has, have, will, do — help to make tenses, questions and negatives. These are called auxiliary or helping verbs.

    • She is writing a letter. "writing" is the real action; "is" only shows that it is happening right now.
    • Do you know him? "Do" comes first to make the question; the meaning is in "know".
    Helping verb Forms Job
    be is, am, are, was, were, been, being making continuous and passive
    have have, has, had making perfect tenses
    do do, does, did questions, negatives and emphasis

    All three can also be main verbs

    I have a bike. — here "have" is the main verb itself.
    I have finished my work. — here "have" is the helper and "finished" is the main verb. Check whether another verb follows — that decides which one it is.

    10.3 Modal verbs — the ones that never change¶

    Modals are helping verbs that show possibility, ability, permission or obligation. They have two fixed rules, and remembering both is enough.

    The two fixed rules for modals

    1. After a modal, the verb is always in its base form — no -s, no -ing, no to.
    2. A modal never changes itself — no -s, no -ed. ✓ She can swim. ✗ She cans swim. ✗ She can swims.

    Modal What it shows Example
    can ability, general permission I can drive.
    could past ability, politeness Could you help me?
    may formal permission, possibility May I come in?
    might weaker possibility It might rain today.
    will future, promise I will call you.
    would politeness, imagination I would like tea.
    shall suggestion (formal) Shall we start?
    should advice You should rest.
    must strong obligation You must wear a helmet.
    ought to moral duty We ought to help them.

    can and could — the difference when asking permission

    Can I borrow your pen? — fine among friends.
    Could I borrow your pen? — more polite, good with someone senior.
    May I borrow your pen? — the most formal, for an office or an interview.

    10.4 Transitive and intransitive — does it take an object?¶

    After the verb, ask "what?" or "whom?". If you get an answer, the verb is transitive. If the question makes no sense, the verb is intransitive.

    Verb Question Answer Type
    She wrote a poem. Wrote what? a poem ✓ Transitive
    He slept early. Slept what? no answer ✗ Intransitive
    They helped us. Helped whom? us ✓ Transitive
    The baby laughed. Laughed what? no answer ✗ Intransitive

    Many verbs can be both

    The bell rang. (intransitive) / She rang the bell. (transitive)
    The window broke. / He broke the window. So look at the verb inside the sentence, not in a list.

    An intransitive verb needs a preposition

    • Wrong: We will reach at Delhi at 9.
    • Right: We will reach Delhi at 9.

    • Why: "Reach" is transitive — it takes its object directly. But "arrive" is intransitive, so it needs a preposition: arrive in Delhi, arrive at the airport.

    • Wrong: Let us discuss about the plan.

    • Right: Let us discuss the plan.
    • Why: "Discuss" is transitive, so the object follows directly. Hindi has "ke baare me" (about), which is why "about" sneaks in. In the same way: enter the room (not enter into), marry her (not marry with her).

    10.5 Linking verbs — verbs that build a bridge¶

    A linking verb shows no action. It connects the subject to its identity or condition — like a bridge.

    Linking verb Example Note
    be (is, am, are, was, were) He is busy. the most common
    seem, appear She seems tired. it looks that way
    become, get, turn, grow The sky turned dark. change
    feel, look, smell, sound, taste This tastes sweet. the senses
    remain, stay He remained calm. staying the same

    After a linking verb, use an adjective, not an adverb

    ✓ The food tastes good. ✗ The food tastes well.
    ✓ She looks beautiful. ✗ She looks beautifully. These verbs describe the subject, not an action. The subject is a noun, and a noun needs an adjective.

    10.6 The five forms of a verb¶

    Every English verb has five forms. Dictionaries usually list three — V1, V2, V3 — and these three are what you use to build tenses.

    Form Name walk (regular) write (irregular) Where it is used
    V1 base walk write present, after a modal, with to
    V1-s third person walks writes with he / she / it
    V2 past simple walked wrote simple past
    V3 past participle walked written after have/has/had, in the passive
    V-ing present participle walking writing continuous tenses

    The difference between regular and irregular

    Regular: V2 and V3 both just add -ed — play → played → played.
    Irregular: the word changes inside — go → went → gone. English has about 200 irregular verbs, but 60-70 are enough for everyday use.

    Irregular verbs you use every day¶

    V1 V2 V3 V1 V2 V3
    go went gone eat ate eaten
    see saw seen take took taken
    do did done give gave given
    come came come run ran run
    buy bought bought bring brought brought
    think thought thought teach taught taught
    speak spoke spoken break broke broken
    write wrote written drive drove driven
    begin began begun drink drank drunk
    put put put cut cut cut

    Mixing up V2 and V3

    • Wrong: I have saw that movie.
    • Right: I have seen that movie.

    • Why: After "have / has / had" you always need V3 — seen, gone, done, written. V2 (saw) is only for the past on its own: I saw that movie.

    • Wrong: He did not went to school.

    • Right: He did not go to school.
    • Why: "did" has already shown the past — so the main verb stays in its base form. We don't show the past twice in one sentence.

    10.7 Finite and non-finite verbs¶

    A finite verb changes with the subject and the tense — it is the real verb of the sentence. A non-finite verb doesn't change; it works like a noun or an adjective.

    Type Form Example Job
    Finite changes with subject + tense She plays. / They played. the real verb
    Infinitive to + V1 I want to sleep. works like a noun
    Gerund V-ing, used as a noun Swimming is good exercise. works like a noun
    Participle V-ing or V3, used as an adjective a broken glass / a running train works like an adjective

    Only one finite verb per clause

    I want to go home. — "want" is finite, "to go" is non-finite. So the tense goes only on "want": I wanted to go home. You never say "to goed" — now you can see why.

    After a preposition, use a gerund, not an infinitive

    ✓ She is good at singing. ✗ good at to sing
    ✓ I look forward to meeting you. ✗ to meet you
    Here "to" is a preposition, not part of an infinitive — so use -ing.

    10.8 Stative verbs — the ones that don't go in the continuous¶

    Some verbs show a state, not an action. A state doesn't "go on" — it just is — so we don't put these verbs into -ing.

    Category Verbs
    Thinking know, think (meaning "believe"), believe, understand, remember, forget, mean
    Feelings like, love, hate, want, need, prefer
    Senses see, hear, smell, taste (meaning "have a flavour")
    Ownership have (meaning "own"), own, belong, possess
    Being be, seem, appear, cost, weigh, contain

    10.9 The mistakes we really make¶

    The most common Indian-English mistakes with verbs

    • Wrong: I am having a car.
    • Right: I have a car.

    • Why: When "have" means ownership, it is a stative verb — it doesn't go in the continuous. "I am having" is fine only when an action is happening: I am having lunch, I am having a bath.

    • Wrong: He don't know the answer.

    • Right: He doesn't know the answer.

    • Why: He / she / it are third person singular, and their helping verb is "does". And notice — after "does" the main verb stays in the base form: doesn't know, not doesn't knows.

    • Wrong: I am agree with you.

    • Right: I agree with you.

    • Why: "Agree" is a complete verb on its own — it doesn't need "am" before it. The Hindi "main sehmat hoon" has "hoon" (am) in it, which is why "am" sneaks in.

    • Wrong: She is knowing the answer.

    • Right: She knows the answer.

    • Why: "Know" is stative — knowing is a state, not an action. That is why "I am knowing", "I am understanding" and "I am liking" are all wrong.

    • Wrong: We discussed about the project yesterday.

    • Right: We discussed the project yesterday.

    • Why: "Discuss" is transitive — it takes its object directly, with no preposition. Same list: enter the hall, attend the meeting, answer the question, reach home.

    • Wrong: One of my friend don't like coffee.

    • Right: One of my friends doesn't like coffee.

    • Why: Two mistakes at once: after "one of" you need a plural noun (friends), but the real subject is "one" — singular — so the verb is singular too: doesn't.

    • Wrong: Please revert back to me soon.

    • Right: Please reply to me soon.

    • Why: "Revert" means to go back to an earlier state, not to reply. And "back" repeats the idea. In an office email, write "please reply" or "please get back to me".

    • Wrong: He can plays the guitar very well.

    • Right: He can play the guitar very well.
    • Why: After a modal the verb is always in the base form. The modal has already taken care of the job, so the verb needs no -s.

    10.10 A short memory map¶

    After this Verb form Example
    he / she / it (present) V1 + s She goes.
    do / does / did V1 She didn't go.
    modal (can, will, must…) V1 She can go.
    have / has / had V3 She has gone.
    is / am / are / was / were V-ing (or V3 in the passive) She is going.
    to V1 She wants to go.
    preposition (at, of, in, for…) V-ing She is tired of waiting.

    10.11 Practice — Verbs¶

    Exercise 1

    She ___ the answer to this question.

    • knows
    • is knowing
    • knowing
    • are knowing

    Hint: Is this verb stative or an action?

    Answer

    knows

    'Know' is a stative verb — knowing is a state, not an ongoing action. So we don't use it in the continuous, even when we mean right now.

    Exercise 2

    He ___ live in Mumbai anymore.

    • doesn't
    • don't
    • isn't
    • didn't

    Hint: The negative for third person singular.

    Answer

    doesn't

    'He' is third person singular, so the helping verb is 'does'. 'don't' goes only with I, you, we, they.

    Exercise 3

    I have ___ that film twice already.

    • seen
    • saw
    • see
    • seeing

    Hint: Which form comes after 'have'?

    Answer

    seen

    After have / has / had you always use V3 (the past participle) — seen. 'saw' is V2, which is for the plain past on its own: I saw that film.

    Exercise 4

    Which sentence is correct?

    • She can play the sitar.
    • She can plays the sitar.
    • She cans play the sitar.
    • She can to play the sitar.
    Answer

    She can play the sitar.

    After a modal the verb is in its base form, and the modal itself never changes. So not 'cans', not 'plays', not 'to play'.

    Exercise 5

    'The soup tastes ___.' — what fills the gap?

    • delicious
    • deliciously
    • deliciousness
    • tastily

    Hint: 'taste' is a linking verb here.

    Answer

    delicious

    After a linking verb you use an adjective, because it describes the subject — what the soup is like. An adverb would be used only to describe how an action is done.

    Exercise 6

    I am looking forward to ___ you next week. (meet)

    Hint: 'to' here is a preposition, not an infinitive.

    Answer

    meeting

    In 'look forward to', 'to' is a preposition, and after a preposition we use a gerund (-ing). That is why 'to meet' is wrong here.

    Exercise 7

    He did not ___ to the office yesterday. (go)

    Hint: 'did' has already shown the past.

    Answer

    go

    After 'did' the verb stays in the base form, because the past is already in 'did'. We don't show the past twice in one sentence.

    Exercise 8

    Tap the wrong word in this sentence.

    I am having two brothers and one sister.

    Answer

    Wrong word: having.

    Corrected: I have two brothers and one sister.

    When 'have' means a relationship or ownership, it is stative — it doesn't go in the continuous. 'I am having' works only for actions like eating or drinking.

    Exercise 9

    Find the wrong word.

    We discussed about the new policy in the meeting.

    Answer

    Wrong word: about.

    Corrected: We discussed the new policy in the meeting.

    'Discuss' is a transitive verb — it takes its object directly. 'about' comes in from the Hindi 'ke baare me', but in English it is extra.

    Exercise 10

    'It rained heavily last night.' — what is the verb showing here?

    • occurrence
    • possession
    • state
    • action

    Hint: Nobody did anything; it just happened.

    Answer

    occurrence

    Rain isn't something anyone does; it is an event — so it is an occurrence. 'She writes' would be an action, 'He is happy' a state, and 'They have a car' possession.

    Exercise 11

    Match each sentence with what its verb shows.

    A B
    She writes a letter. occurrence
    He is happy. possession
    It rained yesterday. action
    They have a car. state
    Answer

    She writes a letter. — action; He is happy. — state; It rained yesterday. — occurrence; They have a car. — possession

    A verb shows one of four things — an action, a state, an occurrence, or possession.

    Exercise 12

    We ___ a big house in the village. (possession, present)

    Hint: Someone owns something.

    Answer

    have

    For possession use 'have / has' — We have, She has. 'have' meaning possession is stative, so we don't say 'We are having a house'.

    Exercise 13

    Match each verb form with where it is used.

    A B
    after have / has V-ing — going, writing
    after a modal V1 + s — goes, writes
    after is / am / are V3 — gone, written
    with he / she in the present V1 — go, write
    Answer

    after have / has — V3 — gone, written; after a modal — V1 — go, write; after is / am / are — V-ing — going, writing; with he / she in the present — V1 + s — goes, writes

    The form of a verb is decided by the word before it. Once you remember these four pairs, half the work of tenses is done.

    Exercise 14

    Put the words in the right order — it uses a modal.

    Words: wear / must / helmet / riding / while / You / a

    Answer

    You must wear a helmet while riding

    The order is subject + modal + V1 + the rest. After 'must', 'wear' stays in the base form — never 'wears'.

    Exercise 15

    Say this line aloud — say 'doesn't' in one breath.

    Answer

    Say: He doesn't know the answer

    If we say 'does not' as two separate words, the habit of 'he don't' doesn't go away. Saying 'doesn't' as a single word brings out the right form automatically.

    What to do next

    Take five irregular verbs every day and say all three forms out loud — go went gone, see saw seen. In two weeks the whole list will be on your tongue, and you will already know half of the tenses chapter.
