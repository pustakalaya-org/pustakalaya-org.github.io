    Chapter 14: Interpreting Tantra: Ethics, Reception and Scholarship¶

    14.1 Introduction¶

    The Tantric traditions have seldom been described without being judged. Their own authors ranked them above the Veda; Brahmanical critics called them heretical; colonial missionaries saw in them the worst of Hinduism; Indian reformers disowned them or reinterpreted them; and modern Western readers have made them, by turns, a scandal, a philosophy and a technique of pleasure. The preceding chapters have described the traditions themselves. This one turns to the questions that their judges have asked. It considers first what the traditions prescribe about conduct and how they justified their transgressions; then the history of how “Tantra” has been condemned, defended, marketed and studied since the late eighteenth century; and finally how scholars now define the subject, and what remains of the traditions as living religions.

    14.2 The Ethics of Tantra¶

    The Āgamas bind their initiates with detailed rules (samayas): devotion to the guru and the deity, regular worship, truthfulness, purity, secrecy and the avoidance of harm, with expiations for every breach (Section 9.8). The Siddhānta, the most widely followed form of Tantric Śaivism, largely accepted the norms of Brahmanical society, and its manuals read in many places like codes of householder discipline. The Bhairava and Kaula traditions, and the Buddhist Yoginītantras, prescribed rites that deliberately violated those norms: the consumption of meat and alcohol, sexual ritual and the use of impure substances (Chapter 4). Their stated rationale was that the conventional distinction of pure and impure is itself a form of bondage, and that its ritual transcendence frees the initiate from the limited self (Sanderson 1988; White 2003). The same texts confined these rites to qualified initiates and condemned those who used them for indulgence, and the Buddhist vows required the practitioner never to abandon compassion for beings (Section 10.7.1).

    Two further elements belong to the historical record as much as the philosophy does. The manuals contain rites for subjugating, paralysing, driving away and even killing enemies, the “six acts” (Section 7.3.3), whose ethics troubled the traditions themselves; and many Āgamic and Śākta traditions practised animal sacrifice (Section 4.12). On caste and gender, the earlier chapters have shown that Tantric openness to initiates of all castes and to women was real but limited: caste distinctions were often suspended in the ritual and retained in social life, priesthood became hereditary and caste-bound, and texts composed by men frequently treated women as means to male attainment (Sections 1.8 and 4.14). The debates over temple priesthood discussed in Section 11.10 show that these questions remain open.

    14.3 Tantra in Modern Hindu Movements¶

    Modern Hindu attitudes to Tantra have been ambivalent. Rāmakṛṣṇa (1836–1886) practised a series of Tantric disciplines under a female teacher at Dakṣiṇeśvar (Section 4.14.3), yet his disciple Vivekānanda, while revering the Goddess, criticised the left-hand rites and presented Vedānta as the core of Hinduism. In Bengal, scholars such as Atal Bihari Ghose worked with Woodroffe on the Tantrik Texts, and Gopinath Kaviraj (1887–1976) devoted a lifetime of scholarship and practice to Tantric traditions. Sri Aurobindo drew on the Tantric affirmation of the world and of Śakti in his integral yoga. In Kashmir, Svāmī Lakṣmaṇ Jū taught the Trika, and in South India Śrīvidyā gurus initiated growing circles of urban disciples (Urban 2003; Brooks 1992).

    At the same time, the word “tāntrik” retains, in much of India, connotations of black magic, and self-styled tantriks offering exorcism, love charms or cures have been prosecuted under laws against superstition and fraud, such as Maharashtra’s anti-superstition act of 2013. The gap between the learned traditions described in this volume and the popular image of the “tantrik” remains wide.

    14.4 “Tantra” in Colonial and Modern Perception¶

    The modern image of Tantra was made in two stages. In the nineteenth century missionaries, orientalists and many Hindu reformers described “Tantrism” as the degenerate underside of Hinduism, and Sir John Woodroffe (Arthur Avalon) and his Indian collaborators answered by presenting it as a profound and ethically respectable philosophy. In the twentieth century Western teachers and writers reinvented it as a technique of sexual fulfilment and spiritual self-development. Hugh Urban has shown how the condemnation, the rehabilitation and the reinvention each constructed “Tantra” as a category in response to the concerns of its own time (Urban 2003). Each stage left its mark on how the traditions are still perceived.

    14.4.1 Missionaries, Orientalists and Reformers¶

    The first detailed European accounts of Tantric worship came from Bengal, where missionaries and East India Company scholars met the Śākta religion of the region at first hand. The Baptist missionary William Ward of Serampore, whose account of the Hindus (first published in 1811 and enlarged in later editions) was widely read in Britain, described the left-hand rites as the extreme point of Hindu idolatry and moral corruption; Horace Hayman Wilson’s survey of the Hindu sects (1828–1832) treated the Śāktas more soberly but still as a degenerate late development, and Monier Monier-Williams later wrote in the same vein. Urban has shown how such accounts made “Tantrism” the convenient image of everything that colonial rule claimed to reform: secret, sexual, irrational and, through blood sacrifice, violent (Urban 2003). Many Bengali reformers accepted the diagnosis. Some, as the case of the Mahānirvāṇa Tantra shows (Section 4.5), sought a purified Tantra compatible with monotheism and law; others, like the Brāhmo leaders, rejected Tantric worship altogether, and the word “tāntrik” acquired the pejorative sense that it still has in much of India.

    14.4.2 Woodroffe and His Collaborators¶

    The rehabilitation of Tantra in English is associated above all with Sir John Woodroffe (1865–1936), a judge of the Calcutta High Court who published, under the name Arthur Avalon, translations and studies that included The Serpent Power (1919), the work that introduced the six-cakra system of Pūrṇānanda to Western readers (Avalon 1913, 1919). Kathleen Taylor’s study has shown that “Arthur Avalon” was in large part a collaborative identity: much of the Sanskrit work was done by Woodroffe’s Bengali associates, above all Atal Bihari Ghose, whose role was obscured by the pseudonym (Taylor 2001). Woodroffe’s own contribution was to present Tantra as a coherent, philosophically serious Śāstra, continuous with the Veda and suited to the present age, and to explain its transgressive rites as symbolic or as reserved for a small qualified elite. This apologetic reading answered the missionary caricature, but it also gave the late Bengali Tantras, and the Mahānirvāṇa above all, a representative status that historical scholarship has since qualified (Chapter 4).

    14.4.3 The Goddess and Bengali Nationalism¶

    In the decades around 1900 the Goddess of the Bengali Śāktas became a symbol of the nation. Bankimcandra Chattopadhyay’s novel Ānandamaṭh (1882), with its hymn Vande Mātaram, presented the motherland as a goddess to be served by ascetic warriors, and the pamphlet Bhavānī Mandir (1905), associated with Aurobindo Ghose, proposed a temple to the Goddess as the power (śakti) of India, served by a dedicated order. Some members of the revolutionary societies of Bengal took vows before images of Kālī. Colonial officials, for their part, read the revolutionary violence of these years through the old image of Tantric blood sacrifice. Urban has traced how the Goddess and the language of sacrifice served both as a nationalist idiom and as a colonial explanation of “terrorism” (Urban 2003). The episode shows that the meanings attached to Tantra were never only religious.

    14.4.4 Pierre Bernard and the American “Tantriks”¶

    In the United States the word “Tantra” was first popularised by Pierre Arnold Bernard (born Perry Baker, 1875 or 1876–1955), who founded a “Tantrik Order” in the first decade of the twentieth century, attracted newspaper notoriety as “Oom the Omnipotent”, and later ran a country club at Nyack, New York, where wealthy clients practised haṭha yoga. Bernard had some genuine knowledge of yoga, and his nephew Theos Bernard became a serious student of Tibetan Buddhism; but the “Tantra” he offered combined physical culture, secret initiation and sexual frankness in a form that had little to do with the Indian traditions (Love 2010; Urban 2003). The pattern of a Western teacher presenting himself as the holder of an exotic secret, and of a press presenting Tantra as scandal, was set for the rest of the century.

    14.4.5 Neo-Tantra¶

    From the 1960s Tantra became, in Western popular culture, a name for sacred sexuality. Mircea Eliade’s influential studies of yoga had already presented it as an archaic Indian spirituality of the body, and the counter-culture, popular writers and teachers such as Rajneesh (Osho) promoted it as sexual liberation and self-realisation. Urban has shown that this “neo-Tantra” drew less on Indian sources than on a Western tradition of sexual magic and erotic mysticism, which he traces from Paschal Beverly Randolph in nineteenth-century America through Aleister Crowley and Julius Evola, and which was later merged with Indian vocabulary by Rajneesh and by a large self-help literature (Urban 2006). Its themes — liberation through pleasure, the body as sacred, the rejection of repression — were those of the counter-culture, not of the medieval texts, whose sexual rites served the production of ritual substances, the gaining of powers or the realisation of non-duality within a demanding discipline of initiation (Chapter 4). White’s Kiss of the Yoginī was written in part to correct this picture (White 2003). Tibetan teachers in exile, meanwhile, introduced Buddhist Tantric practice to Western students in more traditional forms. The global market for “Tantra” workshops nevertheless continues to shape what many people, in India as well as abroad, imagine the word to mean, and Western ideas in turn influence how Tantra is presented in India today.

    The secrecy of the teaching, the authority of the guru and the sexual elements of some traditions have also, in modern times, been used to justify abuse, in India and abroad, by teachers who claimed Tantric legitimacy. Traditional authorities and scholars alike have condemned such misuse, and the medieval texts themselves warn against teachers who exploit their pupils (Section 1.6).

    14.4.6 Rāmakṛṣṇa and the Kālī’s Child Controversy¶

    The tensions between academic and devotional readings came to a head in the debate over Jeffrey Kripal’s Kālī’s Child (1995), a study of Rāmakṛṣṇa that read the saint’s mystical experiences, and his ambivalence towards the Tantric rites in which a woman ascetic had trained him, in terms of homoerotic desire (Kripal 1995). The book was praised by some reviewers and strongly contested by others, especially within the Rāmakṛṣṇa Order. Swami Tyagananda and Pravrajika Vrajaprana later published a detailed critique arguing that the book’s argument depended on mistranslations and selective readings of the Bengali sources (Swami Tyagananda and Pravrajika Vrajaprana 2010). The controversy raised questions that remain central to the study of Tantra: who may interpret a living tradition’s saints and secrets, what weight psychoanalytic readings can bear, and how the sexual elements of Tantric practice can be discussed in a way that both scholars and practitioners can recognise as accurate.

    14.5 Tantra and Modern Science¶

    Modern presentations of Tantra often claim that its doctrines anticipated modern science: that spanda is the vibration of quantum fields, that mantras work through acoustic “frequencies”, that the cakras are endocrine glands, or that yantras channel measurable “energy”. Such claims find no support in the Sanskrit sources, which speak in the categories of their own theology, and they misrepresent both the texts and modern science. The Kashmiri doctrine of consciousness can be compared with modern philosophy of mind as a philosophical position, and contemplative practices derived from Tantric and Buddhist traditions have been studied by psychologists and neuroscientists; but such research concerns meditation as a human practice, not the truth of the cosmological claims of the texts. The traditions deserve to be understood on their own terms, as religious and philosophical systems, without being made to borrow authority from physics or medicine.

    14.6 The Academic Study of Tantra¶

    The academic study of Tantra has passed through four overlapping phases. The colonial period offered condemnation and selective description. The first half of the twentieth century brought rehabilitation: Woodroffe and his collaborators, Gopinath Kaviraj in Varanasi, the editors of the Kashmir Series of Texts and Studies, K.C. Pandey’s study of Abhinavagupta (Pandey 1963) and Lilian Silburn’s translations and studies (Silburn 1988). From the 1960s philology and history took the lead: the editions of N.R. Bhatt and Hélène Brunner at Pondicherry, André Padoux’s studies of the Word, the surveys of Teun Goudriaan and Sanjukta Gupta, the dictionary of technical terms begun in Vienna (Brunner et al. 2000), Alexis Sanderson’s reconstruction of the history of Śaivism, and the editions of early scriptures by Dominic Goodall and his colleagues (Gupta et al. 1979; Goudriaan and Gupta 1981; Padoux 1990; Goodall et al. 2015). Alongside them, ethnographers and social historians have studied living temple priesthoods, goddess worship and Tantric practitioners (Fuller, McDaniel) and the social history of Tantra (Davidson, White, Urban, Samuel) (Fuller 2003; McDaniel 2004; Davidson 2002; Samuel 2008).

    Of these, Sanderson’s work has done most to set the present agenda. His thesis of a “Śaiva Age” (Chapter 12), his demonstration that the Buddhist Yoginītantras borrowed from Śaiva scriptures, and his tracing of Śaiva ritual forms in Vaiṣṇava, Buddhist, Jain and Smārta practice place the Tantric traditions at the centre, not the margin, of medieval Indian religion (Sanderson 1988, 2009). This volume follows his framework in many places, and the reader should bear in mind that much of it rests on one scholar’s reading of a vast and still largely unpublished body of sources, which younger scholars are now testing text by text.

    14.7 What Is “Tantrism”?¶

    The reception history has also made scholars cautious about the category itself. The Sanskrit sources speak of tantras as texts and distinguish what is tāntrika from what is vaidika, but “Tantrism” as the name of a single religious phenomenon is a nineteenth-century Western coinage. André Padoux argued that it is best treated as a scholarly construct, useful for grouping traditions that share features such as initiation, mantra, ritual diagrams, the deification of the body and the pursuit of both liberation and power, but not to be mistaken for an indigenous self-description (Padoux 2002). Douglas Brooks proposed a list of characteristics none of which is necessary on its own (Brooks 1990), and Urban has emphasised how the category was formed in exchanges between Indian and Western authors (Urban 2003). The approach of Sanderson and his colleagues, followed in this volume, sidesteps the problem by starting from the scriptures: a tradition is “Tantric” when it takes the Tantras or Āgamas as its authority (Section 1.1).

    14.8 The Living Traditions Today¶

    The traditions described in this volume are not only objects of study. The great Āgamic temples of South India draw very large numbers of pilgrims and remain centres of ritual, music and community life, and traditional schools and state-run Āgama schools train their priests; the opening of that training to non-Brahmins, and debates over the participation of women, are changing the priesthood. Tibetan Buddhism, in exile communities in India and worldwide, maintains Indian Buddhist Tantric lineages, and the Kālacakra initiation has been given by the Dalai Lama to very large audiences. Goddess worship, from Kāmākhyā to the village shrines of the Tamil country, continues in forms that the Sanskrit texts only partly describe. Thousands of Āgamic and Tantric manuscripts remain unedited, and digitisation projects in India, Nepal and Europe are making them more accessible; and the collaboration of traditional scholars with academic researchers, as at Pondicherry, has brought priests and philologists to the same texts.

    14.9 Conclusion¶

    What emerges from this volume is not a single “Tantra” but a family of traditions that, from about the fifth century CE, transformed Indian religion. They created the temple liturgies that still govern much Hindu worship and much of the ritual of Buddhists and Jains; they offered initiation, at least in principle, beyond the limits of the Vedic order; they produced philosophies of consciousness, language and aesthetic experience of lasting importance; and, in some lineages, they pursued liberation and power through the deliberate transgression of social and ritual norms.

    These elements belong together. The philosopher Abhinavagupta was also the author of a ritual handbook that included the secret Kaula rites; the temple priest who performs the daily worship of Śiva follows manuals that also prescribe rites for kings; and the traditions that opened initiation to women and to the low-born also reproduced the hierarchies of their world. The living temples of South India, the Tibetan monastic traditions and the many lineages of goddess worship show that this inheritance remains a living and contested part of the present.
