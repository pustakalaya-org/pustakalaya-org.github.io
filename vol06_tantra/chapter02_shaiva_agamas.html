    Chapter 2: The Śaiva Āgamas¶

    The Śaiva Āgamas or Tantras are the scriptures of the initiatory Śaiva religion that, in Alexis Sanderson’s phrase, dominated the religious landscape of South and Southeast Asia in the early medieval “Śaiva Age” (Sanderson 2009). They form the scriptural foundation of the Śaiva Siddhānta, the dualist Śaivism that survives today in the temples of Tamil Nadu, and of the Bhairava, Trika and Kaula traditions that culminated in the non-dual Śaivism of Kashmir. Revealed, according to tradition, from the five faces (pañcamukha) of Śiva, these texts govern initiation, daily worship, temple building and consecration, and much of the living temple religion of South India.

    2.1 Origin and Authority of the Śaiva Āgamas¶

    2.1.1 The Five Faces of Śiva¶

    The Śaiva Āgamas claim to issue from the five faces of Śiva, each associated with a direction and with one of the five “Brahma” mantras of the Taittirīya Āraṇyaka. The South Indian tradition distributes the twenty-eight principal Āgamas among the five faces and correlates the faces with Śiva’s five cosmic acts (Table 2.1); both correlations are traditional.

    Table 2.1. The Five Faces of Śiva and the Āgamas Traditionally Assigned to Them
    Face Direction Cosmic act Associated Āgamas
    Sadyojāta West Creation Kāmika, Yogaja, Cintya, Kāraṇa, Ajita
    Vāmadeva North Preservation Dīpta, Sūkṣma, Sahasra, Aṃśumat, Suprabheda
    Aghora South Dissolution Vijaya, Niśvāsa, Svāyambhuva, Āgneya, Vīra
    Tatpuruṣa East Concealment Raurava, Makuṭa, Vimala, Candrajñāna, Mukhabimba
    Īśāna Zenith Grace Prodgīta, Lalita, Siddha, Santāna, Sarvokta, Pārameśvara, Kiraṇa, Vātula

    The five mantras themselves, recited daily in Śaiva worship, are:

    The Pañcabrahma Mantras (Taittirīya Āraṇyaka 10.43–47)

    Sadyojāta: sadyojātaṃ prapadyāmi sadyojātāya vai namo namaḥ | bhave bhave nātibhave bhavasva mām | bhavodbhavāya namaḥ ||
    Vāmadeva: vāmadevāya namo jyeṣṭhāya namaḥ śreṣṭhāya namo rudrāya namaḥ kālāya namaḥ … sarvabhūtadamanāya namo manonmanāya namaḥ ||
    Aghora: aghorebhyo ’tha ghorebhyo ghoraghoratarebhyaḥ | sarvataḥ śarva sarvebhyo namas te astu rudrarūpebhyaḥ ||
    Tatpuruṣa: tatpuruṣāya vidmahe mahādevāya dhīmahi | tan no rudraḥ pracodayāt ||
    Īśāna: īśānaḥ sarvavidyānām īśvaraḥ sarvabhūtānāṃ brahmādhipatir brahmaṇo ’dhipatir brahmā śivo me astu sadāśivom ||

    (Sadyojāta) I take refuge in Sadyojāta; homage to Sadyojāta. Do not consign me to birth after birth; guide me beyond birth. Homage to the source of being. (Aghora) To those forms of Rudra that are not terrible, to those that are terrible, to those more terrible than the terrible, to all, O Śarva, in all places: homage to your Rudra-forms. (Tatpuruṣa) May we know that Supreme Person; may we meditate on the Great God; may Rudra inspire us. (Īśāna) May the lord of all knowledge, the ruler of all beings, the overlord of Brahman, Brahmā, be gracious to me: may he be Sadāśiva. (Vāmadeva: a litany of homage to the god’s names and powers.)

    2.1.2 The Āgamas and the Vedas¶

    The Siddhānta theologians did not reject the Veda. They regarded it as a valid but lower revelation, also spoken by Śiva, suited to the uninitiated; the Āgamas were the higher, specific teaching for initiates. Temple priests (ādiśaivas) recite Vedic mantras alongside Āgamic ones, and Tamil Siddhāntins later spoke of the Veda as the “general” and the Āgama as the “special” scripture. Orthodox Brahmins, for their part, long regarded Śaiva initiates with suspicion (Chapter 1).

    2.1.3 Atimārga and Mantramārga¶

    Sanderson’s influential classification divides early Śaivism into two great branches (Sanderson 1988):

    The Branches of Early Śaivism

    (1) The Atimārga (“outer” or “transcendent path”): orders of ascetics seeking liberation alone — the Pāśupatas, the Lākulas (known in the inscriptions of Karnataka as Kālāmukhas) and the Kāpālikas with their “observance of the skull”.
    (2) The Mantramārga (“path of mantras”): the Tantric Śaivism open to householders as well as ascetics, seeking both liberation and powers through initiation and mantra. It comprises the Śaiva Siddhānta and the more esoteric Bhairava Tantras (the Mantrapīṭha, centred on Svacchandabhairava, and the Vidyāpīṭha, centred on goddesses and including the Trika), from which the Kaula and Kālī-centred Krama traditions developed.
    Each system tended to regard those below it as valid but limited.

    2.1.4 The Pāśupata Tradition¶

    The Pāśupatas are the earliest Śaiva order for which we have substantial evidence. Their founder, Lakulīśa, probably lived in the second century CE: the Mathurā pillar inscription of 380 CE names a teacher in the line of his disciple Kuśika. The Pāśupatasūtra, with the commentary (Pañcārthabhāṣya) of Kauṇḍinya, teaches:

    1. Five categories (pañcārtha): effect (kārya, the world and souls), cause (kāraṇa, the Lord), union (yoga), observance (vidhi) and the end of suffering (duḥkhānta);

    2. Deliberate dishonour: in an advanced stage the ascetic courts public contempt by feigning madness, making lewd gestures or snoring in public, so that those who abuse him take on his demerit and he receives their merit;

    3. Bathing in ash: smearing the body with ash and lying in ash, a practice that became a mark of Śaivism generally.

    2.1.5 Lakulīśa in Art and Memory¶

    Lakulīśa, the founder of the Pāśupatas, is shown in sculpture as a naked, seated ascetic holding a club (lakuṭa, from which his name derives) and a citron, often with an erect phallus signifying his continence and mastery. Images of him are common in temples of Gujarat, Rajasthan, Madhya Pradesh, Odisha and Karnataka from about the sixth to the twelfth century. Tradition places his birth or manifestation at Kāyārohana (modern Karvan, near Vadodara in Gujarat), which remained a Pāśupata centre. Inscriptions record Lakula and Pāśupata teachers across western, central and southern India, showing how widely these ascetic orders spread before the Tantric Mantramārga became dominant (Lorenzen 1972).

    2.1.6 The Atimārga in the Sources¶

    Because the Atimārga orders left few texts of their own, they were long known chiefly from their opponents. Research on early Nepalese manuscripts, above all the Niśvāsamukha, has made it possible to see them as the Tantric scriptures saw them. These sources describe the Atimārga in three tiers, which Sanderson has reconstructed as stages of a single development (Sanderson 2009):

    1. The Pāñcārthika Pāśupatas, who followed the Pāśupatasūtra and its five categories, and whose practice culminated in the courting of dishonour described above.

    2. The Lākulas, who adopted the more extreme “great observance” (mahāvrata): the ascetic carried a skull as a begging bowl and a staff topped by a skull (khaṭvāṅga), wore ornaments of human bone and smeared himself with the ash of cremation grounds. The Lākulas also elaborated a cosmology of many worlds through which the liberated soul ascends, an intermediate stage between the Pāśupata system and that of the Tantras.

    3. The Kāpālikas (“skull-bearers”), who called their system the “Soma doctrine” (Somasiddhānta), lived in cremation grounds and worshipped Bhairava with his consort, with offerings of meat and alcohol and rites involving women. Their practice leads directly to the Bhairava Tantras of the Mantramārga (Section 2.5), and they are often regarded as precursors of the later Aghorīs.

    Lākulas and Kālāmukhas. The two names refer to one order, not two. In the inscriptions of Karnataka the Lākulas of the second tier appear as “Kālāmukhas” (“black-faced”), the spelling used by Lorenzen and by most historians of the region; Sanderson, who identifies the inscriptional Kālāmukhas with the Lākulas of the scriptures, writes the name “Kālamukha” (Lorenzen 1972; Sanderson 2009). This volume uses the conventional form Kālāmukha for the order as it appears in the inscriptions.

    The penance of the skull. The skull observance had a Brahmanical model. The Dharmaśāstras prescribe that a man who has killed a Brahmin should live for twelve years in a hut in the forest, begging, and carrying the skull of a dead man as his emblem (e.g. Manusmṛti 11.73). Śaiva myth told that Bhairava, having cut off one of Brahmā’s heads, had to wander with the skull stuck to his hand until he reached Vārāṇasī, where it fell away. The Kāpālika ascetic imitated the god’s penance, but transformed its meaning: what for the Dharmaśāstra was the expiation of the worst of sins became, for the Śaiva, an identification with Śiva in his most terrible form (Lorenzen 1972). Stella Kramrisch’s study of Śiva’s mythology traces the many tellings of this myth of the god’s own transgression and penance (Kramrisch 1981).

    Hostile portraits. Literary sources from the seventh century onwards present the skull-bearers as figures of fear or ridicule:

    • in Mahendravarman I’s farce Mattavilāsa (7th century) a drunken Kāpālika, Satyasoma, and his female companion lose their skull bowl and accuse a Buddhist monk of stealing it;

    • in the play Mālatīmādhava by Bhavabhūti (early 8th century), the Kāpālika Aghoraghaṇṭa and his female pupil Kapālakuṇḍalā abduct the heroine to sacrifice her to the goddess Karālā at a shrine beside the cremation ground of the city, while another adept of the play has won her powers on the mountain Śrīparvata (Śrīśailam);

    • in Kṛṣṇamiśra’s allegorical drama Prabodhacandrodaya (late 11th century) a Kāpālika expounds the “Soma doctrine” and wins over a Jain and a Buddhist with wine and the embrace of his female partner.

    The Vaiṣṇava theologians Yāmuna and Rāmānuja, refuting the Śaivas, report that the Kāpālikas wore six insignia — necklace, neck ornament, earrings, crest jewel, ash and sacred thread — and that the Kālāmukhas ate from skulls, smeared themselves with the ash of corpses and worshipped the god in a pot of wine. These reports are polemical, but their details match the scriptures’ own descriptions of the skull observance closely enough to show that they were not invented (Lorenzen 1972).

    The inscriptional Kālāmukhas. The inscriptions of Karnataka present an entirely different picture. There the Kālāmukhas, whose ascetics’ names often end in -rāśi or -śakti, appear as heads of richly endowed temple-monasteries, such as the Koḍiya monastery at Baḷḷigāve (Belgami) in the eleventh and twelfth centuries. The inscriptions praise them as masters of grammar, logic, Mīmāṃsā and poetry, and as providers of food, medicine and teaching; they record grants from kings and local lords and describe the ascetics as followers of the Lākula doctrine. Lorenzen argued that the order lost its monasteries and much of its following to the Vīraśaivas in the thirteenth century (Lorenzen 1972). The contrast between the skull-bearing ascetic of the scriptures and the scholarly abbot of the inscriptions shows how far an order founded on renunciation could be drawn into the institutional religion of the early medieval kingdoms (Chapter 12).

    2.2 The Four Pādas¶

    The ideal Siddhānta Āgama consists of four sections (pādas); in practice the ritual section is usually by far the longest and some texts lack one or more of the others.

    2.2.1 The Vidyāpāda or Jñānapāda¶

    The doctrinal section sets out the three categories (padārthas) of the Siddhānta:

    • Pati, the Lord Śiva: omniscient, omnipotent, eternally free, who performs five acts (creation, maintenance, dissolution, concealment and grace) for the sake of souls;

    • Paśu, the soul (“bound beast”): innumerable, eternal and distinct from Śiva. Souls are by nature conscious agents, but their powers of knowledge and action are obscured;

    • Pāśa, the bonds: the innate impurity (āṇavamala), karma, māyā (the material cause of bodies and worlds), and Śiva’s own concealing power (tirodhānaśakti).

    Because impurity is understood as a real substance, the Siddhānta holds that knowledge alone cannot remove it: only a ritual act — initiation — can. The Siddhāntins describe these bonds as three impurities (malas): the beginningless innate impurity (āṇavamala), which obscures the soul’s powers of knowledge and action and makes it “atomic” (aṇu) or limited; the bond of māyā (māyīyamala), the bodies, faculties and worlds that partly reveal the soul’s powers even as they bind it; and karma (kārmamala), the residue of past action that determines birth and experience.

    The cosmos in which souls are bound is described as a hierarchy of thirty-six principles (tattvas), from Śiva down to earth, which extends the twenty-five tattvas of Sāṁkhya by eleven higher ones. The scheme is common to the Siddhānta and the non-dual Kashmiri schools, and it is set out once, for the whole volume, in Table 2.2. The two schools interpret it differently: for the Siddhānta the lower tattvas are the real evolutes of māyā, distinct from souls and from the Lord; for the non-dualists the whole series describes the stages by which a single consciousness contracts itself (Section 6.4). They also group the intermediate levels differently, as the table notes.

    Table 2.2. The Thirty-Six Tattvas of the Śaiva Traditions
    Tattvas Description
    Pure (śuddha), 1–5
    Śiva Pure consciousness
    Śakti The power of consciousness
    Sadāśiva Lord of the pure creation; in Kashmiri non-dualism, “I am this” with stress on “I”
    Īśvara In Kashmiri non-dualism, “I am this” with stress on “this”
    Śuddhavidyā Pure knowledge
    Mixed (śuddhāśuddha), 6–12
    Māyā Principle of limitation; material cause of the impure worlds
    Five kañcukas Kalā, vidyā, rāga, kāla, niyati: limited agency, knowledge, attachment, time and causal order
    Puruṣa The soul as limited experiencer
    Impure (aśuddha), 13–36
    Prakṛti Primal matter
    Buddhi, ahaṅkāra, manas The inner faculties
    Five jñānendriyas Cognitive senses
    Five karmendriyas Faculties of action
    Five tanmātras Subtle elements
    Five mahābhūtas Gross elements

    The grouping 5 + 7 + 24 is that of the Siddhānta, which counts Puruṣa with Māyā and its coverings. Kashmiri presentations commonly treat Māyā and the five coverings (6–11) as the intermediate level and place Puruṣa at the head of the impure level (12–36).

    2.2.2 The Yogapāda¶

    The yoga of the Śaiva Āgamas is not Patañjali’s eight-limbed yoga but usually a “six-limbed” yoga (ṣaḍaṅgayoga) consisting of breath control (prāṇāyāma), withdrawal of the senses (pratyāhāra), meditation (dhyāna), fixation (dhāraṇā), reasoning or discernment (tarka) and absorption (samādhi). Meditations on the tattvas, on mantras and on luminous forms of Śiva, and techniques for gaining yogic powers, are typical (Mallinson and Singleton 2017).

    The fullest early account of this yoga is found in the Mālinīvijayottara, the scripture on which Abhinavagupta built his Trika (Chapter 6), whose yogic chapters Somadeva Vasudeva has edited and translated (Vasudeva 2004). Its yoga is organised around the hierarchy of the cosmos itself. The yogin fixes his mind successively on the levels of reality, beginning with the element earth and rising through the higher tattvas, and each successful fixation is said to give him mastery over that level and over the beings and powers that belong to it. The fixations (dhāraṇās) on the five elements are central: for each element the text prescribes its own visualisation and describes the signs by which progress is recognised and the powers that follow. The yogin’s ascent through the tattvas thus mirrors the ritual ascent through the same levels that the guru performs for the candidate in initiation (Section 2.12); yoga and ritual are two ways of travelling the one map of the cosmos.

    Two further features distinguish this yoga from Patañjali’s. The first is the place of “reasoning” (tarka), which in the Mālinīvijayottara and in Abhinavagupta’s reading of it is not logical argument but a discriminating insight that separates what is to be rejected from what is to be accepted on the path; Abhinavagupta ranked it highest among the limbs. The second is that the goal is not the isolation of the soul from matter but a progressive identification with ever higher levels of Śiva’s power, culminating in a state of “absorption” (samāveśa) into Śiva. The scripture’s threefold classification of such absorption became the basis of Abhinavagupta’s doctrine of the “means” (upāyas) to liberation (Section 6.8) (Vasudeva 2004; Sanderson 1988). The yoga of the Śaiva scriptures, and the subtle-body practices that grew out of it (Section 2.13), are also the background of the later haṭha yoga treated in Volume 5, Yoga Taraṅgiṇī.

    2.2.3 The Kriyāpāda¶

    The ritual section covers:

    • initiation (dīkṣā) and consecration (abhiṣeka);

    • daily obligatory worship (nityapūjā) and fire offering (homa);

    • temple building (vāstu), from testing the site to the finial;

    • the making and installation of images (pratiṣṭhā);

    • occasional and optional rites, festivals (utsava) and expiations (prāyaścitta);

    • funerary and ancestral rites for initiates.

    2.2.4 The Caryāpāda¶

    The section on conduct prescribes the observances of initiates: purity, conduct towards the guru, the rules (samayas) of each grade of initiate, expiations for breaking them, and the outward marks of the Śaiva. Sacred ash (vibhūti, bhasma) is applied in three horizontal lines (tripuṇḍra) on the forehead and other parts of the body. It recalls Śiva’s ash-smeared ascetic form and the Pāśupata bath in ash; theologically it is read as what remains when all else is burnt away, and it marks both purification and membership of the Śaiva community. Rudrākṣa (“eye of Rudra”) beads, the seeds of Elaeocarpus species of the Himalayan foothills and Southeast Asia, are worn and used for counting mantras. Later Purāṇic texts and popular manuals associate beads with different numbers of natural divisions (mukhas) with different deities and benefits; these lists vary, and the benefits claimed for them are matters of devotion.

    2.3 The Śaiva Siddhānta¶

    The Siddhānta arose in North India; its great early exegetes, such as Sadyojyotis (c. 675–725) (Sanderson 2006), Bhaṭṭa Nārāyaṇakaṇṭha and his son Rāmakaṇṭha (10th century), worked in Kashmir. By the eleventh and twelfth centuries its centre of gravity had moved south: the ritual manual of Somaśambhu (1095 CE) and the works of Aghoraśiva of Chidambaram (12th century) became standard in Tamil temples (Brunner-Lachaux 1963–1998; Davis 1991). From the thirteenth century a distinct Tamil school developed, combining the Sanskrit Āgamas with the Tamil devotional hymns.

    2.3.1 The Canonical Literature¶

    The main sources of the Śaiva Siddhānta are summarised in Table 2.3.

    Table 2.3. Sources of the Śaiva Siddhānta
    Stream Language Key texts
    Āgamas Sanskrit The twenty-eight Āgamas and their upaāgamas (in practice especially the Kāmika, Kāraṇa, Ajita, Suprabheda and the Mṛgendra)
    Exegesis and manuals Sanskrit Sadyojyotis’s Nareśvaraparīkṣā; Bhoja’s Tattvaprakāśa; Rāmakaṇṭha’s commentaries; the paddhatis of Somaśambhu and Aghoraśiva
    Tēvāram Tamil Hymns of Campantar, Appar and Cuntarar (7th–9th century)
    Tiruvācakam Tamil Hymns of Māṇikkavācakar (c. 9th century)

    The Tamil hymns were later collected in the twelve-part canon of the Tirumuṟai.

    2.3.2 Meykaṇṭār and the Tamil School¶

    Meykaṇṭār’s Civañāṉapōtam (13th century) consists of twelve short Tamil sūtras with brief explanatory notes (vārttikas). The twelve Sanskrit verses known as the Śivajñānabodha and said to come from the Rauravāgama closely parallel them; whether they are the source or a later back-translation is debated. Meykaṇṭār and his successors, notably Aruḷnanti and Umāpati Śivācārya (14th century), produced the fourteen Meykaṇṭa Śāstras. The Tamil school retains the eternal distinction of souls from Śiva but describes liberation as a state of “non-duality” (advaita) in the sense of inseparability, in which the soul is united with Śiva as a sound is with a letter or a body with its soul.

    2.3.3 The Structure of the Civañāṉapōtam¶

    The twelve sūtras of Meykaṇṭār’s work are traditionally grouped in four sets of three. The first set offers proofs: that the world, which appears as “he”, “she” and “it” and undergoes origination and dissolution, must have a Lord as its cause; that the Lord is one with souls in the sense of being inseparable from them, yet distinct; and that the soul exists as something other than body, senses and mind. The second set defines the nature of soul, bonds and Lord. The third describes the means: the soul’s realisation of its own nature through the Lord’s grace, which comes in the form of the guru. The fourth describes the fruit: the liberated soul’s union with Śiva, the removal of the bonds, and the life of the liberated in the company of Śiva’s devotees, worshipping the guru’s form and the temple image as Śiva himself.

    The Tamil school’s major later works include Aruḷnanti’s Civañāṉacittiyār (13th century), a long verse exposition that also refutes rival schools, and Umāpati Śivācārya’s works of the fourteenth century, which completed the canon of fourteen treatises. The ātīṉams, monasteries headed by non-Brahmin Śaiva ascetics such as those at Tiruvāvaṭutuṟai and Dharmapuram, became the main institutional home of this Tamil theology and of the study of the hymns.

    2.3.4 The Sixty-Three Nāyaṉmārs¶

    The sixty-three Śaiva saints of the Tamil country include kings and peasants, women and men, Brahmins and devotees of low and “untouchable” birth. Their lives are told in Cēkkiḻār’s Periyapurāṇam (12th century), and their images, among them those of the hymnists Appar, Campantar and Cuntarar, the woman poet Kāraikkāl Ammaiyār and the king Cēramāṉ Perumāḷ, stand in many Tamil temples. The hagiographies celebrate extreme devotion that overrides social convention — the hunter Kaṇṇappar offering his own eyes, the Pulaiyar devotee Nandanār passing through fire to enter Chidambaram — even as the stories also reflect the caste order they describe (Peterson 1989).

    2.4 The Niśvāsatattvasaṃhitā¶

    The Niśvāsatattvasaṃhitā, preserved in a single Nepalese palm-leaf manuscript of about the ninth century, is the oldest surviving Śaiva Tantra. It is not a unified work but a collection of five books composed over a period of perhaps two centuries: the Mūlasūtra, the oldest, whose core its editors date to about 450–550 CE; the Uttarasūtra; the Nayasūtra, which deals with doctrine and yoga; the Guhyasūtra, the longest; and the Niśvāsamukha, an introduction that places the Mantramārga in relation to the lay Śaivism of the Purāṇas, the Vedic religion and the ascetic Atimārga (Goodall et al. 2015).

    The text shows Tantric Śaivism at an early stage. It already teaches initiation that liberates, mantras extracted in code, the worship of Śiva with his five faces, the tattvas and the yoga of the Mantramārga, but much of its ritual is simpler than that of the later Siddhānta, and it draws heavily on Vedic and Purāṇic material. It also includes extensive sections on rites for worldly ends, showing that the pursuit of powers was part of Tantric Śaivism from the beginning. Its critical edition, whose first volume appeared in 2015, has transformed our understanding of the origins of the Āgamas.

    2.5 The Bhairava Tantras¶

    Beside the Siddhānta, the Mantramārga comprised a second and more esoteric body of scripture, the Tantras of Bhairava, the fierce form of Śiva. Their followers regarded them as a higher revelation than the Siddhānta; Siddhānta authors, for their part, treated them as meant chiefly for initiates who sought supernatural powers (Sanderson 1988, 2009). They fall into two main groups.

    The Mantrapīṭha (“seat of the mantras”) centres on Svacchandabhairava, a five-faced form of Bhairava, and his consort Aghoreśvarī. Its principal scripture, the Svacchandatantra, with the commentary (Uddyota) of Kṣemarāja, became the basis of the ordinary Śaiva ritual of Kashmir. Closely related is the Netratantra, devoted to Amṛteśvara, the “Lord of the nectar of immortality”, also called Mṛtyujit, “conqueror of death”; it provides rites of protection and healing, including rites for the protection of kings, and was likewise commented on by Kṣemarāja.

    The Vidyāpīṭha (“seat of the female mantras”) is centred on goddesses. Its scriptures are divided into the Yāmala (“union”) Tantras, of which the Brahmayāmala or Picumata is the oldest surviving, and the Śakti Tantras, which include the scriptures of the Trika (the Siddhayogeśvarīmata and the Mālinīvijayottara) and the vast, Kālī-centred Jayadrathayāmala in four books. In the Brahmayāmala the deity is Kapālīśabhairava with the goddess Caṇḍā Kapālinī, surrounded by goddesses and their attendant yoginīs. The text prescribes the “great observance” (mahāvrata) of the skull-bearing ascetic, worship in cremation grounds, offerings of blood, meat and alcohol, ritual with female partners and the propitiation of yoginīs, with the aim of gaining powers such as flight and, ultimately, liberation (Hatley 2018).

    These scriptures are the common source of the Kaula traditions (Chapter 4), of the Trika and Krama of Kashmir (Chapter 6) and, as Sanderson has shown, of much of the Buddhist Yoginītantras (Chapter 10). Their cult of the yoginīs is also reflected in the open-air yoginī temples of central and eastern India (Section 4.10).

    2.6 The Other Streams: Gāruḍa, Bhūta and Vāma Tantras¶

    The Siddhānta and the Bhairava Tantras were not the whole of the Mantramārga. Several scriptures and their exegetes describe the Śaiva revelation as flowing in five “streams” (srotas), each issuing from one of the five faces of Sadāśiva (Table 2.4). In this scheme the Siddhānta comes from the upper face, Īśāna, and the Bhairava Tantras from the southern face, Aghora, which is why they are also called the “southern” (dakṣiṇa) Tantras; the eastern, northern and western faces reveal three further classes of scripture, the Gāruḍa, the Vāma and the Bhūta Tantras (Sanderson 1988). The Brahmayāmala devotes a whole chapter (its thirty-ninth) to a myth of the origin of the streams, which shows that the scheme was already current among the authors of the Vidyāpīṭha (Hatley 2018). It should be distinguished from the later South Indian practice, described above (Table 2.1), of distributing the twenty-eight Siddhānta Āgamas themselves among the five faces: in the older scheme each face reveals a different kind of scripture, and only one of them is the Siddhānta.

    Table 2.4. The Five Streams (Srotas) of the Mantramārga
    Stream Face and direction Concern What survives
    Siddhānta Īśāna, upper Liberation through initiation; the regular worship of Śiva Many Āgamas, ritual manuals and commentaries (this chapter)
    Gāruḍa Tatpuruṣa, east Snakebite and poison Scriptures lost; quotations, the digest Kriyākālaguṇottara, later compendia
    Vāma Vāmadeva, north Tumburu and his four sisters; protection and magic The Vīṇāśikhatantra
    Bhūta Sadyojāta, west Possessing spirits and their exorcism Scriptures lost; digests and quotations
    Bhairava (Dakṣiṇa) Aghora, south Fierce deities; powers and liberation Mantrapīṭha and Vidyāpīṭha Tantras (Section 2.5)

    The three middle streams have fared badly. Their scriptures were concerned less with liberation than with protection, healing and power, they were not adopted by the monastic and temple institutions that copied and commented on the Siddhānta, and almost all of them are lost. Yet they were once widely known: early Śaiva scriptures, the Buddhist Mañjuśrīmūlakalpa and later Purāṇas cite or absorb their teachings, and some of their mantras are still used.

    2.6.1 The Gāruḍa Tantras and Tantric Medicine¶

    Michael Slouber’s study of the Gāruḍa Tantras, the first book-length treatment of this literature, places them within a wider body of “Tantric medicine” with three branches: the exorcism of possessing beings, taught in the Bhūta Tantras; the treatment of illnesses of pregnancy and childhood, taught in the Bāla Tantras; and the treatment of snakebite and other poisons, the domain of the Gāruḍa Tantras (Slouber 2017). The healer in this system is not a physician in the Āyurvedic sense but an initiate (mantrin, gāruḍika) who, by identifying himself in meditation with a mantra-deity, acquires that deity’s power over the poison. The Āyurvedic branches of toxicology (agadatantra) and of the “science of spirits” (bhūtavidyā) are described in Volume 13, Āyurveda; the Tantric literature treated here ran parallel to them and borrowed from them.

    The deity of the Gāruḍa Tantras is Garuḍa, the eagle who is the enemy of snakes. Contrary to what one might expect, he is not here the mount of Viṣṇu but an independent deity, conceived as a form of Śiva in the same way as Bhairava; the practitioner visualises Garuḍa as filling the cosmos and transforms himself into him. In the goddess-centred Tvaritāmūlasūtra Garuḍa even fights and defeats Viṣṇu. The central mantra is a five-syllable formula of Garuḍa, which Slouber traces in at least thirty premodern texts and finds still in use from Kerala to Tibet; another cycle centres on Śiva as Nīlakaṇṭha, the “blue-throated” god who swallowed the poison churned from the ocean, with a three-syllable mantra. A further group of rites is addressed to “snakebite goddesses” such as Tvaritā and the Buddhist Kurukullā, figures who move freely between Śaiva, Śākta, Buddhist and Jain settings (Slouber 2017).

    The scriptures themselves are lost, but much of their content survives in later works. The most important is the Kriyākālaguṇottara, a digest of the Bhūta and Gāruḍa Tantras composed by about the eleventh century, of which Slouber edited and translated ten chapters from Nepalese manuscripts; it incorporates long passages from otherwise lost scriptures. Gāruḍa material also passed into the Agni, Nārada and Garuḍa Purāṇas, into Jain and Buddhist literature — the Mañjuśrīmūlakalpa openly announces that it incorporates Gāruḍa teachings and makes Garuḍa a bodhisattva — and into the late medieval compendia of Kerala, where the treatment of snakebite (viṣavaidyam) remained a recognised specialism. Slouber argues that the Gāruḍa Tantras were not a marginal magical appendix to Śaivism: they taught a complete Tantric system with initiation and a soteriological goal, and the modern habit of contrasting a “rational” Āyurveda with “magical” healing misrepresents both (Slouber 2017).

    Health and Safety Note

    Snakebite is a medical emergency. The mantras and rites described in this section are reported as history; neither they nor traditional antidotes are a substitute for immediate transport to a hospital and treatment with antivenom by trained staff.

    2.6.2 The Bhūta Tantras and Possession¶

    The Bhūta Tantras dealt with the many classes of beings — bhūtas, grahas (“seizers”), piśācas and others — held to cause illness and madness by possessing human beings, and with the rites by which a mantra-master could identify, propitiate or expel them. Like the Gāruḍa Tantras they are known chiefly through the Kriyākālaguṇottara and through quotation. Material of the same kind entered the Bhairava Tantras: the Netratantra, whose protective rites for kings were mentioned above, contains a long treatment of possessing beings and their expulsion by the mantra of Amṛteśvara. Frederick Smith’s survey of possession in Sanskrit literature shows how this Tantric material connects with the Āyurvedic doctrine of the seizers that afflict children and the insane, with Purāṇic and narrative literature, and with the possession and exorcism that remain part of religious life at goddess shrines and healing temples across South Asia (Smith 2006).

    2.6.3 The Vāma Tantras: Tumburu and His Sisters¶

    The northern, “left” (vāma) stream centred on Tumburu, a four-faced form of Śiva, and his four sisters, the goddesses Jayā, Vijayā, Jayantī (or Ajitā) and Aparājitā. Its only surviving scripture is the Vīṇāśikhatantra, edited and translated by Teun Goudriaan, which prescribes initiation, the worship of the five deities of the system, meditations on the subtle body and a range of rites for subjugating, attracting and harming (Goudriaan 1985). In this cult goddesses already hold a prominent place, though subordinate to Tumburu; it is one of the earliest Śaiva systems in which they do. The Vīṇāśikha is one of the four Tantras that the Sdok Kak Thom inscription says were taught at the Khmer court in the ninth century (Section 12.7), and the four sisters were also known to Buddhist literature. The cult nevertheless seems to have faded early in India, and the stream survives mainly as a name in the classifications.

    Taken together, the lost streams show that early Tantric Śaivism was not only a religion of liberation and temple worship but also a broad repertory of services — healing, protection, exorcism — offered to kings and ordinary people alike. This helps to explain the success of the Śaiva traditions in the early medieval centuries (Chapter 12), and why the figure of the mantra-healer, often called a “tāntrik”, has outlived much of the scriptural tradition that produced it.

    2.7 The Mṛgendra Āgama¶

    The Mṛgendra, which presents itself as a supplement to the Kāmika, is among the most studied Siddhānta scriptures. It contains doctrinal, yogic, ritual and conduct sections, and its vidyāpāda sets out Siddhānta doctrine with unusual clarity. Its commentary (vṛtti) by the Kashmiri Bhaṭṭa Nārāyaṇakaṇṭha (10th century) was in turn commented on by Aghoraśiva, and the text was edited by N.R. Bhatt, with a French translation of its ritual and conduct sections by Hélène Brunner, at Pondicherry.

    2.8 Some Major Śaiva Āgamas¶

    2.8.1 The Kāmika¶

    The Kāmika stands first in the traditional list and is one of the most frequently cited authorities in South Indian temple practice. The text now known under this name, printed in Madras from 1975, is a large South Indian compilation dealing with temple construction, consecration, daily worship, festivals and expiations; it is considerably later than the Kāmika quoted by early Kashmiri authors and borrows from the twelfth-century manuals.

    2.8.2 The Kiraṇa¶

    The Kiraṇa is one of the early Siddhānta scriptures; a Nepalese manuscript of it is dated 924 CE. Its doctrinal section was commented on by Rāmakaṇṭha (10th century), who used it to defend the dualist Siddhānta against both non-Śaiva schools and the emerging non-dualist exegesis of the Trika. The commentary has been critically edited and translated by Dominic Goodall (Goodall 1998).

    2.8.3 The Raurava¶

    The Raurava takes its name from the sage Ruru, to whom it is said to have been taught. An early Rauravasūtrasaṅgraha survives; the much longer South Indian Rauravāgama edited by N.R. Bhatt deals principally with ritual, temple building and image installation (Bhatt 1961–1988).

    2.8.4 The Svāyambhuva¶

    An early Svāyambhuvasūtrasaṅgraha, with doctrinal, ritual, yogic and conduct sections, is quoted by the Kashmiri exegetes and survives in Nepalese manuscripts; its doctrinal section was edited and translated by Pierre-Sylvain Filliozat. As with other titles on the list, later South Indian works also circulate under the name.

    2.8.5 The Ajita¶

    The Ajita is a South Indian ritual compendium of the later type, edited in four volumes by N.R. Bhatt at the IFP. It deals at length with temple construction, consecration, worship and festivals.

    Scholarly Note: The Upaāgamas

    Each of the twenty-eight principal Āgamas is said to have subsidiary texts (upaāgamas), traditionally numbered at 207, most of them lost. Some have independent importance: the Pauṣkara, counted as an upaāgama of the Pārameśvara, has a significant doctrinal section, and the Mṛgendra presents itself as part of the Kāmika.

    2.9 The Daily Worship of the Temple¶

    A large Tamil Śaiva temple performs worship up to six times a day. The Tamil names of the services are (Table 2.5):

    Table 2.5. The Six Daily Services in Large Tamil Śaiva Temples
    No. Tamil name Time Character
    1 Tiruvaṉantal Dawn Awakening of the deity
    2 Kālacanti Early morning Morning worship
    3 Uccikālam Noon Midday worship
    4 Cāyarakṣai (Skt. sāyarakṣā) Dusk Evening worship
    5 Iraṇṭāṅkālam Night Second evening service
    6 Arttajāmam (Skt. ardhayāma) Late night Final service; the deity is put to rest

    Each service follows the sequence laid down in the manuals: the priest’s own purification and divinisation of his body, the worship of the doorkeepers and attendant deities, invocation of Śiva into the liṅga, offerings (upacāras) of water, bathing, clothing, ornaments, food, lamps and incense, recitation of mantras, and dismissal or “retention” of the deity (Davis 1991). The temple’s architecture is also interpreted as a body, with the sanctum as the heart or womb and the superstructure as the head; Chapter 11 discusses this homology.

    2.9.1 The Daily Worship of Śiva in the Siddhānta Manuals¶

    The Siddhānta manuals, above all that of Somaśambhu (1095), lay down a daily worship of Śiva (śivapūjā) that every initiate of the higher grades was obliged to perform, whether at home or, in the case of temple priests, on behalf of a temple. Its sequence, studied in detail by Hélène Brunner and Richard Davis, is the skeleton of the temple services described above (Brunner-Lachaux 1963–1998; Davis 1991):

    1. Bath (snāna): a bath with water and with ash, accompanied by mantras, followed by the twilight rites (sandhyā).

    2. Entry and the doorkeepers: the worshipper enters the place of worship after honouring the door and its guardians (dvārapālas) and expelling obstacles with the weapon mantra.

    3. The fivefold purification (pañcaśuddhi): purification of the place (sthāna), of the self (ātman), of the substances (dravya), of the mantras (mantra) and of the liṅga. Purification of the self includes the destruction and re-creation of the body (bhūtaśuddhi) and the placing of mantras on it, so that the worshipper becomes a “Śiva” fit to worship Śiva.

    4. Inner worship: Śiva is worshipped mentally in the heart, and in some manuals in the fire of the navel and at the crown.

    5. The throne: the seat of Śiva is worshipped as a series of cosmic supports — the divine power of support, the cosmic tortoise and serpent, the four legs of virtue, knowledge, detachment and sovereignty, and a lotus — installed over the liṅga.

    6. Invocation: Śiva, in the form of Sadāśiva with five faces and ten arms, is visualised and invoked from the heart into the liṅga with his root mantra; his five Brahma mantras and six “limb” mantras are installed on the form.

    7. Offerings: water for the feet and for sipping, the respectful offering (arghya), bathing, garments, unguents, flowers, incense, lamp and food, each with its mantra and gesture.

    8. The retinue: the attendant deities in the surrounding enclosures (āvaraṇas) — the “limb” mantras, the eight Vidyeśvaras, the guardians of the directions and their weapons — are worshipped in turn.

    9. Recitation and surrender: the root mantra is recited and the merit of the recitation is offered to Śiva; praise and prostration follow.

    10. Fire offering and conclusion: in the full form a fire offering follows; at the end the remains of the offerings are given to Caṇdeśa, the guardian of Śiva’s leftovers, and Śiva is dismissed or asked to remain in the liṅga, with a request for forgiveness for errors.

    The structure — purification and divinisation of the worshipper, installation of the deity, hospitality, and dismissal — is common to almost all Tantric worship, Śaiva, Vaiṣṇava, Śākta and Buddhist.

    2.10 The Vīraśaiva / Liṅgāyata Tradition¶

    The Liṅgāyata or Vīraśaiva movement of twelfth-century Karnataka, associated above all with Basava (Basaveśvara), a minister at the Kalacuri court of Kalyāṇa, was a radical current within Śaivism:

    • The personal liṅga: every Liṅgāyata wears a small iṣṭaliṅga on the body, making the body itself the place of worship.

    • Rejection of temple religion: the poets mocked temple building, pilgrimage, sacrifice and priestly ritual. In one of Basava’s best-known vacanas the poor devotee, unable to build a temple, declares his legs the pillars, his body the shrine and his head the golden cupola (Ramanujan 1973).

    • Social critique: the movement rejected caste hierarchy, ideas of ritual pollution and the subordination of women; tradition holds that a marriage between a Brahmin and an “untouchable” family within the community provoked the violence that ended the early movement.

    • The vacanas: Basava, Akka Mahādēvi, Allama Prabhu, Dēvara Dāsimayya and many others, including women and men of artisan and low-caste backgrounds, composed free-verse “sayings” in Kannada, one of the great bodies of Indian devotional literature.

    • Doctrine and practice: the later theology systematised the path as six stages (ṣaṭsthala) and defined Liṅgāyata identity by eight “coverings” (aṣṭāvaraṇa: guru, liṅga, the wandering teacher or jaṅgama, sacred water, consecrated food, ash, rudrākṣa and mantra) and five codes of conduct (pañcācāra). The ideal of kāyaka, dignified work as worship, is central.

    Whether Liṅgāyatism is a branch of Śaivism or a distinct religion has been debated in Karnataka since the nineteenth century and remains politically live.

    2.11 Liberation in the Siddhānta and in Kashmiri Non-dualism¶

    The two great Śaiva theologies agree that liberation is the work of Śiva’s grace, but they disagree about what it is and how it comes. For the Siddhānta, liberation is “equality with Śiva” (śivasāmya): the soul’s innate omniscience and omnipotence become fully manifest, but it remains a distinct soul, equal to Śiva and not identical with him. Since its bondage is a real impurity, the means is a real act — initiation, followed by lifelong worship — and knowledge alone is insufficient; the soul is finally released from its bonds at death. Siddhānta authors such as Sadyojyotis and Rāmakaṇṭha defended this realist and pluralist position in sustained debate with Buddhists, Mīmāṃsakas and the Śaiva non-dualists (Goodall 1998). For the Kashmiri non-dualists, by contrast, liberation is the recognition (pratyabhijñā) of one’s identity with Śiva, who alone exists. It is reached through grace and insight, by the “means” (upāyas) that culminate in the “means-less” (anupāya); ritual is valued as an expression of insight rather than as its cause; and one may be “liberated while living” (jīvanmukta), experiencing everything as the play of one’s own consciousness (Chapter 6).

    2.12 The Theory of Initiation¶

    Initiation (dīkṣā) is the central transformative event of the Śaiva religion. For the Siddhānta, since impurity is a substance, only the ritual action of initiation, in which Śiva acts through the guru, can remove it. A frequently cited traditional explanation derives the word dīkṣā from “giving” (dā) knowledge and “destroying” (kṣi) bondage.

    2.12.1 The Grades of Initiation¶

    The Siddhānta distinguishes a preliminary initiation, a liberating initiation and two further consecrations, each of which confers a grade with its own entitlements (adhikāra) and obligations (Table 2.6). Other traditions have their own sequences: the Śrīvaiṣṇava five sacraments (Section 3.7), the Buddhist consecrations (Table 10.2) and the “full consecration” (pūrṇābhiṣeka) of later Śākta lineages. A form of the liberating initiation that releases the initiate from its obligations, the “seedless” initiation given to kings, children and others, is discussed in Section 12.4.

    Table 2.6. The Grades of Śaiva Initiation and Their Entitlements
    Rite Grade Nature, purpose and entitlement
    Samayadīkṣā Samayin Preliminary initiation, which purifies the candidate, imposes the rules (samayas) and qualifies him or her to hear the scriptures, receive mantras and attend worship
    Nirvāṇadīkṣā Putraka Liberating initiation, in which the guru, through fire offerings, ritually purifies the candidate’s soul through all levels of the cosmos and destroys the karma that would cause future births; the putraka (“son”) performs the daily worship of Śiva
    Sādhakābhiṣeka Sādhaka Consecration, after the liberating initiation, of one who will propitiate a mantra-deity to gain powers (siddhi)
    Ācāryābhiṣeka Ācārya Consecration, after the liberating initiation, conferring authority to initiate others and to consecrate images and temples; in South India the temple priesthood came to be restricted to hereditary ādiśaiva lineages

    2.12.2 The Ritual of the Liberating Initiation¶

    The nirvāṇadīkṣā is described in detail in the Āgamas and especially in the manuals of Somaśambhu and others (Brunner-Lachaux 1963–1998):

    1. Preliminaries (adhivāsa): purifications of the candidate the day before; the guru interprets the candidate’s dreams as omens.

    2. Fire: the guru establishes and consecrates the sacrificial fire in a fire-pit (kuṇḍa) and invokes Śiva in it.

    3. The cord: a cord (pāśasūtra) is hung from the candidate’s crown to his toe, and the bonds and the levels of the cosmos are ritually “placed” in it.

    4. Purification of the paths (adhvaśodhana): the guru draws the candidate’s soul out and leads it through the six “paths” of the cosmos — including the thirty-six tattvas and the 224 worlds (bhuvanas) of the Siddhānta — purifying each with oblations.

    5. Destruction of karma: all karma except the portion that has begun to bear fruit in the present life (prārabdha) is burnt; this present body remains until death.

    6. Union with Śiva: the soul is joined to Śiva and returned to the candidate’s body; the cord is cut and burnt.

    2.13 The Subtle Body¶

    2.13.1 Channels (Nāḍīs)¶

    Śaiva and later yogic texts describe a network of subtle channels, conventionally 72,000, through which the breath or vital force (prāṇa) moves. Three are paramount: the central channel (suṣumnā), through which the yogin’s breath or kuṇḍalinī is made to rise, and the channels to its left (iḍā), associated with the moon and the left nostril, and to its right (piṅgalā), associated with the sun and the right nostril. Tantric traditions value the body as the instrument and site of liberation rather than an obstacle to it, and this map of the body is the ground of much of their yoga.

    2.13.2 Systems of Centres (Cakras)¶

    The early Siddhānta scriptures describe various sets of centres in the body — ādhāras (supports), granthis (knots), lakṣyas (targets of meditation) — and there was no single canonical list. Different Tantras teach four, five, six or more cakras. The Kubjikā scriptures of the tenth century or later are among the earliest to teach a system of six centres resembling the later standard one (Section 4.11); Abhinavagupta’s Tantrāloka treats kuṇḍalinī not as a physical force but as the contracted power of consciousness, whose awakening is the recognition of one’s own nature; the Nātha Gorakṣaśataka (c. 13th–14th century) and the Haṭhapradīpikā (c. 15th century) give the early haṭha-yoga accounts of its awakening; and the system of six cakras with the thousand-petalled lotus at the crown that is now familiar worldwide was given its classic form in Pūrṇānanda’s Ṣaṭcakranirūpaṇa (1577), the work through which Arthur Avalon’s The Serpent Power made it known in the West (Avalon 1919; Flood 2006). Table 2.7 summarises that scheme.

    Table 2.7. The Six Cakras and the Crown in the Ṣaṭcakranirūpaṇa
    Cakra Location Petals Element Bīja Deities
    Mūlādhāra Perineum 4 Earth laṃ Brahmā; Ḍākinī
    Svādhiṣṭhāna Genital region 6 Water vaṃ Viṣṇu; Rākinī
    Maṇipūra Navel 10 Fire raṃ Rudra; Lākinī
    Anāhata Heart 12 Air yaṃ Īśa; Kākinī
    Viśuddha Throat 16 Ether haṃ Sadāśiva; Śākinī
    Ājñā Between the brows 2 Mind oṃ Śambhu; Hākinī
    Sahasrāra Crown 1000 — — Śiva and Śakti in union

    Earlier texts teach many different systems, and this scheme should not be read back into them. The association of the cakras with endocrine glands, nerve plexuses, rainbow colours or psychological qualities derives from twentieth-century Theosophical and New Age writing, not from the Sanskrit sources (White 2003; Mallinson and Singleton 2017). The place of the cakras in haṭha yoga is treated in Volume 5, Yoga Taraṅgiṇī.

    Health and Safety Note

    Intensive breath-retention and kuṇḍalinī practices are described in the texts as dangerous without expert guidance. Modern reports associate such practices, especially when undertaken without supervision, with psychological disturbance. They should not be attempted on the basis of a book, and they are not a substitute for medical or psychological care.

    2.14 Grace (Anugraha)¶

    Grace is the fifth of Śiva’s five acts (creation, maintenance, dissolution, concealment and grace) and, for the Śaiva theologians, the purpose of the other four. Its descent upon a soul is called śaktipāta, “the falling of power”. Signs of śaktipāta include devotion to Śiva, desire for initiation, and faith in the guru. Abhinavagupta (Tantrāloka 13) classifies it in nine grades, from the most intense, which liberates at once, to the mildest, which produces a gradual turning towards the path. The guru is regarded as the channel through which Śiva’s grace reaches the disciple, and the mantra as Śiva’s power in the form of sound.

    2.15 Festivals¶

    The Śaiva year is punctuated by festivals of the temple and of the household. The great night of Śiva (Mahāśivarātri, in Māgha or Phālguna, February–March) is kept with fasting and a night-long vigil. Each large temple has its own great annual festival (mahotsava, brahmotsava) of about ten days, with the hoisting of a flag, daily processions and a chariot festival. In the Tamil country Ārudrā Darśana (Tiruvātirai, in Mārkaḻi, December–January) celebrates the dance of Naṭarāja, above all at Chidambaram; Paṅkuṉi Uttiram (March–April) the divine marriages, including that of Śiva and Pārvatī; and Kārttikai Dīpam (November–December) is a festival of lights, whose great beacon on the hill at Tiruvaṇṇāmalai recalls Śiva’s appearance as a column of fire. The festivals of Gaṇeśa (Vināyaka Caturthī) and of Murukaṉ’s victory over Sūrapadman (Skanda Ṣaṣṭhī) belong to the same calendar.

    In festival processions the movable festival image (utsavamūrti), distinct from the fixed image in the sanctum (mūlamūrti), is carried through the streets on a sequence of mounts (vāhanas) — the bull Nandi, a chariot, silver and golden mounts and others — bringing the deity to devotees who might not enter the sanctum. The theology of the festival as a re-enactment of Śiva’s five acts is set out in twelfth-century manuals such as Aghoraśiva’s Mahotsavavidhi (Davis 2010).

    2.16 Nyāsa: Ritual Placing of Mantras¶

    Nyāsa (“placing”) is the ritual installation of mantras on the body of the worshipper. Through it the worshipper is understood to replace his ordinary body with a body made of mantras, becoming fit to worship Śiva — a principle often summarised in the dictum that only one who has become Śiva may worship Śiva. In Śaiva worship the six “limb” mantras of Śiva are placed on the heart, head, topknot, shoulders, eyes and the space around the body, and on the fingers and palms; the fifty phonemes of the alphabet may be placed on fifty points of the body; and in some rites the levels of the cosmos are mapped onto it, so that it becomes a microcosm. The kinds of nyāsa common to the Tantric traditions are described in Section 9.2.2.

    2.17 Śaivism and Indian Aesthetics¶

    2.17.1 Śiva Naṭarāja¶

    The bronze image of Śiva as Naṭarāja, “King of Dancers”, dancing the ānandatāṇḍava within a ring of flames, was perfected in the Chōla period (10th–12th centuries). Its iconography is traditionally read as follows:

    • the upper right hand holds the drum (ḍamaru), the sound of creation;

    • the upper left hand holds fire, the power of dissolution;

    • the lower right hand shows the gesture of fearlessness (abhayamudrā);

    • the lower left hand points, like an elephant’s trunk (gajahasta), to the raised foot, the refuge of devotees;

    • the right foot stands on the dwarf Apasmāra, ignorance;

    • the raised left foot (kuñcitapāda) signifies grace and release;

    • the ring of flames (prabhāmaṇḍala) signifies the cosmos and its cycles.

    The Chidambaram temple, where Naṭarāja is the presiding deity, identifies its golden hall (citsabhā) with the “space of consciousness” (cidākāśa) in the heart. The Kashmiri Śaiva tradition contributed in another way: Abhinavagupta’s account of aesthetic experience (rasa) in his commentaries on the Nāṭyaśāstra and Dhvanyāloka draws on his Śaiva theology of consciousness (Chapter 6).

    2.18 Epigraphic and Archaeological Evidence¶

    The history of the Śaiva traditions rests to an unusual degree on inscriptions. The Mathurā pillar inscription of 380 names Pāśupata teachers in the line of Kuśika (Section 2.1); the inscriptions of Karnataka from the eleventh to the thirteenth century document the Kālāmukha monasteries and their teachers; and those of Rājarāja I at Thanjavur (c. 1010) record the endowments and personnel of the Bṛhadīśvara temple. Beyond India, the temples and inscriptions of My Son in Champa, the Śivagṛha inscription of 856 from Prambanan in Java and the Sdok Kak Thom inscription of 1052 from the Thai–Cambodian border show Śaiva initiation and royal religion at work in Southeast Asia. Taken together, this evidence shows that Śaiva initiation and temple worship formed the framework of an extensive institutional network across the subcontinent and beyond; it is examined in Chapter 12 (Sanderson 2009).
