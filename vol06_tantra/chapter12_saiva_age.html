    Chapter 12: The Śaiva Age: Tantra, Kingship and the Early Medieval State¶

    12.1 Introduction¶

    The Tantric traditions are often imagined as marginal: secret lineages of ascetics and adepts working on the edges of society, in cremation grounds and mountain retreats. The historical record of the early medieval period shows something very different. From about the fifth to the thirteenth century initiatory Śaivism was patronised by kings across South Asia and much of Southeast Asia; its officiants consecrated rulers and temples, its monasteries held land and villages, and its ritual systems shaped the religious practice of competing traditions. Alexis Sanderson, who has done more than any other scholar to assemble the evidence, called this period the “Śaiva Age” (Sanderson 2009).

    The “Śaiva Age” is, first of all, one scholar’s synthesis: Sanderson built it from his own reading of a very large body of scriptures, manuals and inscriptions, much of it unpublished, and it has since become the framework of much recent work on early medieval Śaivism. Chapter 1 introduced the thesis briefly (Section 1.8). This chapter examines it in more detail: the kinds of evidence on which it rests, the rites by which Śaiva preceptors served kings, the monastic networks through which Śaiva learning travelled, the response of Vaiṣṇava, Buddhist, Jain and Brahmanical rivals, and the criticisms and qualifications that other historians have offered. The question matters beyond the history of Śaivism. If Sanderson is right, much of what is now regarded as ordinary Hindu, Buddhist and Jain practice — temple consecration, image worship with nyāsa and bīja mantras, initiation by a guru — took its form in competition for the patronage of early medieval courts.

    12.2 The Thesis in Outline¶

    Sanderson’s argument, set out in a long essay of 2009 and in a series of related studies, can be summarised in four propositions (Sanderson 2009, 2013).

    1. Dominance. Between about the fifth and the thirteenth centuries the Śaiva traditions — first the ascetic Atimārga, then above all the Mantramārga of initiation and mantra — received more royal patronage than any rival religion across most of the Indian subcontinent and in Cambodia, Champa and Java.

    2. The reason: service to kings. Śaivism succeeded because its officiants made themselves useful to rulers. They offered initiation in a form that a king could accept; rites of consecration, protection and victory; the foundation and consecration of royal temples; and an organisation of monasteries that could supply trained preceptors to courts far from their home regions.

    3. Accommodation. The Siddhānta, the most widely patronised form, made itself compatible with the Brahmanical social order. It taught that Śiva’s initiation liberates while leaving the initiate’s caste duties in the world intact, so that kings, whose office was to protect the order of the varṇas, could support it without contradiction.

    4. Imitation. Vaiṣṇava, Saura, Buddhist and Jain traditions, and Brahmanical householders, competing for the same patronage, adopted the Śaiva model: its form of initiation, its liturgy of worship, its rites of consecration and its techniques of mantra.

    A fifth element concerns the end of the period. In Sanderson’s account the Śaiva Age closed in North India with the loss of royal patronage after the Turkic conquests of the late twelfth and thirteenth centuries, and with the rise of Vaiṣṇava devotion and of Brahmanical orders centred on Vedānta; it lasted longer in the South, where the Śaiva Siddhānta survived as the liturgy of the Tamil temples (Section 12.12).

    The thesis is not simply that kings were pious. It is a claim about the mechanism by which a religion spread: through the practical value of its specialists to those who held power, and through the transfer of its institutions and rituals to new regions and to rival traditions.

    12.3 The Evidence¶

    The case rests on several kinds of source, each with its own limitations.

    Inscriptions. Copper-plate charters and stone inscriptions record grants of land to temples, monasteries and preceptors, name the gurus of kings, and sometimes describe the religious affiliation of donors in technical terms. They are dated or datable and cover the whole region, but they record donations rather than belief, and the surviving corpus is uneven: some dynasties are richly documented, others hardly at all. Sanderson has argued that inscriptions also change how the scriptures themselves should be read, since they show which texts and lineages were actually patronised (Sanderson 2013).

    Scriptures and manuals. The Śaiva scriptures and the ritual manuals (paddhatis) prescribe rites for kings and give rules for the conduct of royal preceptors. Their prescriptions show what officiants offered, though not how often these rites were performed.

    Monuments and images. Royal temples, their liṅgas and their iconographic programmes are the most visible evidence of patronage. Their scale — from the Kailāsanātha at Kāñcīpuram to the Bṛhadīśvara at Thanjavur and the temple-mountains of Angkor — shows the resources that Śaiva cults could command.

    Literature. Court literature, satire and chronicle give glimpses of Śaiva preceptors at work. Kalhaṇa’s Rājataraṅgiṇī (1148–49) records the religious foundations of the kings of Kashmir; the eleventh-century Kashmiri satirist Kṣemendra mocked self-serving Tantric gurus and their royal and mercantile clients.

    12.4 Initiating the King¶

    12.4.1 A Form of Initiation for Rulers¶

    The full Śaiva initiation imposed demanding obligations. The initiate had to perform daily worship of Śiva, observe the rules (samayas) of his grade, and in some cases undertake further disciplines for the rest of his life (Chapter 2). A king, occupied with government and war, could not be expected to do so. The Siddhānta solved the problem by distinguishing two forms of liberating initiation. In the initiation “with seed” (sabījā), the initiate’s capacity to observe the post-initiatory rules is presupposed and the rules are binding. In the “seedless” initiation (nirbījā) the guru, out of compassion, removes the obligation to observe them. The texts recommend the seedless form for those unable to bear the burden: children, the very old, the sick, women in some texts, and the ruler. Liberation at death was promised all the same; the king’s duty was reduced to devotion to Śiva and to his guru (Sanderson 2009; Brunner-Lachaux 1963–1998).

    This was a decisive innovation. It meant that Śaiva initiation, which had begun as the preserve of ascetics, could be offered to a king without asking him to change his way of life, and it gave the guru who conferred it a lasting claim on the king’s gratitude. Inscriptions accordingly speak of royal “initiation preceptors” (dīkṣāguru) alongside the royal chaplain (purohita).

    12.4.2 The Rājaguru and the Rites of the Realm¶

    The Śaiva royal preceptor (rājaguru) took over many of the functions that Brahmanical law assigned to the king’s purohita. Śaiva and Pāñcarātra texts give their own versions of the royal consecration (abhiṣeka), in which the king is bathed with water from pots charged with mantras and so empowered by the deity. They provide rites to protect the king and his kingdom against enemies, disease, epidemics and evil portents; rites of appeasement (śānti) and of increase (puṣṭi), performed at times of crisis, at the new year and before campaigns; and rites for success in war, sometimes involving the fierce forms of Śiva and the Goddess and, in the Bhairava and Kaula traditions, the propitiation of yoginīs (Section 4.10).

    The Netratantra, commented on by the Kashmiri exegete Kṣemarāja in the eleventh century, gives particular attention to rites performed by a preceptor for the protection of the king, and Sanderson has argued that the ordinary Śaiva ritual of Kashmir, based on the Svacchandatantra and the Netratantra, was also the ritual of the royal court (Sanderson 2009). In this way the Mantramārga’s ancient pursuit of supernatural power (siddhi) was redirected towards the needs of the state.

    12.4.3 A Literary Portrait: Puṣpabhūti and Bhairavācārya¶

    Court literature shows how such relationships were imagined. In the third chapter of Bāṇa’s Harṣacarita (7th century), the founder of King Harṣa’s dynasty, Puṣpabhūti, becomes the devotee of a Śaiva adept from the South named Bhairavācārya. At the adept’s request the king and three companions guard a night-time rite in a cremation ground, in which Bhairavācārya, seated on a corpse, seeks the status of a vidyādhara, a semi-divine being of the air. When a nāga disrupts the rite the king overcomes him, and a goddess rewards the king with the promise that he will found a great royal line. The story is a romance, not a record; but it shows a seventh-century court poet taking for granted that a king might serve a Śaiva adept, and that the adept’s power might serve the dynasty in return (Lorenzen 1972).

    12.5 The Goddess and the King¶

    The Śaiva preceptor was not the only Tantric specialist at court. From the early medieval period onwards, the Goddess in her martial forms became the guardian of kingship across much of India, and her autumn festival became the great royal ritual of the year. The frame story of the Devīmāhātmya already sets the pattern: King Suratha, driven from his kingdom, worships the Goddess on the advice of a sage and regains his throne (Section 4.2).

    Later texts, among them the Kālikā Purāṇa, prescribe the autumn worship of Durgā as a duty of kings, performed for victory and the prosperity of the realm. Its royal form combined several elements: the worship of the Goddess in an image, a pot or a yantra over nine nights; the worship of the king’s weapons, horses, elephants and insignia (āyudhapūjā); blood sacrifice, above all of buffaloes, re-enacting the Goddess’s victory over the buffalo demon; and, on the tenth day, the lustration of the army (nīrājana) and a royal procession marking the start of the campaigning season (Fuller 1992). Bihani Sarkar has traced the growth of this martial cult of Durgā in Sanskrit literature and ritual texts from about the sixth century, and argues that a “heroic” Śāktism, centred on the warrior goddess and her blood offerings, became part of the very idea of early medieval kingship (Sarkar 2017). The household and festival forms of the same worship are described in Section 9.9.

    The festival can be followed across many kingdoms. At Vijayanagara the Portuguese visitor Domingo Paes, writing about 1520, described the nine-day Mahānavamī at which the king received the homage of his nobles, reviewed his troops and witnessed the sacrifice of great numbers of buffaloes and sheep; the stone platform from which the kings watched it still stands at Hampi. The Woḍeyar kings of Mysore continued the Vijayanagara festival as the Mysore Dasarā, and in Nepal the Dasaĩ festival, centred on the royal goddess Taleju, kept its royal character, with sacrifices offered on the king’s behalf, until the abolition of the monarchy in 2008 (Section 4.7). The Marāṭhā king Śivājī took as his tutelary deity the goddess Bhavānī of Tuljapur, and the Rajput clans of the North-west each honoured a lineage goddess (kuladevī) as protector of their rule (Section 13.8).

    Royal goddess worship did not usually require the king’s Tantric initiation, and much of it was conducted according to Purāṇic rather than Tantric texts. But it drew on the same repertoire of mantra, sacrifice and protective rites, its officiants were often Tantric specialists, and in Nepal, Assam and Bengal the royal cults of Taleju, Kāmākhyā and Durgā were explicitly Tantric (Chapter 13).

    12.6 Monasteries, Lineages and Networks¶

    12.6.1 The Śaiva Monastery¶

    The institution that carried Śaiva learning across the subcontinent was the monastery (maṭha). A Śaiva maṭha was headed by an ascetic ācārya who had received the highest consecration and could initiate others and consecrate temples; it housed his ascetic pupils, who were often given initiation names ending in -śiva or -śambhu; and it was usually attached to a temple whose worship its members performed or supervised. Endowed with land and villages by kings and local lords, monasteries became centres of learning, teaching not only Śaiva scripture but grammar, logic, literature and Mīmāṃsā, and they distributed food to ascetics and the poor (Sanderson 2009; Lorenzen 1972).

    Succession passed from teacher to initiated pupil, not from father to son, so that a monastic lineage could found branches in distant regions without depending on a local kinship group. This mobility was central to the Śaiva Age. A king who wished to establish a royal temple could invite a preceptor from an established lineage, endow a new monastery for him, and so acquire a complete religious institution, with its texts, its rites and its trained personnel.

    12.6.2 The Mattamayūra and Golakī Lineages¶

    Two Siddhānta lineages of central India illustrate the pattern. The Mattamayūra lineage took its name from a place in the region of Gwalior, where its first known leaders were settled by a local king in the ninth century. An inscription at Ranod (ancient Raṇipadra) describes the ascetic Purandara, invited by the king to his capital, founding monasteries there and at Raṇipadra; his pupils and their pupils later served as preceptors to the Kalacuri kings of Tripūrī (near Jabalpur) and to other dynasties of central India. The Golakī lineage, also patronised by the Kalacuris, later extended into the Deccan and Andhra (Sanderson 2009).

    The fullest single document of such a network is the Malkāpuram pillar inscription of 1261, in the Guntur district of Andhra Pradesh, issued in the reign of the Kākatīya queen Rudramadevī. It traces the Golakī lineage over several generations, and claims for its preceptors the respect of kings of several dynasties, including the Kalacuris, the Chōḷas and the rulers of Mālava. Its hero is Viśveśvaraśambhu, a Śaiva ascetic from Bengal who was the initiating guru of the Kākatīya king Gaṇapati (r. 1199–1262). The inscription records the gift of land on which he founded a Śaiva settlement: a temple, a monastery, a feeding house said to be open to all, a school with teachers of the Vedas, grammar, logic, literature and the Āgamas, a hospital and a maternity house, together with Brahmin households and the craftsmen needed to sustain them (Talbot 2001; Sanderson 2009). Whatever its rhetorical exaggerations, the inscription shows a Śaiva monastic foundation acting as an instrument of settlement and social provision in a frontier region.

    12.6.3 Preceptors on the Move¶

    The inscriptions repeatedly show Śaiva preceptors working far from their homelands. Viśveśvaraśambhu moved from Bengal to Andhra. Under the Chōḷas, an inscription of the reign of Rājendra I at Thanjavur provides for the support of the Śaiva preceptor of the royal temple, Sarvaśiva Paṇḍita, and of his pupils and their pupils from “Āryadeśa, Madhyadeśa and Gauḍadeśa”, that is, from northern and eastern India (Sanderson 2009). The Kashmiri Siddhānta exegesis of Rāmakaṇṭha and his predecessors reached the Tamil country in the same way, and was absorbed into the manuals of Aghoraśiva of Chidambaram in the twelfth century (Section 2.3). The Tamil Śaiva Siddhānta that survives today is thus, in part, the southern outcome of a network that once reached from Kashmir to Bengal and the Deccan.

    The ascetic orders of the Atimārga formed similar networks. The Kālāmukhas of Karnataka ran temple-monasteries endowed by the Chālukya and Hoysaḷa kings and their feudatories, and Pāśupata and Lākula teachers appear in inscriptions from Gujarat to Nepal (Section 2.1.6).

    12.7 Temples, Liṅgas and the Names of Kings¶

    The royal temple was the most conspicuous product of the alliance between Śaiva preceptors and kings. Its consecration was a Śaiva rite, performed by the ācārya according to the kriyāpāda of the Āgamas and the manuals (Chapter 11). The liṅga of a royal foundation was often named after its founder: the Pallava Rājasiṃha’s temple at Kāñcīpuram enshrines Rājasiṃheśvara; Rājarāja I’s temple at Thanjavur, consecrated about 1010, is called Rājarājeśvara in its inscriptions; his son Rājendra I built Gaṅgaikoṇḍacōḷeśvara at his new capital to commemorate his northern campaign. The king’s name joined to that of the Lord (īśvara) bound the ruler’s fame to the deity’s permanence, and the temple’s endowments made the king’s piety visible in the agrarian economy around it (Section 1.8).

    The same pattern appears in Cambodia. In 881 the Khmer king Indravarman I consecrated a liṅga named Indreśvara in the temple-mountain of Bakong; his successor Yaśovarman I founded āśramas for ascetics of different communities, among them Śaivas, Vaiṣṇavas and Buddhists, and inscribed their rules on steles. The Sdok Kak Thom inscription of 1052, from the present Thai–Cambodian border, is the most detailed single witness. It records that at the beginning of the ninth century a Brahmin named Hiraṇyadāma taught four Tantras — named as the Vināśikha, Nayottara, Sammoha and Śiraścheda — to the priest of King Jayavarman II, in connection with the royal cult, and it traces a single priestly family that served that cult for some two and a half centuries. The first of these Tantras is probably the Vīṇāśikha, the surviving scripture of the Vāma stream (Section 2.6). What that cult was — the devarāja or “god who is king” of the inscription’s Sanskrit, rendered in Khmer as the “lord of the world who is king” — is debated. The older view saw in it the deification of the king; Hermann Kulke argued instead that it was a movable image of a god that protected the king and accompanied the court, and Sanderson has related it to the Śaiva Tantras named in the inscription (Kulke 1993; Sanderson 2003–2004).

    Royal temples did not only express legitimacy. They also absorbed local cults. A goddess of a hill tribe or a village liṅga could be installed within a Śaiva temple complex as a form or attendant of Śiva, given an Āgamic liturgy and a Brahmin priest, and so drawn into the religious order of the kingdom. Historians of the early medieval state, notably Hermann Kulke and B.D. Chattopadhyaya, have made this process of integration a central theme of their work (Kulke 1993; Chattopadhyaya 1994).

    12.8 A Regional Case: Kashmir¶

    Kashmir allows the Śaiva Age to be observed in unusual detail, because it has both a large Śaiva literature and a court chronicle, Kalhaṇa’s Rājataraṅgiṇī. The chronicle shows kings founding temples of every persuasion. Lalitāditya Muktāpīḍa (8th century) built the great Sun temple of Mārtaṇḍa as well as Vaiṣṇava and Buddhist foundations; Avantivarman (r. 855–883) founded the town of Avantipura with a Śiva temple named Avantīśvara and a Viṣṇu temple named Avantisvāmin, after himself, in the manner described above. Royal religion in Kashmir was thus plural; but the scholarly and ritual culture of its Brahmins was overwhelmingly Śaiva.

    Abhinavagupta himself traced his family to this alliance of court and Śaiva learning: at the end of the Tantrāloka he records that his ancestor Atrigupta was brought to Kashmir from the Gangetic plain by Lalitāditya and settled there with an endowment. The Siddhānta exegetes Rāmakaṇṭha and his father were Kashmiri Brahmins of the same milieu. Sanderson has argued that the ordinary Śaiva ritual of the Valley — the initiation and worship prescribed by the Svacchandatantra and the Netratantra, performed by hereditary family preceptors — formed the religious practice of most Kashmiri Brahmins, including those at court, while the non-dual Trika and Krama were cultivated by a smaller circle of virtuosi (Sanderson 2009, 1988).

    Kashmir also supplies the period’s sharpest internal criticism. Kṣemendra (11th century) satirised gurus who exploited the households and officials who patronised them, and Kalhaṇa (12th century) repeatedly censured kings who fell under the influence of self-interested preceptors. Such criticism, from within a Śaiva society, shows that the power of Tantric specialists at court was real enough to provoke resentment. Elements of this culture survive in the domestic religion of Kashmiri Pandits, whose festival of Śivarātri (Herath) includes the worship of Bhairava and of Śiva’s attendants in water-filled pots — a household rite whose structure is Tantric (Chapter 6).

    12.9 Beyond India: Champa, Java, Sumatra and Bali¶

    The reach of the Śaiva model is clearest in Southeast Asia, where Indian scriptures and rites were adopted by courts with no Indian population of any size. Apart from Cambodia (Section 12.7), the evidence comes from four regions.

    In Champa, in present-day central Vietnam, the sanctuary of My Son housed the royal liṅga Bhadreśvara, named after an early king, Bhadravarman, and later re-established by his successors. The Cham kings were mostly Śaiva, though Indravarman II founded a great Buddhist monastery at Dong Duong in 875.

    In Java the Śaiva temple complex of Prambanan, whose foundation is recorded in the Śivagṛha inscription of 856, stood close to the Buddhist Borobudur. A body of Śaiva literature in Sanskrit and Old Javanese, such as the Vṛhaspatitattva, expounds a doctrine of the tattvas, souls and liberation closely related to that of the Siddhānta. In the Majapahit period Mpu Tantular’s Old Javanese poem Sutasoma (14th century) declared that Śiva and the Buddha are one; a phrase from it, bhinneka tunggal ika (“different, yet one”), is now the national motto of Indonesia.

    In Sumatra and East Java Tantric Buddhism was also a royal religion. The East Javanese king Kṛtanagara of Singhasari (r. 1268–1292) is remembered for his Tantric Buddhist observances, and a colossal statue from Sumatra is usually identified as the fourteenth-century ruler Ādityavarman in the form of a Buddhist Bhairava.

    In Bali Śaiva ritual survives to the present. Balinese Brahmin priests (pedanda) of Śaiva and Buddhist lineages consecrate the holy water on which Balinese religion depends. The Śaiva priest’s daily rite of sūrya-sevana, in which he purifies himself, identifies himself with Śiva by mantra and nyāsa, and worships Śiva in the Sun, preserves in simplified form the structure of the Indian Siddhānta’s daily worship (Hooykaas 1966).

    12.10 Competing for Patronage¶

    12.10.1 Vaiṣṇavas and Brahmins¶

    The Śaiva model did not go unchallenged. Vaiṣṇava kings and preceptors built an equivalent system on the Pāñcarātra Saṃhitās, with their own initiation, temple liturgy and royal rites (Chapter 3); in Kashmir the Pāñcarātra had its own royal temples and exegetes, and in the Tamil country the great Vaiṣṇava temples followed the Pāñcarātra or Vaikhānasa liturgies. Brahmin householders of the Smārta tradition, for their part, absorbed Tantric elements into their own domestic and temple worship while keeping their Vedic identity: the worship of five deities (pañcāyatanapūjā), with its nyāsa, mantras and offerings, is Tantric in structure though Vedic in self-image. Sanderson sees in this “Tantricisation” of Brahmanical practice one of the most enduring effects of the Śaiva Age (Sanderson 2009; Bühnemann 1988).

    12.10.2 Buddhists¶

    Buddhist monasteries had long enjoyed royal support. In eastern India the Pāla kings (8th–12th centuries) were the great patrons of Buddhist learning: Dharmapāla (r. c. 770–810) is credited with founding Vikramaśīla, which became a centre of Tantric study (Chapter 10). Even the Pālas, however, made grants to Brahmins and to Śaiva temples, and the Buddhist Tantras that flourished under them borrowed extensively from Śaiva scripture. Ronald Davidson has emphasised a further parallel. The central Buddhist Tantric rite, the abhiṣeka, takes its name and much of its form from royal consecration: the initiate is consecrated as a king of the maṇḍala, which is itself organised like a royal court, with the central deity surrounded by subordinate lords in the directions. In Davidson’s reading, esoteric Buddhism sacralised the political order of the period, in which overlords presided over circles of vassals (Davidson 2002).

    12.10.3 Jains¶

    The Jains, a community of merchants and of royal ministers in western and southern India, also competed for and obtained royal favour — most famously at the court of the Caulukya king Kumārapāla of Gujarat (r. 1143–1172), the patron of the monk-scholar Hemacandra. Jain teachers adopted mantra rites, protective goddesses and image consecration in forms that parallel Śaiva practice while rejecting its sacrificial and transgressive elements (Section 10.19).

    12.10.4 Lay Śaivism and the Gift¶

    Not all Śaiva patronage passed through initiation. A large literature addressed to uninitiated lay devotees, the Śivadharma corpus, taught the merit of building and maintaining temples, of feeding Śaiva ascetics, and of copying manuscripts of Śaiva scripture and presenting them in a solemn “gift of knowledge” (vidyādāna) to a monastery or temple library. Florinda De Simini has shown how these texts, widely copied in Nepal, made the production and donation of manuscripts a religious act in its own right (De Simini 2016). Such teachings gave kings, queens, ministers and merchants a way of earning Śiva’s favour without the obligations of initiation, and they help to explain the scale of Śaiva foundations.

    12.10.5 Queens, Ministers and Merchants¶

    Patronage was not confined to reigning kings. Queens were among the most active founders. The Chōḷa queen Sembiyaṉ Mahādevī, widow of Gaṇḍarāditya, rebuilt a series of older brick shrines in stone in the later tenth century and endowed bronzes for them, and her foundations anticipate the great royal temples of the following generation. In central India an inscription of the Kalacuri queen Alhaṇadevī at Bheraghat (1155) records a temple of Śiva and a monastery placed in the charge of a Pāśupata ascetic; in Andhra, the Malkāpuram foundation was recorded in the name of the ruling queen Rudramadevī. Ministers, feudatories, merchant guilds and village assemblies endowed lamps, festivals and recitations in temples across the South, as the thousands of Tamil and Kannada temple inscriptions show. The Śaiva Age was thus sustained by a broad elite, not by kings alone.

    12.11 Critics and Qualifications¶

    Few scholars have published a sustained critique of the thesis as a whole. The qualifications gathered here come in two kinds. Some address the thesis directly: they question how much the inscriptions can show, or how far the Buddhist Tantras borrowed from the Śaiva. Others come from work on other questions — the Purāṇic religion of the period, the nature of the early medieval state, the politics of Sanskrit and of images — which bears on the thesis without having been written against it. The two should be kept apart.

    The weight of the evidence. The first and most direct qualification concerns the sources. Inscriptions record what was given, and to whom; they do not measure what most people believed or practised. The early medieval record also contains abundant Vaiṣṇava, Saura, Buddhist and Jain patronage: the Gupta kings called themselves devotees of Viṣṇu (paramabhāgavata); the Pālas were Buddhists; great Sun temples were built from Multan to Koṇārka; and in Odisha the cult of Jagannātha eventually became the state cult. “Dominance” therefore describes a balance of patronage across a very wide region, not a monopoly in any one place.

    Purāṇic and lay Śaivism. Work on other sources bears on the thesis without being directed against it. Hans Bakker’s study of the early Skandapurāṇa shows a Śaivism of the sixth and seventh centuries in northern India that is closely tied to Pāśupata ascetics and to kings, but is Purāṇic rather than Tantric in character (Bakker 2014). Together with the Śivadharma literature, this suggests that much royal and popular Śaivism was carried by devotional and Purāṇic forms in which the Mantramārga was one strand among several.

    Feudalism, integration and legitimation. Sanderson’s account intersects with a long debate among historians about the early medieval state. One school, associated with R.S. Sharma, described the period as one of “Indian feudalism”, in which grants to Brahmins, temples and monasteries fragmented royal power; another, associated with Kulke and Chattopadhyaya, saw the same grants as instruments by which states expanded and integrated new regions (see Volume 18, Rājanīti and Dharmaśāstra). Davidson’s account of Buddhist Tantra builds on the first model; Sanderson’s account of Śaiva monasteries fits easily with the second (Davidson 2002; Chattopadhyaya 1994; Kulke 1993).

    Beyond legitimation. Some historians are wary of explaining royal religion as a search for “legitimacy”. Neither of the two works cited here was written as a response to Sanderson, but both bear on his argument. Sheldon Pollock has argued that the political culture of the period expressed itself above all through Sanskrit literary forms common to rulers of every persuasion, so that a king’s choice of god did not by itself determine how his rule was represented (Pollock 2006). Richard Davis has shown that kings could also treat the images of their rivals’ gods as trophies of war, carrying them off to their own capitals; royal relations with the gods were a matter of competitive display as much as of legitimation (Davis 1997). Sanderson’s emphasis on the practical services of Śaiva officiants — protection, consecration, healing, the founding of settlements — partly answers this objection, since it does not depend on a single theory of legitimacy.

    Who borrowed from whom? The second direct qualification concerns Buddhist Tantra. The claim that it borrowed from Śaiva scripture rests on detailed textual parallels, and few specialists now doubt that the Buddhist Yoginītantras redacted Śaiva material. Davidson and Samuel, however, emphasise the shared milieu of wandering adepts, goddess cults and local deities from which both drew, and caution against treating every parallel as a one-way loan (Davidson 2002; Samuel 2008).

    What is not in dispute is the documentary core of the thesis: the inscriptions that name Śaiva preceptors of kings, the monastic lineages that can be followed across regions, and the ritual forms that passed from Śaiva scripture into other traditions. What remains open is how much weight that evidence can bear for the religion of the period as a whole, beyond the courts and monasteries that produced it.

    12.12 The End of the Śaiva Age¶

    From the late twelfth century the conquests of the Ghurids and the establishment of the Delhi Sultanate removed or weakened many of the Hindu dynasties that had patronised Śaiva monasteries in North India. The great endowed monasteries could not survive without their villages, and the lineages that had supplied preceptors to courts disappeared from the record. In Kashmir the Śaiva traditions continued under local rulers into the fourteenth century and survived thereafter in Brahmin families (Chapter 6).

    Elsewhere the religious landscape shifted towards devotional Vaiṣṇavism and towards Brahmanical orders centred on Advaita Vedānta, such as the monastery of Śṛṅgerī, which enjoyed the patronage of the Vijayanagara kings from the fourteenth century. In the Tamil country the Śaiva Siddhānta survived as the liturgy of the temples, administered by hereditary ādiśaiva priests, and as a Tamil theology cultivated in the non-Brahmin ātīṉams (Chapter 2); in Nepal and Bali Śaiva ritual remained tied to the royal court and to priestly families until modern times. The Śaiva Age ended, but its ritual forms, absorbed into temple liturgy, Smārta worship and the practice of other traditions, did not.

    12.13 After the Śaiva Age: Tantric Power at Later Courts¶

    The decline of royal Śaiva monasticism did not end the interest of rulers in the powers that Tantric specialists claimed. Under the Vijayanagara kings and their successors the royal festival of the Goddess flourished (Section 12.5); in Assam the Ahom kings made Kāmākhyā a state cult; in Nepal the Malla and Shah kings maintained the secret worship of Taleju; and at Jodhpur a Rajput king handed his kingdom’s religious life to the Nāth yogis (Chapter 13).

    Islamicate courts were not untouched. Persian translations and adaptations of yogic and Tantric material were made from the sixteenth century, and Sufis and Nāth yogis met and competed at shrines and courts (Section 13.12.1) (Ernst 2003). At Bijapur in the Deccan an illustrated encyclopaedia of the sciences, the Nujūm al-‘ulūm (1570), includes depictions of yoginīs among its treatments of astrology and magic, and Bijapur painters produced celebrated images of yoginīs as figures of beauty and power. The Mughal emperors, beginning with Akbar, issued grants to the Nāth yogis of Jakhbar in the Punjab, whose monastery preserved a series of imperial and later documents confirming its lands (Goswamy and Grewal 1967). None of this amounts to a “Tantric” state; but it shows that the yogi and the Tantric adept remained figures whom rulers of many faiths thought worth cultivating long after the Śaiva Age had passed.
