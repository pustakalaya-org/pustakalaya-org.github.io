    Chapter 3: The Vaiṣṇava Āgamas: Pāñcarātra and Vaikhānasa¶

    sarvasya cāhaṃ hṛdi saṃniviṣṭo
    mattaḥ smṛtir jñānam apohanaṃ ca |
    vedaiś ca sarvair aham eva vedyo
    vedāntakṛd vedavid eva cāham ||

    — Bhagavad Gītā 15.15: “I am seated in the heart of all; from me come memory, knowledge and their loss. I am what is to be known through all the Vedas; I am the maker of the Vedānta and the knower of the Veda.”

    The Vaiṣṇava Āgamic traditions — above all the Pāñcarātra and the Vaikhānasa — are the ritual and theological systems of the worshippers of Viṣṇu-Nārāyaṇa. Just as the Śaiva Āgamas provide the foundation of Śaiva temple worship, the Vaiṣṇava Āgamas govern worship in the great Viṣṇu temples of South India, among them Śrīraṅgam, Kāñcīpuram and Tirumala-Tirupati, and the shrines celebrated by the Tamil Āḻvār poets.

    3.1 The Pāñcarātra Tradition¶

    3.1.1 Origins and the Name¶

    The Pāñcarātra is one of the oldest named Vaiṣṇava traditions: the Nārāyāṇīya section of the Mahābhārata (Śāntiparvan) already refers to it, together with Sāṃkhya, Yoga, the Veda and the Pāśupata, as a body of doctrine. Its surviving Saṃhitās, however, are Tantric scriptures composed from about the sixth or seventh century onwards, first in Kashmir and North India and later, in very large numbers, in South India (Schrader 1916; Matsubara 1994).

    The meaning of the name (“five nights”) was uncertain even to the tradition. The Śatapatha Brāhmaṇa (13.6.1) describes a five-day sacrifice performed by Puruṣa Nārāyaṇa, by which he became all beings; later texts offer several explanations of their own, for instance that the doctrine was taught over five nights, or that it outshines five other systems as the sun outshines the night. None of these can be confirmed.

    3.1.2 Para, Vyūha, Vibhava, Antaryāmin and Arcā¶

    The central theological doctrine of the Pāñcarātra is that the supreme Lord manifests in five modes, which become progressively more accessible to devotees:

    1. Para: the transcendent Vāsudeva, possessing all six divine qualities (Section 3.1.3).

    2. Vyūha (“emanations”; Table 3.1): Vāsudeva and the three further emanations Saṁkarṣaṇa, Pradyumna and Aniruddha, who govern creation, maintenance and dissolution and the teaching of the path. Each of the three later emanations manifests two of the six qualities prominently.

    3. Vibhava (“manifestations”): the descents (avatāras); the Ahirbudhnya Saṃhitā lists thirty-nine, while the Purāṇic list of ten became standard.

    4. Antaryāmin: the Lord as the inner controller dwelling in the heart of every being.

    5. Arcā: the consecrated image in the temple or home, in which the Lord voluntarily makes himself present and accessible.

    Table 3.1. The Four Vyūhas in Pāñcarātra Theology
    Vyūha Qualities stressed Traditional role
    Vāsudeva All six The supreme Lord, source of the other emanations
    Saṁkarṣaṇa Knowledge, strength Presides over individual souls; associated with dissolution and with teaching the scriptures
    Pradyumna Lordship, virility Presides over mind; associated with creation and with teaching right conduct
    Aniruddha Power, splendour Presides over the ego-principle; associated with maintenance and with granting the fruit of practice

    Divine Accessibility (Saulabhya)

    The fivefold scheme expresses a theology of divine condescension (saulabhya): the infinite Lord becomes progressively more accessible to finite devotees, culminating in the arcā form in which, in later Śrīvaiṣṇava theology, he places himself at the mercy of his worshippers. This doctrine underpins the importance of temple worship in Vaiṣṇavism.

    3.1.3 The Six Divine Qualities¶

    The supreme Vāsudeva possesses six qualities (ṣāḍguṇya), which the Pāñcarātra texts define in characteristic terms: knowledge (jñāna), an omniscience that comprehends all things at once; lordship (aiśvarya), independent sovereignty over all; power (śakti), the capacity to be the material cause of the world; strength (bala), the ability to support the universe without fatigue; virility (vīrya), the capacity to remain unchanged though the cause of change; and splendour (tejas), a self-sufficiency that needs no auxiliary cause. Together these qualities constitute bhaga, and the one who possesses them is bhagavat, “the Lord”.

    3.2 The Principal Pāñcarātra Saṃhitās¶

    Later lists name between 108 and over two hundred Saṃhitās, many of them lost. Three early works, the Sātvata, Pauṣkara and Jayākhya, are honoured as the “three gems” (ratnatraya); later South Indian works were regarded as dependent on them: the Īśvara Saṃhitā on the Sātvata (it governs worship at Melkote), the Pārameśvara on the Pauṣkara (Śrīraṅgam), and the Pādma on the Jayākhya (associated with Kañcīpuram).

    3.2.1 Before the “Three Gems”: The Nepalese Pāñcarātras¶

    The “three gems” are not, as was long assumed, the earliest Pāñcarātra scriptures. In cataloguing the Nepalese manuscripts microfilmed by the Nepal-German Manuscript Preservation Project, Diwakar Acharya identified three works of an earlier stage, which he published in 2015: the Svāyambhuvapañcarātra, whose palm-leaf manuscript was copied in 1027; the Devāmṛtapañcarātra, preserved in a single incomplete manuscript of about the twelfth century; and the Aṣṭādaśavidhāna, a ritual manual of the first, transmitted in the same manuscript of 1027 (Acharya 2015). The oldest of them shows affinities with two of the later books of the Niśvāsatattvasaṃhitā (Section 2.4), so that it must postdate them. Acharya proposes no precise date, but on this reckoning the texts could go back to about 700 CE.

    What makes these works important is the stage of development they reveal. Their deity is a single-headed Viṣṇu with two, four or eight arms — the eight-armed form is pan-Indian and very old — and their theology still treats Rudra, Brahmā and Janārdana as aspects of one Lord, in the manner of the Bhagavadgītā. The Devāmṛta prescribes the worship of five images, Narasiṃha, Varāha, Vāmana, Trivikrama and Vāsudeva in his cosmic form. At the same time the texts already follow a Śaiva model: they impose on the worshipper’s body a Vaiṣṇava version of the five “Brahma” mantras that the Śaivas had inherited from the Pāśupatas, and their ritual is organised around a maṇḍala of mantra-deities in concentric enclosures. They also distinguish the Vaiṣṇavas of the Pāñcarātra from the Bhāgavatas, which may reflect an older distinction between the worshippers of Nārāyaṇa and those of Vāsudeva-Kṛṣṇa that the later tradition forgot. The goddess, who is central to the Lakṣmītantra, hardly appears: these are Tantras of an unfeminised kind, comparable to the early Siddhānta (Acharya 2015).

    3.2.2 The Jayākhya Saṃhitā¶

    The Jayākhya, in thirty-three chapters, is among the oldest and most systematic surviving Saṃhitās; it was known in Kashmir by the ninth or tenth century. It treats the extraction (uddhāra) of mantras, initiation, daily worship, fire offering, yoga and the pursuit of powers, in a manner closely parallel to contemporary Śaiva scriptures.

    The parallel is not accidental. Sanderson has shown that the ritual system of the Jayākhya is modelled on that of the Śaiva Mantramārga, from the grades of initiation to the structure of the daily worship (Sanderson 2009), and Acharya has argued that it is a reworking of an earlier scripture, the Jayottaratantra (Acharya 2015). It already knows a fourfold classification of initiates that the earliest Śaiva sources lack, which rules out the very early date (about the fifth century) once proposed for it in the foreword to its first edition (Gaekwad’s Oriental Series, 1931); it is now placed later, though before its use by the Kashmiri authors of the tenth century. Its theology centres on Vāsudeva accompanied by his powers. The same theology underlies a form of the Lord that dominated the art of Kashmir between the eighth and the tenth centuries: Vaikuṇṭha, a four-faced image whose human front face is flanked by the heads of a lion and a boar, with a fierce face at the back, often identified with the sage Kapila. Whether the side faces were understood as Narasiṃha and Varāha or, more abstractly, as the three further vyūhas grouped around Vāsudeva, is debated; the four-faced form expresses in a single image the doctrine of Table 3.1. The Jayākhya’s influence outlasted Kashmir: in South India the Pādma Saṃhitā was regarded as dependent on it.

    3.2.3 The Sātvata Saṃhitā¶

    The Sātvata is centred on the worship of Vāsudeva and the vyūhas and was commented on in South India by Alasiṅgabhaṭṭa. It was quoted, along with the Jayākhya and Pauṣkara, by Yāmuna and Rāmānuja’s school as authoritative.

    3.2.4 The Pauṣkara Saṃhitā¶

    The Pauṣkara is valued for its treatment of worship and of ritual diagrams (maṇḍalas), and through the Pārameśvara Saṃhitā it is the basis of worship at Śrīraṅgam.

    The Pārameśvara itself shows how a scripture could be rewritten for a single temple. Marion Rastelli’s study places its composition in the twelfth or thirteenth century in the milieu of the Raṅganātha temple, and reads it as a record of the Pāñcarātra as it was practised there: it takes over much of the older Pauṣkara, but adapts the daily and festival worship to the needs of a great temple with a large staff, a public of non-initiated devotees and an established Śrīvaiṣṇava community (Rastelli 2006). In this sense the South Indian Saṃhitās are not merely late copies of northern originals but documents of a living temple liturgy, and they are still cited when the procedures of particular temples are disputed.

    3.2.5 The Ahirbudhnya Saṃhitā¶

    The Ahirbudhnya, in sixty chapters and named after the Vedic serpent-deity Ahirbudhnya (here identified with Śiva, who receives the teaching), is the most philosophically elaborate of the Saṃhitās. It is especially concerned with the worship of Sudarśana, Viṣṇu’s discus, as the Lord’s power, and contains an account of other doctrinal systems. Schrader’s study of it (1916) was the foundation of modern Pāñcarātra scholarship (Schrader 1916). Sudarśana here is more than a weapon: he is the Lord’s will (saṁkalpa) and active power, and the text gives rites with a diagram of Sudarśana for the protection of the king and his realm, a Vaiṣṇava counterpart to the Śaiva services to kings described in Chapter 12. Sudarśana shrines, with an image of the discus personified, often backed by Narasiṃha, are still a feature of South Indian Viṣṇu temples.

    3.2.6 The Lakṣmītantra¶

    The Lakṣmītantra is unusual in that the teaching is given by the goddess Lakṣmī to Indra. Lakṣmī is presented as the Lord’s inseparable power (śakti), the agent of creation and the active principle in the universe; she is also the compassionate mediator (puruṣakāra) between souls and the Lord. The text is an important source for the theology of Śrī and for the doctrine of surrender discussed below (Gupta et al. 1979).

    3.3 The Vaikhānasa Tradition¶

    The Vaikhānasa tradition is a distinct stream of Vaiṣṇava temple worship that presents itself as Vedic rather than Tantric. Its priests belong to a hereditary community of Brahmins of the Taittirīya branch of the Black Yajurveda, who follow the Vaikhānasa Gṛhyasūtra and Dharmasūtra. According to tradition the sage Vikhanas received the teaching from Viṣṇu and passed it to four disciples, Atri, Bhṛgu, Marīci and Kāśyapa, to whom the principal ritual texts are attributed:

    1. Atri: the Samūrtārcanādhikaraṇa, on worship with images;

    2. Bhṛgu: several “sections” (adhikaraṇas) on ritual;

    3. Marīci: the Vimānārcanakalpa, on temples and worship;

    4. Kāśyapa: the Jñānakāṇḍa, which includes doctrine.

    The Vaikhānasas do not undergo a separate Tantric initiation: a boy is held to be consecrated to Viṣṇu before birth through the viṣṇubali rite performed during his mother’s pregnancy. Their liturgy gives Vedic mantras a larger place, and their theology distinguishes the “partless” (niṣkala) and “with parts” (sakala) aspects of Viṣṇu and five forms (Viṣṇu, Puruṣa, Satya, Acyuta, Aniruddha) rather than the Pāñcarātra vyūhas. Their texts also give the classification of images as standing, seated or reclining, and as of “yoga”, “enjoyment” (bhoga), “heroic” or “fierce” type, which is widely used in iconographic writing (Table 3.2).

    Table 3.2. Pāñcarātra and Vaikhānasa Compared
    Aspect Pāñcarātra Vaikhānasa
    Self-understanding Revelation of Nārāyaṇa; Tantric Vedic, derived from a Yajurvedic Kalpa tradition
    Priesthood Initiated priests (bhaṭṭācāryas); initiation (dīkṣā) open to devotees Hereditary Vaikhānasa Brahmins only; no separate initiation
    Theology Para, vyūha, vibhava, antaryāmin, arcā Niṣkala and sakala aspects; five forms of Viṣṇu
    Key texts Saṃhitās (Jayākhya, Sātvata, Pauṣkara, Pārameśvara, Īśvara, Pādma) Gṛhya- and Dharmasūtra; texts attributed to Atri, Bhṛgu, Marīci and Kāśyapa
    Examples of temples Śrīraṅgam; Varadarāja, Kāñcīpuram; Melkote Tirumala-Tirupati; many temples of Andhra Pradesh and Tamil Nadu

    Kerala’s great Viṣṇu temples, such as Guruvāyūr and the Padmanābhasvāmi temple in Thiruvananthapuram, follow neither system strictly but the regional Kerala Tantric ritual codified in works such as the Tantrasamuccaya (15th century).

    3.4 Images and Fires: The Vaikhānasa Temple¶

    The Vaikhānasa claim to be Vedic rather than Tantric is not only a matter of self-description; it shapes the whole design of their worship. Gérard Colas has shown that the Vaikhānasa texts conceive temple worship as a transformation of the Vedic domestic and solemn fire rituals that their priests, as Taittirīya Brahmins, also maintained. Where the Vedic sacrificer made offerings into the fire, the Vaikhānasa priest makes offerings to Viṣṇu in the image, and the texts explicitly treat the offering to the image (arcanā) and the offering into the fire (homa) as two forms of the same act (Colas 1996). The image is the place where the “partless” (niṣkala) Viṣṇu, who pervades everything, is made present in a form “with parts” (sakala) that can be worshipped; the priest draws the god into it by meditation and mantra, as fire is kindled from the sticks.

    The five images. A fully equipped Vaikhānasa temple has not one image of the deity but a set of up to five, each with its own function (Table 3.3). The great stone image in the sanctum, the “fixed” (dhruva) image, is too large and too sacred to be moved or bathed every day; the daily services are therefore performed on a small metal “power” image (kautuka), which is linked to the fixed image and receives its power. Other images are used for the bath (snapana), for processions and festivals (utsava) and for the daily distribution of food offerings to the attendant spirits (bali). The Vaikhānasa texts also correlate the five images with the five sacred fires of the Vedic fire ritual — the three fires of the solemn rites and the two further fires of the fivefold scheme — so that the set of images reproduces the set of fires; the details of the correspondence, which vary between texts, are studied by Colas (Colas 1996). Smaller temples may have only some of the five, and the texts allow for this.

    Table 3.3. The Five Images (Pañcabera) of a Vaikhānasa Temple
    Image Function At Tirumala
    Dhruva (fixed) Principal image in the sanctum, usually of stone; source of the power of the others The main image of Veṅkaṭeśvara
    Kautuka (power) Daily worship, bathing rites and the night rest Bhoga Śrīnivāsa
    Utsava (festival) Processions and festivals; the form seen in the streets Malayappa Svāmī
    Snapana (bathing) Special ablutions Ugra Śrīnivāsa
    Bali (offering) Daily food offerings to the guardian deities and spirits Koluvu Śrīnivāsa

    The system has practical as well as theological consequences. Because the fixed image is the permanent seat of the god and its power is extended to the others, the movable images can be taken out, bathed, dressed and carried in procession without disturbing the sanctum; and because the god is understood to be present in all of them at once, a single deity can appear simultaneously as the motionless Lord of the shrine and as the king who holds court, receives accounts and rides out among his people. At Tirumala, the most famous Vaikhānasa temple (Section 3.12), the “court” held each morning before the bali image, at which the day’s calendar and the temple accounts are read out, gives ritual form to this royal conception of the god.

    Consecration. The general sequence of image consecration — the selection of the stone, the carving according to proportional rules, the opening of the eyes, the “sleeping” of the image in water, grain and on a couch, the fire offerings and the installation of the god’s power — is common to Śaiva, Pāñcarātra and Vaikhānasa practice and is described in Section 11.4. The Vaikhānasa versions differ in giving Vedic mantras and fire rituals the leading role, and in their elaborate provision for the relationship between the fixed and the movable images. In Pāñcarātra temples, by contrast, the presence of the god is established by Tantric mantras and by the placing (nyāsa) of the principles (tattvas) on the image, in close parallel to Śaiva practice.

    Rivalry. The two systems competed for temples. Śrīvaiṣṇava hagiography and temple chronicles credit Rāmānuja with introducing Pāñcarātra worship at Śrīraṅgam and elsewhere, and Vaikhānasa authors responded by insisting on the Vedic purity of their own birth-based priesthood against the initiatory credentials of the Pāñcarātrins (Section 3.9). The details of the hagiographic accounts cannot be verified, but the division of South Indian Viṣṇu temples between the two systems, and the litigation it has occasionally produced, are well documented (Colas 1996; Matsubara 1994).

    3.5 The Āḻvārs and the Sacred Geography of Viṣṇu¶

    3.5.1 The Twelve Āḻvārs¶

    The twelve Āḻvārs (“those immersed” in the Lord) were Tamil poet-saints who lived between about the sixth and ninth centuries CE (the Śrīvaiṣṇava tradition assigns them far earlier dates). Their hymns, about four thousand verses, were collected by Nāthamuni (10th century) as the Nālāyira Tivviyappirapantam (“Four-Thousand Divine Compositions”), which the Śrīvaiṣṇava community regards as a Tamil Veda and recites in temples alongside the Sanskrit Veda. The Āḻvārs included a woman, Āṇṭāḷ, and a poet of the low-status Pāṇar community, Tiruppāṇ, whose reception into the Śrīraṅgam sanctum is celebrated in hagiography (Table 3.4).

    Table 3.4. The Twelve Āḻvārs and Their Principal Works

    1 Poykai Mutal Tiruvantāti
    2 Pūtattāḻvār Iraṇṭām Tiruvantāti
    3 Pēyāḻvār Mūṉṟām Tiruvantāti
    4 Tirumaḻicai Nāṉmukaṉ Tiruvantāti; Tiruccanta Viruttam
    5 Nammāḻvār Tiruvāymoḻi (1,102 verses) and three shorter works
    6 Maturakavi Kaṇṇinuṇ Ciṟuttāmpu

    3.5.2 The 108 Divya Deśams¶

    The temples praised in the Āḻvārs’ hymns are known as the 108 divyadeśas (“divine places”). Tradition groups them by the old regions of the Tamil country and beyond (Table 3.5):

    Table 3.5. Traditional Regional Grouping of the 108 Divya Deśams
    Region Count Examples
    Chōḷa country (Kaveri delta) 40 Śrīraṅgam; Kumbakonam
    Pāṇṭiya country (far south) 18 Tirumāliruñcōlai (Alagar Kovil); Srivilliputtur
    Malai country (Kerala) 13 Thiruvananthapuram (Padmanābhasvāmi)
    Middle country 2 Tiruvahīndrapuram
    Toṇṭai country (north Tamil Nadu) 22 Kāñcīpuram (Varadarāja and others)
    Northern country 11 Tirumala; Ahobilam; Ayodhyā; Mathurā; Dvārakā; Badarīnāth; Muktināth (Nepal)
    Celestial 2 Vaikuṇṭha; the Ocean of Milk
    Total 108

    The opening verse of Nammāḻvār’s Tiruvāymoḻi (1.1.1), recited daily in Śrīvaiṣṇava worship, begins by praising the Lord “who possesses the highest good, beyond which there is nothing higher” and who “graciously gave clear understanding, free of confusion”, and ends by urging the poet’s own heart to rise and worship his radiant feet that dispel sorrow.

    3.6 The Sixteen Offerings of Worship¶

    Hindu image worship in general, Vaiṣṇava and Śaiva alike, treats the deity as an honoured guest and offers a sequence of “services” (upacāras). A widely used list of sixteen (ṣoḍaśopacāra) is given below; the exact list varies between texts and traditions (Table 3.6; Bühnemann 1988).

    Table 3.6. A Common List of Sixteen Upacāras
    No. Name Meaning
    1 Āvāhana Invocation of the deity
    2 Āsana Offering a seat
    3 Pādya Water for the feet
    4 Arghya Respectful water offering
    5 Ācamana Water for sipping
    6 Snāna Bath
    7 Vastra Garment
    8 Yajñopavīta Sacred thread

    3.7 The Śrīvaiṣṇava Synthesis¶

    The Śrīvaiṣṇava tradition of South India united three streams: the Vedānta of the Upaniṣads, the Brahmasūtra and the Gītā; the Tamil devotion of the Āḻvārs; and the ritual of the Pāñcarātra temples.

    • Nāthamuni (10th century) is credited with recovering and arranging the Āḻvār hymns and introducing their recitation in temples.

    • Yāmuna (Yāmunācārya, 10th–11th century) wrote the Āgamaprāmāṇya, a defence of the Pāñcarātra’s validity against Mīmāṃsā and other critics, arguing that its revelation by the omniscient Lord makes it as authoritative as the Veda (van Buitenen 1971).

    • Rāmānuja (traditionally 1017–1137; many scholars prefer c. 1077–1157) gave the tradition its Vedāntic theology, Viśiṣṭādvaita (“non-dualism of the qualified”), in his Śrībhāṣya and other works. In commenting on the Brahmasūtra’s section on the Bhāgavata doctrine (2.2.42–45 in Śaṅkara’s numbering), which Śaṅkara read as a refutation of the Pāñcarātra, he argued that the passage in fact upholds it. Hagiography credits him with reorganising temple administration and worship at Śrīraṅgam.

    3.7.1 The Five Daily Observances¶

    The Pāñcarātra divides the devotee’s day into five periods (pañcakāla), each with its observance:

    1. Abhigamana (approach): morning purification, prayer and preparation;

    2. Upādāna (gathering): collecting flowers and materials for worship;

    3. Ijyā (worship): the performance of pūjā;

    4. Svādhyāya (study): recitation and study of scripture;

    5. Yoga (meditation): contemplation of the Lord at night.

    3.7.2 Initiation: The Five Sacraments¶

    Śrīvaiṣṇava initiation, called samāśrayaṇa (“taking refuge”) or pañcasaṃskāra, comprises five elements: branding of the shoulders with the discus and conch (tāpa), the sectarian forehead mark (puṇḍra), a new devotional name (nāma), the mantra (mantra), and instruction in worship (yāga). It is given to men and women of all castes by an ācārya.

    3.7.3 The Three Mantras¶

    Śrīvaiṣṇava teaching centres on three mantras, the “three secrets” (rahasyatraya):

    1. The tirumantra or eight-syllable mantra, oṃ namo nārāyaṇāya, interpreted as expressing the soul’s essential dependence on and service to Nārāyaṇa;

    2. The dvaya (“pair”), śrīman nārāyaṇa caraṇau śaraṇaṃ prapadye | śrīmate nārāyaṇāya namaḥ — “I take refuge at the feet of Nārāyaṇa with Śrī; homage to Nārāyaṇa with Śrī”, expressing the means and the goal;

    3. The caramaśloka (“final verse”), Bhagavad Gītā 18.66.

    Bhagavad Gītā 18.66

    sarvadharmān parityajya mām ekaṃ śaraṇaṃ vraja |
    ahaṃ tvā sarvapāpebhyo mokṣayiṣyāmi mā śucaḥ ||

    Abandoning all duties, take refuge in me alone. I shall free you from all sins; do not grieve.

    3.8 Surrender (Prapatti)¶

    The doctrine of prapatti or śaraṇāgati, surrender to the Lord, is the distinctive soteriological contribution of the Śrīvaiṣṇava tradition. Prepared in the Pāñcarātra Saṃhitās and in Rāmānuja’s Śaraṇāgatigadya, it was developed by his successors into an independent means of liberation, open to all, as an alternative to the demanding discipline of bhaktiyoga. The Ahirbudhnya Saṃhitā and the Lakṣmītantra describe six components of surrender: the resolve to act in accordance with the Lord’s will (ānukūlyasya saṁkalpaḥ); the abandonment of what is opposed to it (prātikūlyasya varjanam); the confidence that the Lord will protect (rakṣiṣyatīti viśvāsaḥ); the choice of the Lord as one’s protector (goptṛtvavaraṇam); the placing of oneself in his charge (ātmanikṣepaḥ); and the humility of one who knows himself helpless (kārpaṇyam).

    3.8.1 The Role of Śrī¶

    In Śrīvaiṣṇava theology Lakṣmī (Śrī) is inseparable from Nārāyaṇa and acts as the mediator (puruṣakāra) who intercedes for erring souls, softening the Lord’s justice with her compassion. The name of the tradition, “Śrīvaiṣṇava”, and the dvaya mantra both give her a central place.

    3.8.2 Vaṭakalai and Teṉkalai¶

    From the thirteenth and fourteenth centuries the Śrīvaiṣṇavas divided into two schools, whose main differences are summarised in Table 3.7.

    Table 3.7. Vaṭakalai and Teṉkalai Compared
    Issue Vaṭakalai (“northern”) Teṉkalai (“southern”)

    The two schools also differ in social attitudes, sectarian marks and temple privileges, and their rivalry has led to long-running litigation over temple rights.

    3.9 Defending and Regulating Pāñcarātra Practice¶

    The status of the Pāñcarātra remained contested long after Yāmuna. Its priests were sometimes regarded by orthodox Brahmins as of lower standing, because their eligibility rested on Tantric initiation rather than on Vedic birth alone, and the rival Vaikhānasa priests stressed their own exclusively Vedic credentials. In the fourteenth century Vedānta Deśika wrote the Pāñcarātrarakṣā (“Protection of the Pāñcarātra”), which defends the tradition’s orthodoxy and regulates its practice on the basis of the Saṃhitās, including the five daily observances, the initiatory rites and the conduct of worship.

    In the temples, the Saṃhitās prescribe the daily worship of the image in several services, the great festival with its flag-hoisting, processions on the vehicles of the Lord (Garuḍa, the serpent Śeṣa, the elephant and others), the bath and the chariot, and the periodic reconsecration of the temple. They also regulate who may enter which part of the temple and who may touch the image. As in the Śaiva temples, these prescriptions have been the subject of modern litigation, both between Vaṭakalai and Teṉkalai over ritual precedence and recitation, and between temple priests and the state over appointments.

    3.9.1 Initiation and Caste in the Pāñcarātra¶

    Several Saṃhitās allow initiation of members of all four classes, and the Śrīvaiṣṇava tradition prides itself on teachers of low birth: the Āḻvār Nammāḻvār was of a cultivator caste, and hagiography tells how Rāmānuja proclaimed the eight-syllable mantra from the temple tower at Tirukkoṭṭiyūr so that all might hear it. At the same time, the Saṃhitās often prescribe different mantras and ritual roles according to caste, and temple priesthood remained restricted to Brahmins (Matsubara 1994).

    3.10 Cosmogony¶

    The Pāñcarātra describes creation as a real, divinely willed emanation, not an illusion. It distinguishes a “pure creation” (śuddhasṛṣṭi), consisting of the vyūhas and the eternal heaven (Vaikuṇṭha) made of a pure, non-material substance (śuddhasattva), from the “impure creation” of matter (prakṛti) and time, which follows a Sāṃkhya-like sequence of principles. Time (kāla) is treated as a real instrument of the Lord, operating in the material world but not in the eternal realm.

    3.11 Iconography¶

    The Vaiṣṇava Āgamas contain detailed iconographic prescriptions (mūrtilakṣaṇa) (Smith 1969; Rao 1914–1916). Two features are especially characteristic:

    • Posture and type: Vaikhānasa and śilpa texts classify images of Viṣṇu as standing (sthānaka), seated (āsana) or reclining (śayana), and each as of the yoga, bhoga, vīra or ābhicārika type. The reclining Raṅganātha on the serpent Śeṣa at Śrīraṅgam and the standing Veṅkaṭeśvara at Tirumala are famous examples.

    • The twenty-four forms (caturviṃśatimūrti): the twenty-four ways of distributing conch, discus, mace and lotus among four hands (\(4! = 24\)) are each given a name (Keśava, Nārāyaṇa, Mādhava, Govinda and so on); different texts assign the names differently.

    3.12 Śrīraṅgam and Tirumala¶

    The Raṅganātha temple at Śrīraṅgam, on an island in the Kāverī near Tiruchirappalli, is often described as the largest functioning Hindu temple complex. Its seven concentric enclosures (prākāras) contain streets and houses as well as shrines. Worship follows the Pārameśvara Saṃhitā; the Lord is awakened with Toṇṭaraṭippoṭi Āḻvār’s Tiruppaḷḷiyeḻucci, and the temple maintains both Sanskrit Vedic recitation and the dramatised Tamil recitation of the Āḻvār hymns by the araiyars.

    The Veṅkaṭeśvara temple at Tirumala above Tirupati, by contrast, follows the Vaikhānasa Āgama and has hereditary Vaikhānasa priests. It is among the most visited and wealthiest religious institutions in the world. The Lord is awakened each morning with the Sanskrit Veṅkaṭeśa Suprabhātam composed by Prativādi Bhayaṅkara Aṇṇaṉ (15th century), and a nine-day brahmotsava is celebrated annually.

    3.13 Vaiṣṇava Temples, Bronzes and Art¶

    The Vaiṣṇava Āgamas, like their Śaiva counterparts, prescribe the temple plan, the flag-staff (dhvajastambha) bearing Garuḍa before the sanctum, the offering platform (balipīṭha) on the main axis, and subsidiary shrines for Śrī, Bhūdevī, the Āḻvārs and the ācāryas. South Indian bronze processional images (utsavamūrtis), cast by the lost-wax process by hereditary sculptors (sthapatis), are among the great achievements of Indian art.

    Each South Indian dynasty left its mark on Vaiṣṇava art. The Pallavas (7th–9th centuries) carved the Varāha and Trivikrama panels of Māmallapuram and built the Vaikuṇṭhaperumāḷ temple at Kāñcīpuram; the Chōḷa period (9th–13th centuries) was the classic age of bronze casting and of the expansion of Śrīraṅgam and other shrines; the Hoysaḷas (12th–14th centuries) built the temples of Belur and Halebidu and patronised Rāmānuja’s tradition at Melkote; and under Vijayanagara and the Nāyakas (14th–18th centuries) the great temples acquired their towering gopuras, pillared halls, further enclosures and painted ceilings.

    3.14 Mantra Manuals of Kṛṣṇa Worship¶

    Outside the temple Saṃhitās, Tantric methods also shaped the personal worship of Kṛṣṇa in northern and eastern India. From about the fifteenth century a group of ritual manuals taught the worship of Kṛṣṇa, above all as the cowherd Gopāla of Vṛndāvana, by the same means that Śaiva and Śākta manuals used for their deities: initiation by a guru, the conferral of a mantra, the purification of the elements of the body, the placing of mantras on the limbs (nyāsa), the visualisation of the deity in his paradise, worship with the sixteen offerings and recitation of the mantra.

    The most influential of these manuals was the Kramadīpikā of Keśava, whom tradition identifies with Keśava Kāśmīrī, a teacher of the Nimbārka school, probably of the fifteenth century. It sets out, in a compact series of chapters, the mantras of Kṛṣṇa and the procedures for their worship, and it circulated widely. Texts of this kind made a Vaiṣṇava “mantraśāstra” available to devotees who had no connection with the temple priesthoods of the South.

    The Gauḍīya Vaiṣṇavas of Bengal and Vṛndāvana, followers of Caitanya, gave this tradition its most elaborate form in the Haribhaktivilāsa, a ritual code in twenty chapters composed in Vṛndāvana about 1540. Its authorship is disputed between the two Gosvāmīs Sanātana and Gopāla Bhaṭṭa; its first critical edition, by Måns Broo, attributes it to Sanātana. The work is largely a compilation: it quotes more than two hundred earlier texts, but Broo has shown that most of these quotations were taken from about twenty works of the same kind, among them Tantric manuals and Purāṇas. Its first five chapters treat the qualities of guru and disciple, the choice of mantras, initiation and the preparations for worship (Broo 2023). The Haribhaktivilāsa thus gave a devotional movement that stressed the singing of the divine name a fully Tantric framework of initiation and daily worship, and it remains the normative ritual handbook of the Gauḍīya tradition. Its emphasis on the guru, who confers the mantra and on whom the disciple’s progress depends, is shared with the Śaiva and Śākta traditions (Section 1.6); its exclusion of transgressive rites shows how far a Tantric ritual form could be separated from the Kaula content often associated with the word.

    3.15 Tantric Vaiṣṇavism in Bengal: Sahajiyās and Bāuls¶

    The Pāñcarātra and the Vaikhānasa are Vaiṣṇavisms of the temple. Eastern India also produced an esoteric and explicitly Tantric Vaiṣṇavism outside the temple, in which the love of Rādhā and Kṛṣṇa became the model for a discipline of the body. Its antecedents include the songs of the Buddhist siddhas (Chapter 10), with their ideal of the “innate” (sahaja); its theology was shaped by the devotional movement of Caitanya (1486–1533) and by his followers, the Gosvāmīs of Vṛndāvana, who analysed devotion in the categories of aesthetic savour (rasa) (Dimock 1966).

    The Vaiṣṇava Sahajiyās, whose Bengali texts are mostly later than Caitanya, taught that Kṛṣṇa and Rādhā are present in every man and woman as their essential form (svarūpa) beneath the physical form (rūpa), and that the divine love of the pair can be realised by a man and a woman through a ritual of union learned from a guru and controlled by breath and the retention of seed. They took the love of a woman who is not one’s wife (parakīyā), like the cowherd women’s love for Kṛṣṇa, as the model of a passion indifferent to social convention, and they claimed as their own the poet Caṇḍīdāsa, whose legendary love for the washerwoman Rāmī became emblematic. Edward C. Dimock’s study remains the standard account (Dimock 1966). Orthodox Gauḍīya Vaiṣṇavas rejected this reading: for them the love of Rādhā and Kṛṣṇa is to be entered through devotion and contemplation, not enacted.

    The Bāuls, wandering singers of Bengal who may be of Hindu or Muslim background (the latter often called fakirs), reject temple, image and scripture; their songs seek the “man of the heart” (maner mānuṣ) within the body and mock caste, ritual and sectarian division. The most celebrated, Lālan Fakir (d. 1890), is revered in both Bangladesh and West Bengal, and Rabindranath Tagore admired and published Bāul songs. Behind the songs lies an esoteric practice, transmitted by gurus to initiates, that works with the body and its fluids in ways recalling both the Sahajiyās and older Tantric and Nātha yoga (Openshaw 2002). Related communities, such as the Kartābhajās of colonial Bengal, combined devotional egalitarianism with secrecy and esoteric ritual (Urban 2001). In 2005 UNESCO proclaimed the Bāul songs a Masterpiece of the Oral and Intangible Heritage of Humanity. These traditions show that Tantric ideas of the body and of transgressive love were not confined to Śaiva and Śākta milieus.
