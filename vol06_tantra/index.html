    Preface¶

    This is Volume 6 of the series Bhāratīya Jñāna Paramparā: Tantra–Āgama: Tantric and Āgamic Traditions. It surveys the scriptures, doctrines and practices of the Śaiva, Vaiṣṇava and Śākta Āgamas and Tantras, the Śrīvidyā and Kashmiri Śaiva traditions, the theory and practice of mantra, yantra and ritual, Buddhist and Jain Tantra, the temple religion that the Āgamas still govern in South India, the patronage of Tantric Śaivism by the kings of the early medieval “Śaiva Age”, and the regional Tantric traditions of Kerala, Bengal, Assam, Odisha, Nepal, western India and the Tamil country, together with the Nātha and Siddha orders that moved between them. It also considers the lesser-known Tantras of healing and exorcism, the roles of women as practitioners, partners and gurus, and, in its last chapter, the ethics of the traditions and the history of how “Tantra” has been condemned, defended and studied in colonial India and the modern West.

    The word “Tantra” has attracted both romantic fantasy and hostile caricature. This volume tries to avoid both. It follows the findings of historical and philological scholarship — that the surviving Tantric scriptures date from about the fifth century CE onwards, that their traditions are many rather than one, and that they include both sublime philosophy and practices of power and transgression — while presenting the traditions’ own self-understanding with respect. Traditional dates and attributions are reported as such; quotations from Sanskrit texts are given only where their wording and source could be checked, and many unattested verses found in earlier drafts have been removed. Questions of caste, gender and access, which the traditions themselves debated, are discussed openly.

    The volume is an introduction, not a substitute for the critical editions and specialist studies listed in the bibliography, to which readers are referred for further study. Every primary-text quotation should be checked against a critical edition before citation.

    Nikhil Kumar Rajput
    Ramanujan College, University of Delhi
    Guru Pūrṇimā, 2026

    A Note on Transliteration and Pronunciation¶

    Sanskrit, Pali and Prakrit words are given in the International Alphabet of Sanskrit Transliteration (IAST). A macron marks a long vowel: ā is pronounced as in “father”, ī as in “machine”, ū as in “rule”, while short a is like the u of “but”. The vowels e and o are always long, ai is pronounced as in “aisle” and au as in “how”. The vowel ṛ is a syllabic r, close to the ri of “rig”. Consonants written with a dot below (ṭ, ḍ, ṇ, ṣ) are retroflex, pronounced with the tip of the tongue turned back; ś and ṣ both sound roughly like English sh; c is always the ch of “church”; and an h after a consonant marks aspiration, so that th is the t of “hothouse” and ph the p of “uphill”, never English th or f. Ṃ marks nasalisation of the preceding vowel, ḥ a light echo of it, and ṅ and ñ are the nasals of “sing” and “canyon”.

    Tamil words follow the transliteration of the Tamil Lexicon of the University of Madras, which distinguishes short e and o from long ē and ō and writes the distinctively Tamil sounds as ḻ (as in Āḻvār), ṟ and ṉ. Tibetan, Chinese and Japanese terms are given in simplified or standard romanisations without diacritics. Modern place names (Thanjavur, Bhubaneswar, Guwahati) and the names of modern persons are spelled in their customary English forms, and Sanskrit titles of texts are printed in italics.
