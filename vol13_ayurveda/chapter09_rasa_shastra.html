    Chapter 9: Rasaśāstra: Mercury, Metals and Medicine¶

    yogād api viṣaṃ tīkṣṇam uttamaṃ bheṣajaṃ bhavet
    bheṣajaṃ cāpi duryuktaṃ tīkṣṇaṃ saṃpadyate viṣam
    By right use even a sharp poison may become an excellent medicine;
    and a medicine wrongly used becomes a sharp poison.

    — Caraka Saṃhitā, Sūtrasthāna 1.126

    Health and Safety Note: read before this chapter

    This chapter is a history. It gives no dose, recipe, method of preparation, “purification” procedure, firing schedule or vehicle for any preparation, and nothing in it should be used to prepare or take one. Mercury, lead, arsenic, copper and the other metals and minerals discussed are toxic. “Purification” and “incineration” change their chemical form but do not make them safe, and the traditional tests cannot detect toxicity. Preparations containing them—whether called bhasma, rasa, sindūra, parpaṭī, vaṭī or anything else—can cause serious and sometimes permanent harm to the nervous system, kidneys, liver and blood, and are especially dangerous in pregnancy and for children; surveys have found such metals in about one Ayurvedic product in five (Section 9.5). Anyone who has taken them and has abdominal pain, anaemia, fatigue, numbness or a change in behaviour should see a doctor and say what they took; blood tests for lead and mercury are available.

    9.1 Alchemy and Medicine¶

    In medieval Sanskrit, rasa—which in the older medical texts means “taste” or “nutrient fluid”—came also to mean mercury, and “the science of mercury” (rasaśāstra or rasavidyā) became the name of a body of knowledge that combined alchemy, metallurgy, pharmacy and religious practice. Its practitioners sought two kinds of transformation: the transmutation of base metals into gold (lohavedha or lohavāda) and the transformation of the human body into an immortal, perfected body (dehavedha or dehavāda). Mercury, the liquid metal that “eats” other metals and can be “killed” and revived, was the agent of both. In the religious imagination of the tradition it was the semen of Śiva, and sulphur the menstrual blood of the goddess; their union in the alchemist’s vessel reproduced the union of the divine couple. David Gordon White’s The Alchemical Body is the standard study of these “Siddha” alchemical traditions and their links with Tantra and Haṭha Yoga (White 1996).

    Over time the medical side of the tradition became dominant. By the later medieval period, metals, minerals and mercury compounds prepared according to the rules of rasaśāstra had become a regular part of Āyurvedic practice, and many of the formulae used today contain them. This chapter outlines the history of the tradition and its main ideas, and then explains why its products raise serious safety concerns. The history of Indian alchemy and metallurgy is treated more fully in Volume 14 of this series, Rasāyana: Chemistry and Metallurgy.

    9.2 Historical Development¶

    The early compendia make little use of metals in internal medicine. Caraka and Suśruta prescribe preparations of iron, and gold occurs in rites for the newborn, but their remedies are overwhelmingly vegetable and animal. Mineral substances such as salts, alkalis, mineral pitch (śilājatu) and arsenic sulphides occur, the latter mainly in external applications.

    Iron had a special place. Caraka’s chapter on rejuvenation describes preparations in which thin sheets of iron were heated, quenched in decoctions and ground, and he recommends iron in pallor and as a rejuvenating drug; Suśruta and Vāgbhaṭa also prescribe it. These preparations are the ancestors of the iron “ashes” (lauha bhasma) of the later pharmacy. Gold was given to newborns in the birth rite, and gold and silver were used in eye preparations and collyria. But the systematic internal use of mercury, lead, arsenic and many minerals belongs to the later period.

    A distinct alchemical literature appears in Sanskrit from about the tenth century CE, within Śaiva and Siddha religious traditions, and the internal use of mercury and of incinerated metals spreads into medical works from about the same period. The Rasahṛdayatantra of Govinda Bhagavat (perhaps 10th–11th century) and the Rasārṇava (c. 11th–12th century) are among the earliest surviving alchemical works; later centuries produced a large literature that increasingly emphasised medicine (Table 9.1).

    9.2.1 Nāgārjuna and the “Alchemical Ghost”¶

    Popular histories often credit Indian alchemy to Nāgārjuna, the great Buddhist philosopher of the second or third century CE, and cite a Rasaratnākara by him. Dominik Wujastyk has shown that this text is an “alchemical ghost”: the work usually cited under that name is the Rasendramaṅgala attributed to a Nāgārjuna, which is a much later work, while the Rasaratnākara proper is by Nityanātha (Wujastyk 1984). The several Nāgārjunas of Indian tradition—philosopher, alchemist, redactor of Suśruta, author of a Rasavaiśeṣikasūtra on pharmacological “taste” (which is not an alchemical work)—should be kept apart unless there is evidence that they are the same.

    9.2.2 Alchemy, Tantra and Yoga¶

    White has shown that Indian alchemy was closely bound up with the Tantric and yogic traditions of the Siddhas and Nāths, who were also the founders of Haṭha Yoga (White 1996). In their teaching, the transformation of mercury in the alchemist’s vessel, the transformation of the yogin’s body by breath control and the raising of internal energies, and the ritual union of Śiva and the goddess were parallel processes, each of which could lead to a perfected, immortal body (divyadeha). Legends of alchemists who turned iron to gold, or who lived for centuries, cluster around holy mountains such as Śrīśailam in Andhra Pradesh. The alchemical texts are therefore religious works as well as technical ones: they prescribe initiations, mantras, the worship of the rasaliṅga (a liṅga made of “bound” mercury), and the ritual purity of the laboratory.

    9.2.3 Mercury in Other Medical Traditions¶

    India was not alone in using mercury and other metals in medicine, and the comparison is sobering. Chinese alchemists sought elixirs of immortality containing mercury, arsenic and lead, and the official histories record that several emperors of the Tang dynasty died, apparently of elixir poisoning. Islamic alchemists, such as those of the Jābirian corpus, developed laboratory chemistry and some mercurial medicines. In sixteenth-century Europe, Paracelsus and his followers championed chemical medicines, and mercury became the standard treatment for syphilis for some four hundred years, despite its well-known toxicity; “a night with Venus, a lifetime with Mercury” was the grim proverb. Indian physicians, too, adopted mercury for syphilis in the sixteenth century (Chapter 5). Mercury, arsenic and antimony were removed from Western pharmacopoeias in the twentieth century, as better and safer treatments became available and their toxicity was understood. Tibetan medicine, by contrast, still prepares a processed mercury sulphide for its most prestigious pills (Chapter 11).

    9.3 The Materials¶

    The Rasaratnasamuccaya established a classification of mineral substances that later works followed. Its main groups are the “great rasas” (mahārasa), the “lesser rasas” (uparasa), the “common rasas” (sādhāraṇarasa), metals (loha or dhātu), gems (ratna) and poisons (viṣa). Several of the modern identifications are uncertain or disputed. Table 9.2 lists some members of each group, with the main toxic element involved where there is one.

    9.4 The Processes in Outline¶

    The operations, apparatus and laboratory practice of the alchemists are described in detail, as history, in Volume 14 of this series, Rasāyana. A short summary of concepts is enough here; nothing in it is a description of a procedure.

    The alchemical works describe eighteen “refinements” (saṃskāra) through which mercury was to pass, from steaming and grinding through “swooning”, “reviving” and “binding” to the “digestion” of other metals, transmutation and, finally, use in the body; the first eight were treated as preparatory and medical, the rest as transmutational alchemy. Metals and minerals were to be “purified” (śodhana) by repeated heating and quenching in various liquids, and then “killed” (māraṇa) by repeated firing with plant juices and other substances until they became a fine powder called bhasma, “ash”. Mercury was usually “bound” with sulphur as black or red mercuric sulphide, and sublimed preparations such as rasasindūra and makaradhvaja (which includes gold) became among the most esteemed medicines of the later tradition. Empirical tests—that the ash should not glitter, should lodge in the lines of the fingers, should float on water and should not revert to metal—were used to decide whether the process was complete. The alchemists worked in a consecrated laboratory with a characteristic range of crucibles, furnaces, sand-baths and distillation and sublimation apparatus; their production of metallic zinc by downward distillation, attested archaeologically at Zawar in Rajasthan, is discussed in Volume 14.

    These processes are of real interest to the historian of chemistry: they show long experience of the behaviour of metals and their compounds under heat and with different reagents. But the tradition’s claim that śodhana and māraṇa remove toxicity rests on its own theory, not on toxicological evidence. They convert metals into oxides, sulphides and other compounds whose toxicity varies; lead and arsenic remain lead and arsenic. The classical tests measure fineness and appearance, not safety.

    9.5 Rasaśāstra Today¶

    The tradition did not die out. Families of physicians known for their mercurial preparations (rasavaidyas) continued to practise into the twentieth century. Rasaśāstra is now taught as a subject (together with pharmaceutics, as rasaśāstra and bhaiṣajyakalpanā) in Indian Āyurvedic colleges, and “herbo-mineral” products are manufactured on a large scale under government licence; they are among the most expensive Ayurvedic medicines on the market, and their prestige rests on the tradition’s own claims about processing. Supporters argue that properly prepared bhasmas are safe because of their processing, and point to analytical studies showing that some consist of very fine particles, in some cases in the nanometre range, and that mercury in rasasindūra is largely in the form of mercuric sulphide, which is less readily absorbed than many other mercury compounds. Some have gone further and called bhasmas an “ancient nanomedicine”.

    Animal experiments with some bhasmas have been published, mostly by Indian laboratories, and some report little toxicity at the doses tested. Such studies are of limited value: they are often short, use small numbers of animals, test a single batch of a product, and do not measure the accumulation of metals in the body over months or years of use.

    These arguments do not establish safety. Particle size says nothing about toxicity, except that very fine particles may be absorbed more easily. The toxicity of mercury sulphide preparations taken repeatedly is not well studied. Lead, arsenic and copper preparations have no safe level of use as medicines. Most importantly, the products actually sold are often contaminated or mislabelled, and their content varies from batch to batch.

    The evidence from outside the tradition is consistent. In a study published in 2004, Robert Saper and colleagues bought seventy Ayurvedic products in shops in the Boston area and found that one in five contained lead, mercury or arsenic, at levels that, if the products were taken as recommended, could exceed regulatory safety limits (Saper et al. 2004). A larger study of products bought over the internet and made in both India and the United States, published in 2008, found detectable metals in about one in five products, and more often in rasaśāstra products than in others (Saper et al. 2008). Public health agencies have repeatedly reported lead poisoning in people taking Ayurvedic medicines, including pregnant women and children (Centers for Disease Control and Prevention 2004).

    9.6 Alchemy and the History of Indian Science¶

    The modern study of Indian alchemy began with the chemist Prafulla Chandra Ray, whose History of Hindu Chemistry (Ray 1902–1909) presented the Sanskrit texts as evidence that India had possessed a chemistry of its own, and later historians have emphasised their religious and tantric setting and corrected their dates (White 1996; Wujastyk 1984). That history, and the place of alchemy in the history of Indian chemistry and metallurgy, are the subject of Volume 14, Rasāyana.

    9.7 Significance¶

    The alchemical tradition of medieval India is a major chapter in the history of science and religion. It developed apparatus and techniques for distillation, sublimation and calcination; it built up empirical knowledge of the behaviour of metals, minerals and salts; it produced metallic zinc; and it expressed in its own idiom a striking vision of the human body as something that could be perfected. Its integration into medicine, however, introduced into the pharmacopoeia some of the most toxic substances known. To recognise both of these facts is to take the tradition seriously.

    Summary

    Rasaśāstra grew from about the tenth century within Śaiva and Siddha religious traditions, as an alchemy that sought both gold and an immortal body, and over time became a pharmacy whose metallic preparations entered everyday Āyurvedic practice. Its laboratory knowledge, including the distillation of zinc, belongs to the history of chemistry, and its attribution to the philosopher Nāgārjuna rests on confusion. Its claim that processing removes the toxicity of metals is not supported by evidence, and its products are a documented cause of poisoning.

    Further Reading¶

    White’s The Alchemical Body is the essential study (White 1996); Ray’s History of Hindu Chemistry remains valuable for its texts and translations (Ray 1902–1909); on the “alchemical ghost” see Wujastyk (Wujastyk 1984).
