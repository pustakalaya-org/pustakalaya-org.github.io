    Preface¶

    This is Volume 13 of the series Bhāratīya Jñāna Paramparā: Āyurveda: Medicine and Healing Traditions. It introduces the classical medical literature of India in Sanskrit—above all the compendia of Caraka, Suśruta and Vāgbhaṭa—together with its theory of the body, its materia medica, its anatomy, its therapeutic procedures and clinical branches (including women’s and children’s medicine and the disorders of the mind), the transmission of its texts in manuscript, translation and print, its later developments in diagnosis and in the mercurial and metallic pharmacy of rasaśāstra, and its encounter with colonial and modern medicine, through which it became a modern profession. A further chapter introduces the other learned medical traditions practised in India—Siddha, Unani and Sowa-Rigpa—and the local and tribal health traditions that have always existed alongside them.

    The volume is an edited and corrected version of an earlier draft. In revising it, three principles were followed. First, the history: dates are given as the range accepted in current scholarship (principally the work of G. Jan Meulenbeld and Dominik Wujastyk), and traditional dates and attributions are labelled as such. The great compendia are composite works, revised and supplemented over several centuries, and are treated as such. Second, the texts: every verse quoted has been checked, and passages that could not be confirmed as genuine, with a correct reference, were removed rather than repaired. Third, safety. This book is a history of a medical tradition. It is not a guide to treatment, and it deliberately gives no doses, recipes or instructions for any remedy. Many classical preparations contain mercury, lead, arsenic and other toxic substances, and modern studies have found dangerous levels of heavy metals in Ayurvedic products on sale in India and abroad. Where the chapters describe such preparations, purification procedures, pregnancy and childbirth, poisoning or therapeutic claims, a short boxed Health and Safety Note is given; the full statement on metallic preparations opens Chapter 9, and the practical warnings are gathered in one place in Section 12.7. Claims that a remedy cures a disease are reported as what the texts say, not as established fact. Readers who are ill should consult a registered medical practitioner.

    References to the classical texts use the following abbreviations: Ca. = Caraka Saṃhitā; Su. = Suśruta Saṃhitā; AH = Aṣṭāṅgahṛdaya; and for the sections, Sū. = Sūtrasthāna, Ni. = Nidānasthāna, Vi. = Vimānasthāna, Śā. = Śārīrasthāna, In. = Indriyasthāna, Ci. = Cikitsāsthāna, Ka. = Kalpasthāna, Si. = Siddhisthāna, Utt. = Uttaratantra or Uttarasthāna. Thus “Ca.Sū. 1.41” is Caraka Saṃhitā, Sūtrasthāna, chapter 1, verse 41. Numbering follows the Nirṇaya Sāgar editions of Jādavjī Trikamjī Ācārya; other editions may differ by a verse or two. Sanskrit, Pali and Prakrit words are given in IAST; Tamil, Arabic and Persian terms in standard scholarly transliteration, and Tibetan terms in the Wylie system (most Tibetan personal names in a common phonetic form).

    No survey of this size can be exhaustive. The select bibliography lists the editions, translations and studies on which the volume relies, and is meant as a guide to further reading. A glossary of technical terms, a short chronology, guides to the principal medical works, plants and disease names, and an index are provided at the end. Before scholarly use, every quotation should be checked against a critical edition.

    Nikhil Kumar Rajput
    Ramanujan College, University of Delhi
