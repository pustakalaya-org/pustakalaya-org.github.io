    Chapter 11: Siddha, Unani, Sowa-Rigpa and Local Health Traditions¶

    mikiṉum kuṟaiyiṉum nōyceyyum nūlōr
    vaḷimutalā eṇṇiya mūṉṟu
    The three that the authors of the treatises count,
    beginning with wind, cause disease
    when they are in excess or in deficiency.

    — Tirukkuṟaḷ 941

    11.1 A Plural Medical Landscape¶

    Sanskrit Āyurveda was never the only form of learned medicine in India, still less the only form of healing. Alongside it, and in constant exchange with it, there developed a Tamil medical tradition, now called Siddha; Greco-Arabic medicine, known in India as Unani (Yūnānī, “Greek”); and, in the Himalayan regions, the Tibetan medical tradition called Sowa-Rigpa. Beneath and around all of these lay the healing knowledge of households, midwives, bonesetters, snakebite healers and the herbalists of forest and hill communities. The Government of India now recognises Ayurveda, Unani, Siddha and Sowa-Rigpa, together with Yoga, Naturopathy and Homoeopathy, under the Ministry of AYUSH (Section 12.3). This chapter introduces Siddha, Unani and Sowa-Rigpa and the local traditions on their own terms. Each deserves fuller treatment than is possible here.

    11.2 Siddha Medicine¶

    11.2.1 The Siddhars and the Literature¶

    Siddha medicine (Tamil cittamaruttuvam) is the learned medical tradition of the Tamil-speaking south. It takes its name from the cittar (Sanskrit siddha), “perfected ones”: yogis, alchemists and poets who were believed to have attained bodily immortality and supernatural powers, and to whom a large body of Tamil verse on medicine, alchemy, yoga and religion is attributed. Tradition counts eighteen great Siddhars, though the lists differ from source to source. Agastya (Tamil Akattiyar), the sage who in Sanskrit and Tamil legend brought culture to the south, is regarded as the founder of the medical tradition; Bhogar (Pōkar), Tirumūlar, Yūki, Tēraiyar, Korakkar and Pulippāṇi are among the other names associated with medical works.

    The dates of this literature are difficult to establish. The Tirumantiram attributed to Tirumūlar, an important early Tamil text on yoga and Śaiva doctrine (perhaps of the later first millennium CE), contains verses on the body and its preservation but is not a medical treatise. Most of the medical poems attributed to the Siddhars are written in a language and style that suggest composition in the second millennium, many of them probably between the fifteenth and the nineteenth centuries; some are known only from nineteenth- and twentieth-century printed editions. The attribution of works of several thousand verses to individual Siddhars—the “Agastya 12,000” or “Bhogar 7,000”—is traditional. Richard Weiss has shown how, in the nineteenth and twentieth centuries, Tamil physicians and publishers shaped this literature into a self-conscious “Siddha system” and presented it as an ancient Tamil science distinct from, and older than, Sanskrit Āyurveda, as part of the wider Tamil revival (Weiss 2009). Kamil Zvelebil’s study of the Siddha poets remains a useful introduction to the wider tradition (Zvelebil 1996).

    11.2.2 Early Tamil Evidence¶

    The earliest Tamil evidence for a learned medicine is indirect. The anthologies of early Tamil poetry mention healing herbs and the dressing of wounds only in passing. The Tirukkuṟaḷ, the collection of 1,330 couplets on virtue, wealth and love (c. 5th century CE; the date is disputed), is more explicit. One of its chapters (ch. 95, Maruntu, “Medicine”) consists of ten couplets on health, most of them about moderation in eating and eating only when the previous meal has been digested. Its first couplet (Kuṟaḷ 941), quoted at the head of this chapter, says that the three beginning with wind, as the authors of the treatises (nūlōr) count them, cause disease when they are in excess or in deficiency. Another (Kuṟaḷ 949) tells the learned physician to weigh the patient’s condition, the extent of the disease and the time before he acts. The last (Kuṟaḷ 950) divides medicine into four parts: the patient, the healer, the remedy and the attendant at the bedside. These correspond to the three doṣas and to the “four feet” of treatment of the Caraka Saṃhitā (Ca.Sū. 9; Chapter 3).

    The couplets show that by the middle of the first millennium a humoral medicine, which the poet attributes to the authors of books, was known in the Tamil country in terms that match the Sanskrit compendia. This evidence does not settle the modern argument in either direction. It does not show an independent Tamil humoral theory older than the Sanskrit, and it does not show that Tamil medicine was a mere copy, since it tells us nothing about what Tamil physicians themselves wrote.

    11.2.3 The Medical Corpus¶

    The medical works attributed to the Siddhars are almost all in verse. They are usually known by the name of their supposed author and the number of their verses. They range from general works on therapeutics (vaittiyam) to treatises on the pulse, on the examination of urine, on the preparation of metals and minerals, and on the properties of drugs; collections of single formulae circulated in great numbers. A work on diagnosis and the classification of diseases attributed to Yūki Muṉivar (the Yūki Vaittiya Cintāmaṇi) is regarded as the source of the traditional Siddha count of 4,448 diseases, a figure that modern textbooks still repeat.

    Much of this verse is deliberately obscure. The poets use a coded technical vocabulary (paripāṣai), in which drugs and processes are named by allusive synonyms, so that a text could be understood only with a teacher’s key. The code kept knowledge within lineages and gave the teacher authority. It also makes many drug identifications uncertain today.

    Manuscripts survive in family collections, in the Government Oriental Manuscripts Library in Chennai and in the Sarasvati Mahal Library at Thanjavur. There the Maratha ruler Serfoji II (r. 1798–1832) had medical works copied and the prescriptions of his court physicians collected. Most Siddha texts reached their present form only when they were printed, from the late nineteenth century onwards. Their editors were often practitioners who rearranged, supplemented and sometimes rewrote them; much of Weiss’s study is an account of this process (Weiss 2009). Modern college teaching relies on twentieth-century textbooks that arrange the material under headings borrowed partly from Western medicine, such as materia medica (kuṇapāṭam), physiology and pathology.

    11.2.4 Theory¶

    Siddha theory shares much with Āyurveda: the five elements; three humours (together mukkuṟṟam, “the three faults”); seven bodily constituents; the urges that must not be suppressed (fourteen in the Siddha lists); and the importance of diet, season and constitution. It also has distinctive features. The human being is described as composed of ninety-six principles (tattuvam), a scheme derived from Śaiva Siddhānta philosophy that includes the elements, senses and organs of action, the inner faculties, the vital airs and channels, the sheaths of the self, the bodily “supports” (ātāram), the humours, the qualities, the passions and the states of consciousness. The usual modern list is summarised in Table 11.1.

    The correspondence between the body and the universe—summed up in the Tamil phrase aṇṭattil uḷḷatu piṇṭattilum uṇṭu, “what is in the macrocosm is in the microcosm”—is a favourite theme of the Siddha poets.

    11.2.5 Humours, Constituents and Tastes¶

    In Tamil the three humours are called vaḷi (wind), aḻal (heat or fire) and aiyam (phlegm), or by the Sanskrit-derived names vātam, pittam and kapam. Siddha writers hold that in a healthy person the three stand in a fixed proportion, which modern textbooks give as one to one-half to one-quarter and which the physician is said to feel in the pulse. Vātam has ten forms, the ten vital airs of the yogic texts (Table 11.1). Pittam and kapam have five each, and these correspond closely to the five forms of pitta and kapha in the Sanskrit compendia (Chapter 2).

    The seven bodily constituents (uṭal kaṭṭukaḷ) are chyle (cāram), blood (ceṉṉīr), flesh (ūṉ), fat (koḻuppu), bone (eṉpu), marrow (mūḷai) and the reproductive fluid (cukkilam in men, curōṇitam in women), the same sequence as the Sanskrit dhātus. There are six tastes (aṟucuvai): sweet, sour, salt, bitter, pungent and astringent. As in Āyurveda, each is formed from two of the elements.

    The differences from Āyurveda lie less in these lists than in their setting. They include the ninety-six principles, the yogic physiology of channels and centres, the alchemical pharmacy described below, and a Tamil technical vocabulary that grew up alongside the Sanskrit one.

    The Siddha poets also took a distinctive attitude to the body. Much Indian religious literature treats the body as impure and an obstacle to liberation. The Siddhars made its preservation a spiritual aim. In a verse of the Tirumantiram that modern Siddha writers often cite (verse 724 in the usual numbering), Tirumūlar says that when the body perishes the life perishes with it, and true knowledge cannot be reached; having learned how to foster the body, he fostered it, and so fostered life. Yoga, breath control, diet and alchemical medicines all served this end. A perfected, undecaying body was the goal of the most ambitious practitioners, and medicine in the ordinary sense was the lower application of the same knowledge.

    11.2.6 Diagnosis¶

    Siddha diagnosis uses an “eightfold examination” (eṇvakait tērvu) of pulse (nāṭi), touch (paricam), tongue (nā), colour (niṟam), speech (moḻi), eyes (viḻi), stool (malam) and urine (mūttiram). Siddha physicians place particular weight on the pulse and on the examination of urine, including the observation of a drop of oil placed on it (neykkuṟi), a test similar to that of the late Sanskrit texts (Chapter 10). Like the Sanskrit pulse and urine tests, these methods have not been shown to be reliable means of diagnosis.

    Reading the pulse is the most prestigious diagnostic skill. The physician feels the radial pulse with three fingers, as in the late Sanskrit texts, reads vātam under the index finger, pittam under the middle finger and kapam under the ring finger, and judges the relation between the three against the healthy proportion. The examination of urine (nīrkkuṟi) considers colour, density, smell, froth and deposit in a sample collected early in the morning, before the oil-drop test is made. Works on the pulse and the urine are attributed to several Siddhars, among them Tēraiyar.

    11.2.7 Three Kinds of Treatment¶

    Siddha writers divide treatment into three classes. “Divine” medicine (tēva maruttuvam) comprises the potent metal and mineral preparations, such as centūram, paṟpam and kaṭṭu; “human” medicine (māṉiṭa maruttuvam) the herbal preparations, such as juices, decoctions, powders and medicated oils; and “demonic” medicine (acura maruttuvam) incision, cautery, leeches, caustics and other surgical measures. The ranking shows the prestige of the alchemical tradition, in which the mineral medicines are the highest form of the art. It also explains why the safety debate about Siddha has centred on those medicines (see the note below).

    11.2.8 Pharmacy and Alchemy¶

    Siddha pharmacy is noted for its extensive use of metals and minerals. Medicines are classified into thirty-two forms for internal use and thirty-two for external use. The internal forms include, besides juices, decoctions, powders, pills, confections and medicated oils, preparations of metals and minerals such as centūram (red calcined preparations), paṟpam (white calcined preparations), kaṭṭu (“bound” preparations of mercury and other substances) and kaḷaṅku. The tradition recognises sixty-four pāṣāṇams, “stones” or mineral poisons, thirty-two natural and thirty-two prepared. Among the latter are vīram (mercuric chloride, corrosive sublimate), pūram (mercurous chloride, calomel) and kauri pāṣāṇam (arsenic trioxide). These are among the most toxic substances known.

    Siddha alchemy also aimed at rejuvenation and immortality. The regimen called kāyakaṟpam (“making the body last an aeon”) combined yogic discipline with special diets and preparations, sometimes taken in seclusion; the legendary muppu, a “universal salt” whose composition was kept secret, was said to be its key. A celebrated legend attributes to Bhogar the image of Murukaṉ at the hill temple of Palani, said to be made of nine pāṣāṇams (navapāṣāṇam); the composition of the image has never been verified.

    Health and Safety Note

    Siddha preparations containing mercury, arsenic, lead and other metals or mineral poisons, including the pāṣāṇams, can cause serious poisoning. The modern hospital use of arsenic trioxide for one form of leukaemia, under specialist monitoring, does not make traditional preparations safe. See Chapter 9 and Section 12.7.

    11.2.9 External Therapies¶

    Siddha practice includes a range of external therapies: medicated oil massage, a form of manipulative massage called tokkaṇam with several distinct techniques (pressing, squeezing, kneading, tapping and others), fomentation with heated bundles of herbs, poultices, medicated baths, fumigation, and the application of caustics, leeches and cautery. The thirty-two external forms of medicine in the Siddha classification include many of these.

    11.2.10 Varmam¶

    Varmam (Sanskrit marman) is the Tamil lore of vital points, shared with the martial art varmakkalai and with the kaḷarippayaṟṟu tradition of Kerala. Modern Siddha texts usually speak of 108 points, of which twelve are “striking” points (paṭu varmam) whose injury may be fatal and ninety-six are “touching” points (toṭu varmam). Practitioners use pressure and manipulation at these points to treat injuries, pain and paralysis and to revive the unconscious. The tradition is old and was transmitted largely orally and within families, often among non-brahmin communities. Its therapeutic claims have not been adequately tested.

    11.2.11 Siddha Today¶

    Siddha medicine is taught in government and private colleges in Tamil Nadu and practised in Tamil Nadu, Kerala, Puducherry, Sri Lanka and the Tamil diaspora. Its practitioners include both formally trained graduates and hereditary physicians, and much Siddha knowledge is still held in family palm-leaf manuscripts, many of them uncatalogued.

    Institutional Siddha is largely a twentieth-century creation. In the Madras Presidency, after the report of the Usman Committee (1923; Chapter 12), a government School of Indian Medicine opened in Madras in the mid-1920s. It taught Āyurveda, Siddha and Unani together, with elements of Western medicine. A separate government college for Siddha was founded at Palayamkottai in 1964. Siddha later acquired its own pharmacopoeia and a National Institute of Siddha at Chennai (opened in 2005). Siddha research was administered jointly with Āyurveda by the Central Council for Research in Ayurveda and Siddha until 2010, when a separate Central Council for Research in Siddha was created. In Sri Lanka, Siddha forms part of the state system of indigenous medicine and is taught at the University of Jaffna.

    Weiss’s study of the modern history of Siddha shows how its identity was shaped by the politics of language and caste in twentieth-century Tamil Nadu (Weiss 2009). Its advocates presented it as the original medicine of the Tamils, older than Sanskrit Āyurveda and free from brahmanical domination; Siddha physicians were often non-brahmins, and the Siddhar poets themselves had denounced caste and ritual. The government recognition of Siddha as a system separate from Āyurveda, in the decades after independence, was the result of this movement. Historically, the boundary between Tamil and Sanskrit medicine was more porous than these claims suggest: Siddha theory shares much with Āyurveda, and Tamil physicians long used Sanskrit texts. But the movement drew attention to a large body of Tamil medical knowledge that the Sanskrit-centred history of Indian medicine had ignored.

    11.3 Unani Medicine¶

    11.3.1 Origins¶

    Unani (ṭibb-i yūnānī, “Greek medicine”) is the Greco-Arabic medical tradition. Its theoretical foundations are those of Hippocrates (c. 460–370 BCE) and Galen (129–c. 216 CE), which were translated into Arabic in Abbasid Baghdad in the eighth to tenth centuries and developed by physicians of the Islamic world writing in Arabic and Persian. Among them were Abū Bakr al-Rāzī (Rhazes, c. 854–925), whose al-Ḥāwī (“The Comprehensive Book”) is a vast collection of clinical notes and excerpts; Ibn Sīnā (Avicenna, 980–1037), whose al-Qānūn fī al-ṭibb (“The Canon of Medicine”) became the standard textbook from Central Asia to Europe; and Abū al-Qāsim al-Zahrāwī (Albucasis, d. c. 1013), whose al-Taṣrīf contains a celebrated illustrated treatise on surgery (Pormann and Savage-Smith 2007). The Islamic medical tradition itself drew on Indian as well as Greek and Persian sources (Chapter 5).

    Greek medicine reached the Islamic world through the translation movement of the eighth to tenth centuries, in which Christian, Jewish and Muslim scholars put most of the Greek medical corpus into Syriac and Arabic. The most prolific translator, Ḥunayn ibn Isḥāq (d. 873), and his circle rendered almost the whole of Galen (Pormann and Savage-Smith 2007). The same milieu took in Indian medicine. The Firdaws al-ḥikma (“Paradise of Wisdom”) of ʿAlī ibn Sahl Rabban al-Ṭabarī, completed about 850, is mainly an account of the Greek system, but it ends with a long summary of Indian medicine drawn from Caraka, Suśruta and other Sanskrit authorities (Ullmann 1978). The Unani that later came to India had therefore already absorbed some Indian learning.

    Its most influential synthesis, Ibn Sīnā’s Canon, is in five books: general principles of physiology, pathology, hygiene and therapy; simple drugs; diseases particular to each organ, from head to foot; diseases not confined to one organ, such as fevers, swellings, wounds and poisons; and compound drugs. This progression, from principles to particular diseases and remedies, became the model for later Unani textbooks in India.

    11.3.2 Unani in India¶

    Physicians trained in this tradition came to India with the Ghaznavids and the Delhi Sultanate from the eleventh and thirteenth centuries, and the tradition flourished under the patronage of the Sultans, the regional Muslim kingdoms and the Mughals. Much of its Indian literature is in Persian. Indian physicians wrote major works, such as the Ṭibb-i Akbarī of Muḥammad Akbar Arzānī (early eighteenth century), and engaged with Āyurveda: the Maʿdan al-Shifāʾ-i Sikandar-Shāhī (1512) of Miyān Bhuwa presented Sanskrit medical learning in Persian, and Unani pharmacy absorbed many Indian drugs. Hindu physicians learned and practised Unani, and Muslim physicians used Āyurvedic remedies.

    Interest in Indian drugs among Islamic scholars long predates the Delhi Sultanate. The polymath al-Bīrūnī, who travelled in India in the early eleventh century with the armies of Maḥmūd of Ghazna and learned Sanskrit, wrote both a celebrated description of India and a work on pharmacy (Kitāb al-ṣaydana) in which he recorded the Indian names of many drugs alongside their Greek, Arabic and Persian names.

    The Persian medical literature of India is very large and still incompletely studied. It includes general compendia, pharmacopoeias, works on particular diseases and on the pulse, translations from Arabic and from Sanskrit, and medical sections in encyclopaedias compiled for rulers. Some of its authors were Hindus who wrote in Persian, the language of administration and high culture, and some works deliberately combined the two traditions, describing Indian drugs in Unani terms or presenting Āyurvedic diagnosis to Persian readers. The court physicians of the Mughals included Persians, Indian Muslims and Hindus, and European travellers such as François Bernier, himself a physician, left accounts of medical practice at the Mughal court.

    Under the Mughals, Indian Unani reached its classical form. Physicians from Iran found employment at the courts of Akbar and his successors and wrote in Persian for Indian patrons. Nūr al-Dīn Muḥammad ʿAbd Allāh Shīrāzī compiled two important works. The first is a dictionary of drug names, the Alfāẓ al-adwiya (1628), dedicated to Shāh Jahān; Francis Gladwin translated it into English in 1793, and it long served European physicians in India. The second is a vast medical compendium, the Ṭibb-i Dārā Shikōhī (also called ʿIlājāt-i Dārā Shikōhī). It was completed in the 1640s and dedicated to Shāh Jahān’s eldest son, the scholar-prince Dārā Shikōh. Both works give Indian drug names alongside Arabic and Persian ones. In the eighteenth century, as Mughal power declined, Delhi physicians such as Arzānī and Ḥakīm Muḥammad Sharīf Khān, from whom the Sharīfī family takes its name, wrote Persian works that remained in use into the twentieth century.

    Seema Alavi shows how, in the nineteenth century, the loss of court patronage pushed Unani families towards new patrons, towards print and towards Urdu. She also shows how Unani came to be identified with Muslim identity in a way it had not been in Mughal times, when Hindu and Muslim physicians practised it alike (Alavi 2008).

    In the colonial period Unani, like Āyurveda, lost official support but retained wide popular following, and in the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries it was reorganised and defended by reformers. The most important was Ḥakīm Muḥammad Ajmal Khān of Delhi (1868–1927), physician, nationalist and educator, who founded the Ayurvedic and Unani Tibbia College in Delhi, opened in 1921, to teach both systems together with modern sciences. Guy Attewell’s study traces how Unani was “refigured” in this period in dialogue with Western medicine and Āyurveda (Attewell 2007). India today has the largest Unani establishment in the world, with colleges, a research council, a National Institute of Unani Medicine at Bengaluru, and a major pharmaceutical industry.

    11.3.3 The Unani Physician in Indian Society¶

    Unani physicians served the courts of Delhi, Agra, Lucknow, Hyderabad and the other Indo-Muslim states, and families of ḥakīms, such as the Sharīfī family of Delhi to which Ajmal Khān belonged, maintained hereditary traditions of learning and practice over many generations. They taught in their homes and in madrasas, wrote in Persian and later in Urdu, and served patients of all communities. In the princely state of Hyderabad, Unani received official support and was taught in government institutions well before independence. The Hamdard company, founded in Delhi in 1906 by Ḥakīm Ḥāfiẓ ʿAbd al-Majīd, became the largest manufacturer of Unani medicines, and its founders’ family established educational institutions in both India and Pakistan (below).

    11.3.4 Reform, Nationalism and Partition¶

    Ajmal Khān’s career shows how closely Unani reform was bound up with nationalism. Besides founding the Tibbia College, he was a leader of the Khilafat and Non-Cooperation movements. He presided over the Ahmedabad session of the Indian National Congress in 1921 and was the first chancellor of the Jamia Millia Islamia. He also wanted Unani drugs to be studied with the methods of modern chemistry. The research department he planned at the Tibbia College brought the chemist Salimuzzaman Siddiqui to Delhi. In 1931 Siddiqui isolated alkaloids from Rauvolfia serpentina and named one of them ajmaline after Ajmal Khān. This was an early step in the story told in Chapter 12 (see also Volume 15, Kṛṣi and Jīva Vijñāna).

    Colonial medical legislation gave the reformers a common cause. In 1910, as provincial governments prepared bills to register medical practitioners, Ajmal Khān founded the All-India Vedic and Unani Tibbi Conference, which brought practitioners of both systems together in their defence. The Acts that followed, beginning with Bombay’s in 1912, created registers of practitioners qualified in Western medicine. They did not forbid indigenous practice, but they denied vaidyas and ḥakīms official standing (Attewell 2007).

    Elsewhere reform took similar forms. In Lucknow the ʿAzīzī family of physicians founded the Takmīl al-Ṭibb College early in the twentieth century, and in Hyderabad the Nizam’s government taught Unani in state institutions. Attewell shows how the reformers standardised teaching and pharmacy and adopted the language of science. They also argued over how much Western anatomy and physiology Unani should absorb, while defending humoral theory as its core (Attewell 2007).

    The partition of 1947 divided the Unani world. Many ḥakīm families moved to Pakistan, and Hamdard was split. In India, Ḥakīm ʿAbdul Ḥamīd, the founder’s elder son, turned the company into a charitable trust (waqf) and used its income to build educational institutions in Delhi that grew into the Jamia Hamdard university. His younger brother, Ḥakīm Muḥammad Saʿīd, founded Hamdard in Karachi. In independent India, Unani was placed with Āyurveda and Siddha under the Indian Medicine Central Council Act of 1970, acquired its own research council and pharmacopoeia, and is now taught in many colleges. In recent years Ajmal Khān’s birthday, 11 February, has been marked as Unani Day.

    11.3.5 Theory¶

    Unani physiology is built on seven “natural matters” (umūr-i ṭabīʿiyya): the elements (arkān), temperament (mizāj), humours (akhlāṭ), organs (aʿḍāʾ), spirits or vital pneumata (arwāḥ), faculties (quwā) and functions (afʿāl). There are four elements—fire, air, water and earth—and four primary qualities: hot, cold, moist and dry. Every person, organ, food and drug has a temperament, a particular mixture of these qualities.

    Table 11.2. The four humours of Unani medicine
    Humour English Qualities Associated element
    dam blood hot and moist air
    balgham phlegm cold and moist water
    ṣafrāʾ yellow bile hot and dry fire
    sawdāʾ black bile cold and dry earth

    Health depends on the balance of the humours (Table 11.2) and on the proper management of six “essential factors” (asbāb-i sitta ḍarūriyya): air, food and drink, bodily movement and rest, sleep and wakefulness, evacuation and retention, and the emotions—the “six non-naturals” of Galenic medicine. The body’s own power of self-preservation and healing is called ṭabīʿat (“nature”), and the physician’s task is to assist it.

    Temperament (mizāj) is the central idea. It is the quality that results when the opposed qualities of the elements act on one another in a mixture. The physicians recognised one ideally balanced temperament, which exists only as a norm, and eight unbalanced ones: four simple (hot, cold, moist, dry) and four compound (hot and moist, hot and dry, cold and moist, cold and dry). Every person, organ, age, sex, season and region has its own temperament, so that what is health for one person may be disease for another. The physician therefore judges a patient against that patient’s own normal temperament, much as the Āyurvedic physician judges against the patient’s prakṛti (Chapter 2).

    The body is governed by three faculties. Each has its seat in a principal organ and is carried by a “spirit” (rūḥ), a subtle vapour formed from the finest part of the humours (Table 11.3).

    Table 11.3. The three faculties (quwā) of Galenic and Unani physiology
    Faculty Seat Carried by Functions
    natural (ṭabīʿiyya) liver veins nutrition, growth, reproduction
    vital (ḥayawāniyya) heart arteries life, the pulse and the passions
    psychic (nafsāniyya) brain nerves sensation, voluntary movement, thought

    The humours themselves are produced by digestion (haḍm), which takes place in four stages: in the stomach, where food becomes chyle; in the liver, where chyle is turned into the four humours; in the vessels; and in the organs, where nutriment is finally assimilated. Each stage leaves a residue that the body must expel. The scheme recalls the successive transformations of the Āyurvedic dhātus (Chapter 2), but it grew independently out of Galen’s physiology.

    For the Unani physician, disease is a contest between the morbid matter and the body’s nature. Its turning point is the crisis (buḥrān), which the Hippocratic tradition expected on particular “critical days”. Treatment follows the principle of contraries (ʿilāj bi’l-ḍidd): a hot disease is treated with cooling drugs and regimen, a moist one with drying measures. Morbid matter is first “ripened” with concoctive drugs (munḍij) and then evacuated with purgatives (mushil), a sequence that parallels the Āyurvedic preparation for purification (Chapter 7).

    11.3.6 Diagnosis and Treatment¶

    The Unani physician (ḥakīm) diagnoses by the pulse (nabḍ), the urine (bawl) and the stool, as well as by history and examination; pulse and urine lore are highly elaborated in the Greco-Arabic tradition. Treatment is of four kinds: by regimen (ʿilāj bi’l-tadbīr), including exercise, baths, massage, cupping (ḥijāma), venesection and leeches; by diet (ʿilāj bi’l-ghidhāʾ); by drugs (ʿilāj bi’l-dawāʾ), mostly herbal but including mineral preparations; and by surgery (ʿilāj bi’l-yad). Unani pharmacy includes calcined metallic preparations (kushta), to which the same safety concerns apply as to Āyurvedic bhasmas.

    Regimental therapy has a long history. Cupping (ḥijāma), with or without small incisions, was practised throughout the Islamic world and is associated in Muslim tradition with the Prophet’s approval, which has contributed to its popularity; venesection followed elaborate rules about which vein to open for which condition; and the Turkish bath (ḥammām), exercise, massage and changes of air were prescribed for health. Like bloodletting in the Āyurvedic and European traditions, these practices rest on humoral theory and have little modern evidence in their favour, though cupping is popular today in many countries.

    Unani pharmacy classifies drugs by their temperament and its degree, from the first degree (mild) to the fourth (strong enough to be dangerous), and prepares them in characteristic forms: electuaries (maʿjūn), confections made with honey or sugar (khamīra, jawārish), syrups (sharbat), distilled waters (ʿaraq), pills (ḥabb), powders (safūf), decoctions (joshānda) and oils. The Persian and Urdu pharmacopoeias of India include many Indian drugs alongside those inherited from the Greek and Arabic tradition, such as poppy, saffron, rose and the myrobalans, which the Arabic writers themselves had taken from India.

    Ibn Sīnā’s Canon lays down conditions under which the action of a drug may be judged from experience. The drug must be free from accidental change. It must be tried on a simple, not a compound, disease, and in contrasting cases. Its strength must be matched to the strength of the disease. Its effect must be observed over time, so that it is not confused with the natural course of the illness, and it must act consistently. And it must be tested on human beings, not only on animals. These rules are often cited as an early statement of the logic of drug trials. In practice the Unani pharmacopoeias rested mainly on authority and accumulated experience, and modern controlled trials of Unani preparations are still few.

    11.3.7 Unani Surgery and Hospitals¶

    Surgery (ʿilāj bi’l-yad) had an important place in the classical Arabic tradition, above all in al-Zahrāwī’s treatise, with its illustrations of instruments; cautery, bloodletting and the setting of fractures were practised by Unani surgeons in India. The Islamic world developed large public hospitals (bīmāristān) in Baghdad, Damascus and Cairo from the ninth century onwards (Pormann and Savage-Smith 2007), and similar institutions were founded by Indian rulers: the fourteenth-century Sultan Fīrūz Shāh Tughluq, for example, is recorded to have founded a hospital (dār al-shifāʾ) in Delhi where the sick were treated free of charge. In the Deccan, the Quṭb Shāhī ruler Muḥammad Qulī Quṭb Shāh built at Hyderabad, in 1595, a large hospital known as the Dār al-Shifāʾ, which also served as a place of teaching; its building still stands. Among the ordinances that the Mughal emperor Jahāngīr issued at his accession in 1605 was an order to found hospitals in the great cities and appoint physicians to them, paid from the treasury. How far such orders were carried out is uncertain, but they show that the public hospital was an established ideal of Indo-Islamic kingship.

    11.4 Sowa-Rigpa: Tibetan Medicine¶

    11.4.1 Origins and Texts¶

    Sowa-Rigpa (Tibetan gso ba rig pa, “the science of healing”) is the medical tradition of Tibet, practised also in Bhutan, Mongolia, Nepal and the Himalayan regions of India. Its foundational text is the Four Tantras (rGyud bzhi), which tradition regards as the teaching of the Medicine Buddha, transmitted through the physician Yuthog Yönten Gönpo. Historians date its compilation to the twelfth century and associate it with a later physician of the same name, Yuthog Yönten Gönpo “the Younger”. The Four Tantras draws heavily on Indian medicine, above all the Aṣṭāṅgahṛdaya (Chapter 5), and also on Chinese, Greco-Persian and indigenous Tibetan sources; its pulse diagnosis and some of its anatomy and therapy reflect these non-Indian influences.

    In the late seventeenth century, Desi Sangye Gyatso, regent of the Fifth Dalai Lama, wrote the authoritative commentary on the Four Tantras known as the Blue Beryl, commissioned a celebrated series of medical paintings to illustrate it, and founded the medical college on Chakpori hill in Lhasa. Janet Gyatso’s study of this period shows Tibetan physicians debating the relation between Buddhist doctrine and empirical observation in strikingly critical terms (Gyatso 2015). From the fifteenth century two schools of interpretation, the Northern (Byang) and the Southern (Zur), had developed rival commentaries and pharmaceutical traditions. Desi Sangye Gyatso’s work was in part an attempt to settle their disagreements with an authoritative text and images. Physicians on both sides sometimes appealed to what could be seen with the eyes, including the anatomy visible in corpses at sky-burial grounds, to question or defend descriptions of the body inherited from the texts.

    11.4.2 Indian Sources and the Question of Origins¶

    Tibetan histories of medicine tell that the early emperors of the seventh and eighth centuries invited physicians from India, China and the lands to the west, and that the meeting of their teachings laid the foundations of Tibetan medicine. These accounts were written down centuries later, and they should be read as tradition rather than history.

    What is certain is that Sanskrit medical works were translated into Tibetan in the period of the “later diffusion” of Buddhism. The great translator Rin chen bzang po (958–1055) translated the Aṣṭāṅgahṛdaya with an Indian collaborator. The canonical collection of translated treatises (bsTan ’gyur) also contains Candranandana’s commentary on the Aṣṭāṅgahṛdaya, the Yogaśataka attributed to Nāgārjuna, and several shorter medical works. The Tibetan Aṣṭāṅgahṛdaya is a careful, literal translation. Modern editors of the Sanskrit text use it as a witness to readings older than many surviving Sanskrit manuscripts.

    The Four Tantras opens with a Sanskrit title, as if it were translated from an Indian original. No Sanskrit text has ever been found, however, and much of its content cannot be derived from Indian sources, including its pulse diagnosis, its moxibustion and much of its terminology. Tibetan scholars disputed the work’s status for centuries. Gyatso has shown that the sixteenth-century physician Zurkharwa Lodrö Gyelpo argued, with careful qualifications, that it had been composed in Tibet by Yuthog rather than spoken by the Buddha (Gyatso 2015). Most modern historians regard it as a Tibetan composition of the twelfth century. It reworks Indian material, above all Vāgbhaṭa’s, together with Chinese and other elements, and is neither a translation nor an independent invention.

    11.4.3 Theory and Practice¶

    Its physiology closely parallels the Indian model: seven bodily constituents (lus zungs), from nutritional essence to reproductive fluid, and three waste products (faeces, urine and sweat). Like Āyurveda, Sowa-Rigpa describes three “faults” or humours—rlung (wind), mkhris pa (bile) and bad kan (phlegm)—whose balance is health; but it relates them explicitly to the three Buddhist mental poisons of desire, hatred and delusion. Diagnosis relies heavily on pulse and urine examination and on questioning; treatment uses diet, behaviour, medicines—mostly multi-ingredient herbal pills—and external therapies such as moxibustion, bloodletting and baths.

    Each of the three humours has five forms, and these correspond closely to the five forms of each doṣa in the Aṣṭāṅgahṛdaya. The “life-sustaining” wind seated in the head, for example, corresponds to Vāgbhaṭa’s prāṇa.

    The Explanatory Tantra’s embryology follows the Indian pattern: conception comes from the father’s seed, the mother’s blood and a consciousness driven by past action. But it groups the weeks of gestation into three phases named after the fish, the tortoise and the pig. Frances Garrett has traced how this account was elaborated in Tibetan medical and religious literature (Garrett 2008).

    Tibetan medicine also gave sustained attention to disorders of the mind. Many of these were attributed to a disturbance of the “life-wind” (srog rlung) and treated with diet, rest, warmth, massage and calming medicines, as well as with ritual (Clifford 1984).

    Diagnosis has a characteristic form. The physician reads the radial pulse on both wrists with three fingers. Each fingertip is divided into two halves, so that twelve positions in all report on particular organs, a scheme close to Chinese practice. Urine is examined in a white vessel, ideally at dawn: it is stirred and observed as it cools for colour, vapour, smell, bubbles, sediment and the film on its surface.

    11.4.4 Pharmacology¶

    Tibetan pharmacology analyses drugs, like Āyurveda, by taste, potency and post-digestive taste, but it counts eight potencies and seventeen qualities and organises the materia medica somewhat differently. Its medicines are usually compound pills of many ingredients, including plants of the high Himalaya and the Tibetan plateau, imported spices and woods from India, minerals and animal products. The most prestigious, the “precious pills” (rin chen ril bu), contain metals and minerals and are prepared by elaborate procedures that include the processing of mercury into a mercury sulphide preparation called btso thal, which Tibetan physicians regard as having been detoxified. Barbara Gerke’s study of this preparation shows how Tibetan physicians understand its toxicity and the processing that is meant to “tame” it, and how these ideas meet modern toxicology and regulation (Gerke 2021). As with rasaśāstra, independent evidence for the safety of such preparations is lacking, and the same cautions apply.

    Most Tibetan medicines, however, are plant-based compounds given as decoctions, powders, pills, medicated butters or pastes. Many of their ingredients are named after their Indian originals. For centuries they were prepared by the physician himself, or in monastic and institutional pharmacies, from plants he collected or bought. In the late twentieth century production moved increasingly to factories in India, Nepal, Bhutan, Mongolia and China. Rules of standardisation, licensing and “good manufacturing practice” changed what was made and how. Craig has studied how Tibetan physicians understand and defend the efficacy of their medicines while working within these regulatory systems (Craig 2012).

    11.4.5 Sowa-Rigpa and Āyurveda¶

    The relationship between the two traditions is close but not simple. The Four Tantras adopts the three humours, the seven constituents, the six tastes and much of the therapeutic framework of Āyurveda, and Tibetan physicians have long regarded India as the land from which their medicine came. But Tibetan medicine developed its own anatomy (including a system of channels influenced by Buddhist tantric yoga), its own pulse and urine diagnosis, its own pharmacy adapted to the plants of the Himalaya, and its own institutions, above all the monastic medical colleges. It also integrated Chinese elements, such as aspects of pulse diagnosis and moxibustion. Tibetan medicine is therefore best understood as an independent tradition that drew on Indian sources, not as a branch of Āyurveda.

    11.4.6 Sowa-Rigpa in India¶

    In India Sowa-Rigpa is practised by amchi physicians in Ladakh, Lahaul and Spiti, Sikkim, the Darjeeling hills and Arunachal Pradesh, as well as in the Tibetan exile community, whose Men-Tsee-Khang (Tibetan Medical and Astro-Science Institute) was refounded at Dharamsala in 1961. In 2010 Parliament recognised Sowa-Rigpa as one of the Indian systems of medicine, and a National Institute of Sowa-Rigpa has since been established at Leh.

    The full name of Men-Tsee-Khang (sman rtsis khang, “house of medicine and astro-calendrical science”) reflects a link that runs through Tibetan medicine. Physicians were often trained in calendrical science (rtsis) as well as medicine. Its Indian part derives largely from the astronomy of the Kālacakra Tantra. The calendar governed the timing of collecting and compounding medicines and of some treatments, and an astrological reckoning could form part of a consultation. The connection resembles the late medieval Indian combination of medicine and astrology described in Chapter 5.

    Sowa-Rigpa has also received international recognition as cultural heritage: in 2018 the Lum medicinal bathing tradition of Sowa-Rigpa, nominated by China, was inscribed on UNESCO’s list of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity. Its classical texts are shared across national borders, and practitioners in India, Nepal, Bhutan, Mongolia and the Tibetan regions of China maintain distinct institutions and debates about the future of the tradition.

    In the villages of Ladakh and Spiti, the amchi was traditionally a hereditary practitioner, often a member of a respected family, who collected and prepared his own medicines from the plants of the high Himalaya, treated patients in return for gifts and services, and combined medicine with Buddhist practice. Anthropologists have documented how this role has changed with the arrival of government health services, roads and markets, and with state recognition and certification (Pordié 2008; Craig 2012). The amchis’ dependence on wild plants, many of them slow-growing high-altitude species now heavily harvested for the commercial Tibetan medicine industry, has made the conservation of medicinal plants a pressing concern.

    Recognition has also changed how practitioners are trained. The name chosen for the system, Sowa-Rigpa, rather than “Tibetan medicine” or “amchi medicine”, presents it as one of India’s own systems with Himalayan roots, and not only as the medicine of a refugee community. Formal teaching is offered by Men-Tsee-Khang, by the Central Institute of Buddhist Studies at Leh and by the Central University of Tibetan Studies at Sarnath, and their degrees have become the basis of state registration. As with Āyurveda, the move from household apprenticeship to college degree has changed who practises and how. The hereditary village amchi now works alongside graduates, manufacturers and town clinics.

    11.4.7 The Structure of the Four Tantras¶

    The Four Tantras is presented as a dialogue between two emanations of the Medicine Buddha. It has 156 chapters in four parts (Table 11.4).

    Table 11.4. The four parts of the Four Tantras (rGyud bzhi)
    Part Tibetan Ch. Main contents
    Root Tantra rTsa rgyud 6 summary of the whole system; the tree of medicine
    Explanatory Tantra bShad rgyud 31 embryology, anatomy, physiology, causes of disease, regimen, diet, drugs, the physician
    Oral Instruction Tantra Man ngag rgyud 92 diagnosis and treatment of particular diseases
    Subsequent Tantra Phyi ma rgyud 27 pulse and urine, preparation of medicines, purification and external therapies

    The four parts are the short Root Tantra, which summarises the whole system with the image of a tree whose roots, trunks, branches and leaves represent the body in health and disease, diagnosis and treatment (in the usual count, three roots, nine trunks, forty-seven branches and 224 leaves, with flowers and fruits standing for health, long life and the goals of a good life); the Explanatory Tantra, on anatomy, physiology, embryology, the causes of disease, regimen, diet, drugs and the conduct of the physician; the long Oral Instruction Tantra, on the diagnosis and treatment of particular diseases; and the Subsequent Tantra, on pulse and urine diagnosis, the preparation of medicines and external therapies. The illustrated medical paintings commissioned by Desi Sangye Gyatso, which show anatomy, plants, instruments, the causes of disease and the ethics of the physician, are among the most remarkable works of Tibetan art; a complete set has been published with translations of its inscriptions (Parfionovitch et al. 1992).

    11.5 Exchanges Between the Traditions¶

    The modern institutions separate Āyurveda, Siddha, Unani and Sowa-Rigpa into distinct “systems”, each with its own colleges, councils and pharmacopoeias. Historically the traditions were in constant contact, and the evidence of exchange runs in every direction.

    Āyurveda and Unani. Sanskrit medicine absorbed drugs and ideas from Islamic medicine. Opium and other new drugs entered the Sanskrit pharmacopoeia in the medieval and early modern periods (Chapter 5). Pulse diagnosis, absent from the classical compendia, may owe something to Greco-Arabic pulse lore (Chapter 10). A late Sanskrit work on distilled medicinal waters (arka), the Arkaprakāśa, takes as its subject a form of pharmacy that is generally thought to have reached Indian physicians from Islamic practice, where such distillates were called ʿaraq. In the early modern period, some authors set out the principles of Unani medicine in Sanskrit verse for Hindu physicians. In the other direction, Persian authors translated and summarised Sanskrit works for Muslim readers, Unani pharmacy took in many Indian drugs, and Hindu physicians practised Unani at the Mughal and later courts (Section 11.3).

    Siddha, Āyurveda and Unani in the South. Tamil physicians used Sanskrit texts and Sanskrit physicians used Tamil formularies. Tamil-speaking Muslim physicians practised Unani alongside both. The first government school of indigenous medicine in Madras taught all three together, and many twentieth-century Tamil practitioners moved between them in practice even as the institutions drew boundaries.

    Sowa-Rigpa and Indian medicine. Tibetan physicians obtained many of their drugs from the Indian plains through the trans-Himalayan trade, and the shared materia medica is visible in iconography. The Medicine Buddha holds in his hand a myrobalan (a ru ra, Sanskrit harītakī), which Tibetan pharmacology calls the “king of medicines”. Its place in Āyurveda is described in Chapter 6. In Ladakh and Spiti, amchis have long treated patients who also consulted Āyurvedic vaidyas, ritual healers and, more recently, government doctors.

    Patients, then as now, have moved freely between practitioners, choosing according to cost, reputation, the kind of illness and the advice of family. Anthropologists describe this as medical pluralism and have studied how patients combine traditional and biomedical care, and how the state’s categories shape what practitioners may legally do (Leslie 1976; Sujatha and Abraham 2012). The comparison in Section 11.7 should be read with this history of exchange in mind.

    11.6 Local, Folk and Tribal Health Traditions¶

    Most healing in India has always taken place outside the learned systems. Households have their own remedies and dietary rules; villages have their midwives (dāī), bonesetters, snakebite healers, exorcists and herbalists; Adivasi communities possess detailed knowledge of the plants of their forests. These practitioners rarely wrote books, and much of their knowledge was transmitted orally, within families, castes or communities. The Sanskrit compendia themselves acknowledge such knowledge: Caraka says that herdsmen, shepherds, forest-dwellers and others know plants by name and form, though only the learned physician knows how to use them properly (Ca.Sū. 1.120–121).

    11.6.1 Healing and Religion¶

    Much local healing is inseparable from religion. Smallpox, for example, was understood across northern and eastern India as the presence of the goddess Śītalā, “the cool one”, and treated by worship, cooling measures and the avoidance of certain foods, rather than by medicine. At the same time, Bengali practitioners called ṭīkādārs practised variolation—the deliberate inoculation of material from smallpox pustules to produce a milder infection and lasting immunity—as the British surgeon J. Z. Holwell described in 1767, before Jenner’s introduction of vaccination with cowpox in 1796. The colonial government later banned variolation in favour of vaccination, a policy that met long resistance. Spirit possession, the evil eye, sorcery and the anger of local deities were, and remain, common explanations of illness, and healing at shrines and by ritual specialists is an important part of the medical landscape. Sudhir Kakar’s study of healers in India, and William Sax’s study of ritual healing in the Himalaya, explore these practices with sympathy and critical attention (Kakar 1982; Sax 2009).

    11.6.2 Recording and Appropriation¶

    Much of what outsiders learned about Indian medicinal plants came from such local experts, often without acknowledgement. The Portuguese physician Garcia da Orta’s Colóquios dos simples e drogas da Índia (Goa, 1563) drew on Indian informants. The twelve-volume Hortus Malabaricus (Amsterdam, 1678–1693), compiled under the Dutch governor Hendrik van Rheede, was based on the knowledge of the Kerala physician Itty Achuden, of the Īḻava community, and of Konkani brahmin collaborators, whose testimony is printed in the first volume.

    The colonial period produced many catalogues of Indian drugs that relied on local informants. Whitelaw Ainslie, a surgeon of the Madras establishment, published a Materia Indica (1826) that recorded Tamil, Sanskrit, Persian and other names of hundreds of drugs; William Roxburgh’s Flora Indica and later the multi-volume Dictionary of the Economic Products of India (1889–1896) edited by George Watt included extensive medical information gathered from Indian practitioners. These works are valuable records of nineteenth-century practice, though they rarely name the people from whom the knowledge came.

    In recent decades the question of who owns traditional knowledge has become a legal and political issue. A frequently cited example is that of the Kani people of the Western Ghats in Kerala, whose knowledge of the plant ārōkyappacca (Trichopus zeylanicus) led scientists at the Tropical Botanic Garden and Research Institute in Thiruvananthapuram to develop a commercial herbal product in the 1990s, with an agreement, as the case is usually described, to share fees and royalties with the community—an arrangement often cited as an early model of benefit-sharing, though not without controversy. India’s Biological Diversity Act of 2002 and the Traditional Knowledge Digital Library (Chapter 12) are attempts to protect such knowledge from misappropriation.

    11.6.3 Kinds of Local Healer¶

    The range of local healers is very wide, and varies from region to region. Among the most widespread are:

    • Birth attendants (dāī and their regional equivalents), usually women of low caste status, who attended most births in India until recent decades. Their knowledge of normal labour was considerable, but some customary practices—delayed breastfeeding, unclean cutting of the cord, the application of substances to the cord stump—contributed to maternal and newborn deaths. Programmes since the 1970s have tried to train them, and the shift to births in health facilities has transformed their role.

    • Bonesetters, who treat fractures and dislocations by manipulation and splinting, sometimes with great skill and sometimes with serious complications.

    • Snakebite healers, who use mantras, herbs and rituals; in Kerala, specialist viṣavaidyas maintained a learned tradition in Sanskrit and Malayalam.

    • Herbalists of Adivasi and rural communities, whose detailed knowledge of local plants has been documented by ethnobotanists since the colonial period.

    • Ritual healers, exorcists and shrine-based healers, to whom many people take mental illness and misfortune; studies of such shrines have shown both the comfort they provide and the abuses—chaining, beating, neglect—that sometimes occur.

    11.6.4 Value and Limits¶

    Organisations such as the Foundation for Revitalisation of Local Health Traditions in Bengaluru, and scholars working with them, have argued that “local health traditions” are an important resource for primary health care and should be documented and supported (Shankar and Unnikrishnan 2004). Local healers are often the first and sometimes the only source of care in remote areas, and they possess genuine knowledge of plants and of their communities. They also, like all healers, sometimes do harm: through delay in the treatment of serious illness, through harmful practices in childbirth and snakebite, and through the use of toxic substances. Respect for local knowledge and insistence on safety are not in conflict.

    11.7 Comparison¶

    Table 11.5. The learned medical traditions of India compared
    Āyurveda Siddha Unani Sowa-Rigpa
    Language of the classics Sanskrit Tamil Arabic, Persian, Urdu Tibetan
    Foundational texts Caraka, Suśruta, Vāgbhaṭa poems attributed to the Siddhars Hippocrates, Galen, Ibn Sīnā Four Tantras
    Elements five five four five
    Humours three three four three
    Characteristic diagnosis (later) pulse, eightfold examination pulse, urine and oil-drop test pulse and urine pulse and urine
    Metallic preparations bhasma, rasa preparations centūram, paṟpam, pāṣāṇams kushta precious pills with processed mercury

    The traditions compared in Table 11.5 share a broadly humoral understanding of the body, a reliance on diet and regimen, a rich materia medica and, in each case, a prestigious but hazardous use of metals and minerals. They have borrowed from one another for centuries, and the boundaries between them were more fluid in practice than the modern institutional categories suggest.

    Each tradition also has its own picture of the good physician, to set beside the Sanskrit ideal described in Chapter 1. The Explanatory Tantra of the Four Tantras closes with a chapter on the physician, which lists the qualities he needs—among them intelligence, compassion, fidelity to his vows and his teacher, skill, diligence and right conduct among people—and, like Caraka, ranks the physician who works out of compassion above the one who works for gain. In the Unani tradition the ninth-century physician Isḥāq ibn ʿAlī al-Ruhāwī wrote one of the earliest books devoted entirely to medical ethics, Adab al-ṭabīb (“The Conduct of the Physician”), on the physician’s own regimen and piety, his behaviour towards patients and their visitors, and the examination of physicians; in later Islamic cities the market inspector (muḥtasib) was expected to test physicians and pharmacists and to check their drugs (Pormann and Savage-Smith 2007). The Siddha texts stress instead the bond between teacher and pupil and the secrecy of knowledge, promising effective remedies only to the worthy disciple and warning against revealing them to the unworthy. These ideals differ. They share a concern that the power of medicine be used for the patient’s good, and a conviction that the physician’s character matters as much as his learning (Section 1.8).

    Summary

    Siddha, Unani and Sowa-Rigpa are learned traditions in their own right, each with its own texts and languages, though all three share with Āyurveda a humoral understanding of the body and a prestigious but hazardous use of metals. Their modern identities—Siddha as the ancient medicine of the Tamils, Unani as the medicine of Indian Muslims, Sowa-Rigpa as one of India’s own systems—were shaped in the nineteenth and twentieth centuries, while their history is one of constant exchange. Around and beneath all of them, local healers have always provided much of the care, with real knowledge and real risks.
