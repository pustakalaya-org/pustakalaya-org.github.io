    A Guide to Further Reading¶

    This guide is a short, annotated selection from the works listed in the Select Bibliography, arranged by topic, for readers who wish to go further. Full details are given in the bibliography.

    The Classical Texts¶

    The Sanskrit texts of the great compendia are most conveniently read in the editions of Jādavjī Trikamjī Ācārya, which print the standard commentaries of Cakrapāṇidatta on Caraka and of Ḍalhaṇa on Suśruta (Trikamji Acharya 1941; Trikamji Acharya and Acharya 1938). Complete English translations are those of Priyavrat Sharma for Caraka (Sharma 1981–1994), Kunja Lal Bhishagratna for Suśruta (Bhishagratna 1907–1916) and K. R. Srikantha Murthy for Vāgbhaṭa’s Aṣṭāṅgahṛdaya (Srikantha Murthy 2012). Translations made for practitioners sometimes modernise the text; readers should compare them with the Sanskrit where possible. The best starting point for the general reader is Dominik Wujastyk’s The Roots of Āyurveda, which translates key chapters of the major works, including the Bower Manuscript, with a clear and reliable introduction (Wujastyk 2003a). The Bower Manuscript itself was edited and translated by Hoernle (Hoernle 1893–1912).

    Histories and Surveys¶

    G. Jan Meulenbeld’s History of Indian Medical Literature is the indispensable reference for authors, texts, commentaries and dates; it is arranged as a work of reference rather than a narrative (Meulenbeld 1999–2002). Older surveys by Jolly (Jolly 1951), Filliozat (Filliozat 1964), Kutumbiah (Kutumbiah 1962) and Zimmer (Zimmer 1948), and the collaborative volume edited by Subbarayappa (Subbarayappa 2001), remain useful, though their dates should be checked against Meulenbeld. Wujastyk’s essay on “the science of medicine” is a concise modern overview (Wujastyk 2003b). M. S. Valiathan’s three volumes on the legacies of Caraka, Suśruta and Vāgbhaṭa retell the great compendia, section by section, for the modern Indian reader (Valiathan 2003, 2007, 2009), and Meulenbeld’s annotated translation of the first chapters of the Mādhavanidāna with its chief commentary shows how a diagnostic handbook was read (Meulenbeld 1974).

    Origins: Veda, Buddhism and the Ascetics¶

    Kenneth Zysk’s two books—on healing in the Vedic hymns and on medicine in the early Buddhist monasteries—are the fundamental studies of the period before the compendia, and develop the influential but still debated thesis that rational medicine emerged among the heterodox ascetics (Zysk 1985, 1991). On the transmission of Indian medicine to China, see Salguero (Salguero 2014); on a Sanskrit handbook carried into Tibetan and Khotanese, Emmerick’s edition of the Siddhasāra (Emmerick 1980–1982).

    Surgery and Anatomy¶

    Guido Majno’s The Healing Hand places Suśruta’s surgery in the context of wound care across the ancient world (Majno 1975). Hoernle’s study of the bones and Mukhopadhyaya’s study of the surgical instruments are pioneering works that are still consulted (Hoernle 1907; Mukhopadhyaya 1913–1914). Wujastyk discusses how the body was pictured in premodern India (Wujastyk 2009).

    Body, Gender and Society¶

    Rahul Peter Das’s The Origin of the Life of a Human Being is a detailed study of the medical and sexological texts on conception and the female body (Das 2003); Martha Ann Selby’s article reads the classical narratives of conception, gestation and labour (Selby 2005), and Jeffery, Jeffery and Lyon’s ethnography describes childbirth and the work of birth attendants in modern north India (Jeffery et al. 1989). Francis Zimmermann’s The Jungle and the Aroma of Meats is the classic study of the ecological theme in classical dietetics and pharmacology (Zimmermann 1987), and Dagmar Wujastyk’s Well-Mannered Medicine the fullest study of medical ethics and etiquette in the compendia (Wujastyk 2012); K. T. Achaya’s history of Indian food sets the medical dietetics beside the culinary literature (Achaya 1994). Olivelle’s translation of Manu’s law code shows the place of physicians in brahmanical law (Olivelle 2005), and his translation of the Arthaśāstra the place of medicine in statecraft (Olivelle 2013).

    Alchemy and Rasaśāstra¶

    David Gordon White’s The Alchemical Body is the essential study of the Siddha alchemical traditions (White 1996); P. C. Ray’s History of Hindu Chemistry remains valuable for its texts (Ray 1902–1909); Wujastyk’s article on the “alchemical ghost” corrects a persistent error (Wujastyk 1984). Volume 14 of this series, Rasāyana, treats the subject in detail.

    Siddha, Unani, Sowa-Rigpa and Local Traditions¶

    On Siddha medicine and its modern history, see Weiss (Weiss 2009) and, for the wider Siddha poetic tradition, Zvelebil (Zvelebil 1996). On Islamic medicine generally, see Ullmann’s concise survey and Pormann and Savage-Smith (Ullmann 1978; Pormann and Savage-Smith 2007); on Unani in India, Alavi and Attewell (Alavi 2008; Attewell 2007). On Tibetan medicine, Gyatso’s intellectual history and the anthropological studies of Craig and of the contributors to Pordié’s volume (Gyatso 2015; Craig 2012; Pordié 2008); Garrett on embryology, Clifford on mental illness, and the published set of the Blue Beryl paintings (Garrett 2008; Clifford 1984; Parfionovitch et al. 1992); Gerke on the Tibetan mercury preparation and its safety (Gerke 2021). On medical pluralism in India today, see the volume edited by Sujatha and Abraham (Sujatha and Abraham 2012). On healing and religion, Kakar and Sax (Kakar 1982; Sax 2009); on local health traditions, Shankar and Unnikrishnan (Shankar and Unnikrishnan 2004).

    Colonial and Modern Āyurveda¶

    The essays in Leslie’s Asian Medical Systems and in Leslie and Young’s Paths to Asian Medical Knowledge remain classic (Leslie 1976; Leslie and Young 1992). Arnold surveys medicine in colonial India (Arnold 2000); Ernst’s volume and Alter’s discuss plural medicine and globalisation (Ernst 2002; Alter 2005); Langford’s ethnography and Banerjee’s study of the pharmaceutical industry are essential for modern practice (Langford 2002; Banerjee 2009); and the essays edited by Dagmar Wujastyk and Frederick Smith survey “modern and global Ayurveda” (Wujastyk and Smith 2008). The making of Āyurveda into a modern profession is studied regionally by Mukharji for Bengal (who has also studied the Bengali practitioners of Western medicine (Mukharji 2009)), Berger for the United Provinces and Sivaramakrishnan for the Punjab, and Brass analyses the politics of Āyurvedic education after independence (Mukharji 2016; Berger 2013; Sivaramakrishnan 2006; Brass 1972).

    Evidence and Safety¶

    On heavy metals in Ayurvedic products, see the studies of Saper and colleagues and the report of the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (Saper et al. 2004, 2008; Centers for Disease Control and Prevention 2004); on liver injury, the case series of Björnsson and colleagues and of Nagral and colleagues (Björnsson et al. 2020; Nagral et al. 2021); on the reliability of Āyurvedic diagnosis, the overview of Kurande and colleagues (Kurande et al. 2013); and on snakebite in India, the mortality study of Suraweera and colleagues and the WHO guidelines (Suraweera et al. 2020; World Health Organization, Regional Office for South-East Asia 2016). Examples of rigorous clinical research include Vakil’s study of Rauvolfia and the trial of guggulipid (Vakil 1949; Szapary et al. 2003). Broader discussions of research on Ayurvedic drugs are given by Patwardhan and colleagues and by Mukherjee and Wahile (Patwardhan et al. 2005; Mukherjee and Wahile 2006); the policy context is set out in the WHO strategy (World Health Organization 2013).
