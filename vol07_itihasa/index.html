    Preface¶

    This is Volume 7 of the series Bhāratīya Jñāna Paramparā: Itihāsa–Purāṇa: Epics and Purāṇas. After an introductory chapter on the genre, it treats the Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa (Chapters 2–3), the Mahābhārata and the Harivaṃśa (Chapters 4–5), the Bhagavad Gītā (Chapter 6), and the Mahāpurāṇas, Upapurāṇas and māhātmyas (Chapters 7–8). Three thematic chapters then take up what the narrative chapters leave open: the hard cases and the limits of epic dharma (Chapter 9), the women of the epics (Chapter 10) and the philosophy of the Mokṣadharma and the Nārāyaṇīya (Chapter 11). The last chapter follows the epics’ afterlife in the regional languages, in Persian at the Mughal court and in the Sikh Dasam Granth, in Southeast Asia, in sculpture, dance, ritual theatre, film and festival, and in modern debates about their historicity.

    Two perspectives run through the book. The first is that of the tradition, for which these texts are sacred history and scripture. The second is that of modern textual and historical scholarship, which dates their composition (academic dates are given first throughout, with traditional dates labelled as such), studies their growth through critical editions, and reads them alongside the Jain, Buddhist, regional and oppositional retellings — including feminist and Dalit critiques — that are part of their history. Quotations from the Sanskrit texts are given only where their wording and reference could be checked; claims that archaeology or astronomy confirm the epics are presented as the contested proposals they are. Where a heading or a sentence reports what the tradition holds, it says so. The book is a work of synthesis and introduction; a note on texts, translations and abbreviations follows, and readers are referred to the select bibliography for the standard scholarship on which it draws.

    Nikhil Kumar Rajput
    Ramanujan College, University of Delhi
    Guru Pūrṇimā, 2026
