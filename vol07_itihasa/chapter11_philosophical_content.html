    Chapter 11: Philosophical Content Embedded in the Epics¶

    nāsti buddhir ayuktasya na cāyuktasya bhāvanā /
    na cābhāvayataḥ śāntir aśāntasya kutaḥ sukham //
    “The undisciplined has no wisdom, nor has he contemplation; without contemplation there is no peace, and for one without peace, how can there be happiness?”

    — Bhagavad Gītā 2.66

    The epics teach philosophy in dialogue and in situations of crisis: Bhīṣma teaches from his deathbed, Kṛṣṇa on the battlefield, Sanatsujāta in the sleepless night before a war. Because the Mahābhārata took shape before the classical philosophical systems (darśanas) were codified in their sūtras and commentaries, these passages are a principal source for the early history of Sāṅkhya, Yoga and Vedāntic thought, and of devotional theism (Brockington 1998; Sutton 2000). The Bhagavad Gītā has its own chapter (Chapter 6); the political teaching of the Śānti Parva is summarised in Section 4.17, and Vidura’s practical wisdom in Section 9.4. This chapter is concerned with the Mokṣadharma, the third and longest section of the Śānti Parva (Section 5.12), with the shorter philosophical texts of the epic, and with the philosophy of the Purāṇas.

    11.1 The Mokṣadharma¶

    The Mokṣadharma (“the dharma of liberation”) is a vast collection of dialogues on the nature of the self and the means of release from rebirth. Bhīṣma rarely speaks in his own voice; he answers Yudhiṣṭhira’s questions with old stories (itihāsa purātana), in which named teachers instruct a pupil or a king. The sage Bhṛgu teaches Bharadvāja on creation, the elements and the soul; Vasiṣṭha, Yājñavalkya and Pañcaśikha each instruct a King Janaka of Videha; Vyāsa teaches his son Śuka; and a woman ascetic, Sulabhā, refutes Janaka’s claim to be liberated (Section 10.10.1). The collection is not a system. Its dialogues offer different and sometimes incompatible accounts of the world, the self and God, and it ends with the Nārāyaṇīya (Section 11.5), which subordinates them all to devotion.

    11.1.1 Consolation and the Problem of Suffering¶

    The occasion of the Mokṣadharma is grief, and much of it is consolation. In the Strī Parva Vidura has already consoled the blind king with the parable of the man in the well: fleeing a wild elephant through a forest full of beasts, a man falls into a well and hangs from a creeper, with a great snake below and the elephant above, while black and white mice gnaw at the creeper; bees above him drip honey, and he goes on tasting it. The forest is the world, Vidura explains, the elephant old age, the snake death, the mice the nights and days, and the honey the pleasures that keep people from thinking of their end. In the Śānti Parva Bhīṣma recounts how a brahmin consoled King Senajit, mourning his son, with verses on the impermanence of every union, among them the song of the courtesan Piṅgalā, who waits all night for lovers who do not come and finds peace at dawn when she gives up hope. Other “songs” in the same part of the book make the same point, such as that of Maṅki, who learns from the loss of the two young bulls he had bought that desire is the root of suffering.

    The dialogues also debate what causes suffering and success. Some teachers ascribe them to past action (karman), others to fate (daiva), to a being’s own nature (svabhāva) or to time (kāla). In one dialogue the demon king Bali, stripped of his sovereignty, tells the gloating Indra that it is time, not Indra’s prowess, that has brought him low, and that time will humble Indra in his turn. Time is also measured on a cosmic scale: the four ages (yugas) succeed each other in decline, dharma losing one “foot” in each, and the cycle repeats (Table 7.6). Comparable schemes of declining ages are found elsewhere, as in Hesiod’s ages of gold, silver, bronze and iron. Against all these powers the Mokṣadharma sets the possibility of release: the self that knows itself to be distinct from body, mind and the world is not touched by them.

    11.2 Sāṅkhya and Yoga in the Mokṣadharma¶

    The Mahābhārata contains the most extensive pre-classical exposition of Sāṅkhya and Yoga. The classical systems are known from Īśvarakṛṣṇa’s Sāṅkhyakārikā (c. 4th–5th century CE) and Patañjali’s Yogasūtra (c. 325–425 CE); the epic dialogues are older in large part, and they show both traditions before they were fixed.

    What the two words mean.

    Franklin Edgerton argued in a classic article that in the epic “Sāṅkhya” and “Yoga” are not yet the names of two philosophical systems but of two methods of reaching liberation: Sāṅkhya is the way of knowledge, reached by reasoning, reckoning (saṅkhyā) and discrimination; Yoga is the way of disciplined practice and meditation (Edgerton 1924). The metaphysics behind both, he held, is largely that of the Upaniṣads, and it is usually theistic or monist rather than the dualism without God of classical Sāṅkhya. The epic’s teachers insist that the two methods lead to the same goal, as the Gītā does when it says that only the unlearned speak of Sāṅkhya and Yoga as different, and that whoever sees them as one sees truly (5.4–5).

    The enumerations.

    The dialogues count the constituents of the world and the person in different ways — seventeen, twenty-four or twenty-five principles (tattvas). The fullest lists, in the teachings given to Janaka, come close to the classical scheme. From unmanifest nature (avyakta, prakṛti), composed of the three guṇas, evolve the intellect (mahat or buddhi), the sense of self (ahaṃkāra), the mind (manas), the senses and organs of action, and the subtle and gross elements; the conscious self (puruṣa) is the twenty-fifth. Liberation comes from discriminating the self from all that it is not, and the teachers describe in detail how the evolutes dissolve back into their source at the end of a cosmic cycle. The dialogue of Vasiṣṭha with King Karāla Janaka adds a decisive further step: it distinguishes the twenty-fifth principle, the individual self that “is awakening” (budhyamāna), from a twenty-sixth, the “awakened” (buddha), the supreme self or God, with whom the liberated soul is united. This is a theistic Sāṅkhya, close in spirit to the Gītā’s supreme person beyond the perishable and the imperishable (Gītā 15.16–18) and to the theology of the Nārāyaṇīya. The teacher Pañcaśikha, whom the later Sāṅkhya tradition counts, after Kapila and Āsuri, among the founders of the school (Sāṅkhyakārikā 70), appears here instructing Janaka in the discrimination of the self from the elements and faculties.

    Yoga.

    The Mokṣadharma’s yoga passages describe a recognisable discipline: a solitary place, a light diet, the restraint of the senses, the control of breath (prāṇāyāma), and the fixing of the mind (dhāraṇā) until the yogin sees the self within himself. They also describe the powers that the yogin acquires and the dangers of becoming attached to them. Two narratives show yoga in action. Sulabhā, the woman ascetic, enters King Janaka’s mind by yogic power in order to show him that he is not yet free (Section 10.10.1); and Vyāsa’s son Śuka, having learned Sāṅkhya and Yoga, rises by yoga into the sky and passes beyond the world, leaving his father calling after him. Bhīṣma’s own teaching on the self, given through dialogues of earlier sages and in his own voice, echoes the Kaṭha and other Upaniṣads: the self is unborn and undying, subtler than the subtle, hidden in the cave of the heart, and known by those who are free from desire.

    The scholarly debate.

    E. Washburn Hopkins, who catalogued the epic’s philosophical vocabulary at the beginning of the twentieth century, stressed its variety and its lack of system (Hopkins 1901). Erich Frauwallner tried to reconstruct from the Mokṣadharma a sequence of stages through which Sāṅkhya developed before Īśvarakṛṣṇa (Frauwallner 1973); Gerald Larson traced a movement from the speculative thought of the Upaniṣads and the epic, which he called “proto-Sāṅkhya,” to the classical system (Larson 1969). Scholars still disagree whether a single original Sāṅkhya ever existed, or whether the epic preserves several related traditions that the classical system later unified; and the dating of individual dialogues is uncertain (Brockington 1998). What is not disputed is that the Mokṣadharma is the main evidence for the period in which the vocabulary of Sāṅkhya and Yoga — prakṛti and puruṣa, the guṇas, the evolutes, discrimination and the disciplined mind — became the common language of Indian thought about liberation, shared by theists and non-theists alike.

    11.3 The Sanatsujātīya¶

    The Sanatsujātīya (Udyoga Parva, chapters 41–45 in the CE) is a philosophical dialogue between the ancient sage Sanatsujāta and the blind king Dhṛtarāṣṭra, which follows Vidura’s counsel on the sleepless night before Sañjaya reports the Pāṇḍavas’ response. When Dhṛtarāṣṭra asks about death, Vidura declares himself unqualified to teach on it, since he is a Śūdra by birth, and summons Sanatsujāta — an instance of the epic’s acknowledgement of caste limits on teaching.

    Sanatsujāta teaches that death, in the sense that matters, is not the end of the body but heedlessness (pramāda) about one’s true nature; that the self is unborn and imperishable; that liberation comes through knowledge of Brahman, not through ritual alone; and he discusses the meaning of mauna, the silence of the sage, and the virtues and faults of the ascetic.

    Sanatsujātīya on Death — Udyoga Parva 5.42.4 (Critical Edition)

    pramādaṃ vai mṛtyum ahaṃ bravīmi sadāpramādam amṛtatvaṃ bravīmi
    Translation:
    “Heedlessness, I say, is death; constant heedfulness, I say, is immortality.”

    A commentary on the Sanatsujātīya is attributed to Śaṅkara, which made the text an important source for later Advaitins; the attribution, like that of several works ascribed to him, is debated.

    11.4 The Anu Gītā — Kṛṣṇa’s Second Discourse¶

    After the war and the horse sacrifice, Arjuna, who confesses that he has forgotten what Kṛṣṇa taught him on the battlefield, asks him to repeat it. Kṛṣṇa replies that he cannot repeat it exactly, and instead gives the Anugītā (Āśvamedhika Parva), a long discourse that revisits many Gītā themes with a different emphasis: more on yoga, the subtle body and the path of knowledge, with the teaching of a perfected sage (siddha) to the brahmin Kāśyapa on rebirth and liberation, and the Brāhmaṇagītā, a dialogue between a brahmin and his wife on the inner sacrifice.

    11.5 The Nārāyaṇīya — Early Vaiṣṇava Theology in the Epic¶

    Near the end of the Mokṣadharma stands a group of chapters (12.321–339 in the Critical Edition) that the tradition calls the Nārāyaṇīya. It is the fullest statement in the epic of a theology centred on Nārāyaṇa, and it is the earliest substantial witness to the ideas of the Pāñcarātra tradition, whose later scriptures are treated in Volume 6, Tantra–Āgama. Most scholars regard it as one of the later layers of the Śānti Parva; a collaborative study edited by Peter Schreiner has examined its structure, its sources and its place in the history of Vaiṣṇavism in detail (Schreiner 1997).

    11.5.1 The Narrative Frame¶

    The section is built, like the epic itself, of stories within stories. Yudhiṣṭhira asks Bhīṣma which god the four stages of life should worship. Bhīṣma answers with a tale. The sage Nārada visits the twin sages Nara and Nārāyaṇa in their hermitage at Badarī in the Himalaya and finds Nārāyaṇa engaged in worship; asked whom the supreme god himself worships, Nārāyaṇa replies that he honours his own highest self. Nārada then travels to Śvetadvīpa, the “White Island” that lies north of the ocean of milk (12.322.8), whose radiant inhabitants are devoted to the one god with undivided minds. There Nārāyaṇa appears to him in a cosmic form and teaches him the doctrine that Nārada carries back to the world.

    Within this frame is set the story of King Uparicara Vasu, a devotee who performs a great horse sacrifice in which no animal is killed. In a related episode, when the gods and the sages dispute whether the word aja in the injunction to sacrifice means “goat” or “seed grain that will not sprout again”, Vasu decides in favour of the goat and as a result falls from the sky into a chasm in the earth, until his devotion to Nārāyaṇa restores him. The episode is one of several places in the epic where non-violence is set against the Vedic sacrifice of animals.

    11.5.2 The Teaching¶

    The doctrine revealed at Śvetadvīpa combines devotion with the cosmology of Sāṅkhya. The supreme god Vāsudeva is the highest self of all beings. From him proceed, in succession, three further forms, each named after a member of the Vṛṣṇi clan and each identified with a Sāṅkhya principle: Saṃkarṣaṇa with the individual soul (jīva), Pradyumna with mind (manas), and Aniruddha with the sense of self (ahaṃkāra). This scheme of four “emanations” (vyūha) became the central doctrine of the Pāñcarātra, and it shows how the deified Vṛṣṇi heroes known from inscriptions (Section 4.9) were absorbed into a philosophical theology.

    The Nārāyaṇīya also contains one of the earliest lists of Viṣṇu’s descents in the world (12.326). It names the Swan (haṃsa), the Tortoise, the Fish, the Boar, the Man-Lion, the Dwarf, the two Rāmas (Bhārgava and Dāśarathi), Kṛṣṇa as “the Sātvata,” and the future Kalki — a list close to, but not identical with, the later ten (Table 7.3). A later chapter of the Nārāyaṇīya tells separately how Viṣṇu, in a horse-headed form (Hayaśiras), recovered the Vedas stolen by the demons Madhu and Kaiṭabha. The text names the religion it teaches the “single-minded” (ekāntin) dharma, traces its transmission through a line of teachers back to Nārāyaṇa himself, and identifies it with the teaching that Kṛṣṇa gave Arjuna on the battlefield (12.336.8): it thus offers one of the earliest interpretations of the Bhagavad Gītā from within the epic.

    11.5.3 Interpreting the White Island¶

    The White Island has attracted much speculation. Some nineteenth-century scholars suggested that its pale, monotheist worshippers reflected reports of Christian communities reaching India, a theory that later scholarship has abandoned; Śvetadvīpa belongs with the other luminous mythical lands of Purāṇic cosmography, and its whiteness expresses purity rather than ethnicity. Modern study has concentrated instead on the Nārāyaṇīya’s composite structure, its relation to the Pāñcarātra Saṃhitās and to the Harivaṃśa, and its use of Sāṅkhya categories, which shows that in the period of its composition the boundary between devotional theology and the philosophy of the Mokṣadharma was fluid (Schreiner 1997; Hiltebeitel 2001). For the tradition, the Nārāyaṇīya is the epic’s own declaration that the long instruction of the Śānti Parva culminates in devotion to Nārāyaṇa, and later Vaiṣṇava theologians cited it as authoritative scripture.

    11.6 Philosophical Content in the Purāṇas¶

    The Purāṇas continue the epic practice of setting philosophy in dialogue. The Bhāgavata (Book 3) gives Kapila’s discourse to his mother Devahūti, a Sāṅkhya made subordinate to devotion, and in its eleventh book the Uddhava Gītā, Kṛṣṇa’s last teaching before he leaves the world. The Kūrma Purāṇa contains the Īśvara Gītā, Śiva’s discourse on knowledge of the self and on yoga, and the Devī Bhāgavata the Devī Gītā, in which the Goddess teaches a Śākta Vedānta; all three are modelled on the Bhagavad Gītā (Table 6.6).

    11.7 The Epics and the Classical Darśanas¶

    The six schools of classical Indian philosophy (ṣaḍ darśana) — Sāṅkhya, Yoga, Nyāya, Vaiśeṣika, Mīmāṃsā and Vedānta — were systematised after, or alongside, the later stages of the epics, and they are represented unequally (Table 11.1).

    Table 11.1. Classical Darśanas and the Epic and Purāṇic Literature
    Darśana Key Source Presence in Epics/Purāṇas
    Sāṅkhya Mokṣadharma; Gītā; Bhāgavata 3 Extensive, in pre-classical and often theistic forms
    Yoga Gītā 6; Mokṣadharma Extensive: meditation, breath control, sense withdrawal; not yet in the form of the Yogasūtra
    Vedānta Gītā; Mokṣadharma; Bhāgavata Upaniṣadic teaching on Brahman and the self, later read by all Vedāntic schools
    Mīmāṃsā Ritual passages; debates on sacrifice The value of Vedic ritual is defended and also criticised by renouncers
    Nyāya Scattered references Reasoning (ānvīkṣikī, hetuśāstra) is known, and sometimes condemned, as when a jackal in the Śānti Parva confesses that he was reborn as a jackal for having been a sceptical logician
    Vaiśeṣika Little trace Not significantly represented

    The Bhagavad Gītā combines Sāṅkhya’s analysis, Yoga’s discipline and Upaniṣadic teaching on Brahman within a theistic framework of devotion to Kṛṣṇa. The epics also preserve, mostly in order to refute them, the views of materialists and sceptics, and they share many ideas and terms with early Buddhism and Jainism.

    11.8 Conceptions of the Divine¶

    The epics and Purāṇas speak of God in several registers at once. The Gītā presents Kṛṣṇa as the absolute beyond all attributes (para brahman; chapters 7, 9 and 15), as the cosmic lord revealed in the universal form (chapter 11), and as the inner controller seated in the hearts of all beings (antaryāmin, 18.61); the Bhāgavata makes him above all bhagavān, the personal god who is the object of love; and the Devī Bhāgavata gives the same supremacy to the Goddess as śakti, the creative power. Commentators of different schools ranked these registers differently — the Advaitins placing the attributeless Brahman highest, the Vaiṣṇava theologians the personal Lord — and the variety of the texts allowed each to find its own God in them (Figure 6.4).
