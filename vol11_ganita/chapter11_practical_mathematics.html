    Chapter 11: Mathematics at Work: Trade, Building and Surveying¶

    The arithmetic books described in Chapter 10 were written for people who had to calculate for a living. Their “determinations” (vyavahāra) turn the operations of arithmetic into procedures for particular trades: the merchant’s proportion and interest, the partner’s share of a profit, the goldsmith’s alloys, the overseer’s wages, the mason’s bricks, the sawyer’s cuts and the surveyor’s fields. This chapter follows those procedures through the texts of Śrīdhara, Mahāvīra and Bhāskara II, with worked examples. It asks also what the problems reveal about the economy they served. The institutions of trade, banking and credit are treated in Volume 19 of this series, Vāṇijya and Samudra, and the practice of architecture in Volume 16, Vāstu–Śilpa; here the subject is the mathematics (Plofker 2009; Datta and Singh 1935).

    11.1 Proportion¶

    11.1.1 The rule of three¶

    No rule was used more often than the rule of three (trairāśika, “the three quantities”). It is stated in a single verse by Āryabhaṭa (Chapter 3) and in every later arithmetic; Bhāskara II’s praise of it was quoted in Chapter 1. The three given quantities have fixed names. The “measure” (pramāṇa) is a known quantity and the “fruit” (phala) is what corresponds to it; the “requisition” (icchā) is the quantity for which a corresponding fruit, the “fruit of the requisition” (icchāphala), is sought. The terms are written in a row in the order measure, fruit, requisition. The first and last must be of the same kind (sajāti), and the answer is the fruit multiplied by the requisition and divided by the measure:

    \[\text{}\mathit{icchāphala}\text{} = \frac{\text{}\mathit{phala}\text{} \times \text{}\mathit{icchā}\text{}}{\text{}\mathit{pramāṇa}\text{}} .\]

    The rule needs no justification beyond proportion, and the texts give none. Its power lay in the discipline of setting the terms down in the right places, which turned a wide range of everyday questions into a single routine.

    Worked example: the rule of three

    If 6 measures of rice cost 45 paṇas, what do 14 measures cost?
    Measure 6, fruit 45, requisition 14:

    \[\frac{45 \times 14}{6} = 105 \text{ }\mathit{paṇa}\text{s.}\]

    The same layout serves an astronomer: if a planet makes a given number of revolutions in the civil days of a yuga, how many does it make in the days elapsed since the epoch? (Chapter 3).

    11.1.2 The inverse rule¶

    When the fruit decreases as the requisition increases, the “inverse rule of three” (vyastatrairāśika) applies: the answer is the fruit multiplied by the measure and divided by the requisition. The texts give a standard list of cases. The price of living beings—slaves, cattle, horses—was assumed to fall as their age rose beyond youth. The weight of gold needed to equal a given value rises as its fineness falls. And the number of measures in a heap of grain falls as the size of the measure rises. Bhāskara II’s example prices a female slave by her age: if one of sixteen years fetches 32 niṣkas, one of twenty fetches \(32 \times 16/20 = 25\tfrac35\) (Colebrooke 1817). The example is a plain reminder that slavery was part of the economy these books served, and that the arithmetic of the market applied to human beings as to other goods; the legal and social side of the matter is treated in Volume 18, Rājanīti and Dharmaśāstra.

    Worked example: the inverse rule

    A heap of grain measures 120 when measured with a vessel holding 7 units. How many times will a vessel holding 8 units be filled?
    The larger the vessel, the fewer the fillings:

    \[\frac{120 \times 7}{8} = 105 .\]

    11.1.3 Compound proportion¶

    Many questions involve more than one ratio: the interest on a sum depends on both the principal and the time, the price of cloth on the number of pieces, their length and their breadth. Indian arithmetic handled these by the “rule of five” (pañcarāśika), “of seven” (saptarāśika), “of nine” (navarāśika) and so on, always with an odd number of terms. The data are written in two columns, one for the known case and one for the case sought, with the fruit at the foot of the known column. The fruit is then moved to the other column (“transposition of the fruits”), and the product of the column with more terms is divided by the product of the column with fewer. The procedure is a mechanical form of the reasoning that the answer is proportional to each factor in turn (Sarma 2002; Shukla 1959).

    Worked example: the rule of seven

    If 3 pieces of cloth, each 8 cubits long and 2 cubits wide, cost 60 paṇas, what do 5 pieces, each 12 cubits long and 3 cubits wide, cost?

    Known case Case sought
    pieces 3 5
    length 8 12
    breadth 2 3
    price 60 —

    Moving the price to the second column, the answer is

    \[\frac{5 \times 12 \times 3 \times 60}{3 \times 8 \times 2} = \frac{10800}{48} = 225 \text{ }\mathit{paṇa}\text{s}.\]

    11.1.4 Barter¶

    Barter (bhāṇḍapratibhāṇḍa, “goods for goods”) is treated as a special case of compound proportion. Two commodities have known prices in money, and the question is how much of one should be exchanged for a given quantity of the other. The money cancels out, and the rule of five gives the answer directly.

    Worked example: barter

    Mangoes sell at 3 for one paṇa, and pomegranates at 5 for two paṇas. How many pomegranates should be given for 30 mangoes?
    Thirty mangoes are worth \(30/3 = 10\) paṇas, which buy \(10 \times \tfrac52 = 25\) pomegranates. In the form of the rule of five:

    \[\frac{30 \times 1 \times 5}{3 \times 2} = 25 .\]

    11.1.5 The travels of the rule¶

    The rule of three is found in the Chinese Nine Chapters and in Indian texts from Āryabhaṭa onward; it may have been found independently in both. The Indian form, with its named terms and its extension to five, seven and more quantities, passed to the Islamic world: al-Bīrūnī wrote a treatise on the Indian rules of proportion, and the Arabic and Persian arithmetics treated the rule of three as a basic method. From them it reached medieval Europe, where merchant arithmetics called it the “golden rule” and taught it, together with the rules of five and seven, until the nineteenth century. S. R. Sarma has traced the Indian versions and their variants in detail (Sarma 2002; Plofker 2009).

    11.2 Interest and Loans¶

    11.2.1 Terms and rates¶

    The arithmetic books use a small vocabulary for loans. The principal is the “root” (mūla), the interest the “growth” (vṛddhi) or “increase over time” (kalāntara), and the principal and interest together the “mixture” (miśra). Rates are stated as so much on a hundred for a month—“five on a hundred each month”—and problems are solved with the rule of five, the five known quantities being the principal, time and interest of the stated rate and the principal and time of the case sought. Rates of this size (5 per cent a month is 60 per cent a year) look high to a modern reader, but they are typical of the problem collections, which were not concerned with the rates actually charged in any one market.

    The legal texts also fixed rates. The Manusmṛti, for example, allows monthly interest of two, three, four and five per hundred according to the borrower’s varṇa, in descending order of rank (8.142), so that the lower the borrower’s status, the higher the lawful rate. The arithmetic books do not discuss the law; their rates are those convenient for calculation. The history of credit and of the regulation of interest is treated in Volumes 18 and 19.

    Given any three of principal, rate, time and interest, the fourth follows by proportion. The texts go further and pose problems in which the data are mixed: the principal and interest are known only in sum, or several sums lent at different rates are compared.

    Worked example: principal from the “mixture”

    A sum lent at 5 per hundred a month amounts, with its interest, to 900 paṇas after 10 months. What was the principal?
    In 10 months a principal of 100 earns 50, so 100 becomes 150. By the rule of three, the principal of 900 is \(100 \times 900 / 150 = 600\), and the interest is 300.

    Worked example: equal amounts at different rates

    A total of 1770 paṇas is divided into three parts, lent for 10 months at 2, 3 and 5 per hundred a month respectively. At the end, the three amounts (principal with interest) are equal. Find the parts.
    In 10 months each 100 becomes 120, 130 and 150 respectively, so the parts must be proportional to \(\tfrac{1}{120} : \tfrac{1}{130} : \tfrac{1}{150}\), that is, to \(65 : 60 : 52\), whose sum is 177. The parts are therefore \(1770 \times \tfrac{65}{177} = 650\), \(600\) and \(520\).
    Check. \(650 \times 1.2 = 600 \times 1.3 = 520 \times 1.5 = 780\).

    Problems of this second kind are a variant of the “proportional division” described below; Mahāvīra gives many of them, including problems in which the times differ instead of the rates. Interest on interest appears already in the Āryabhaṭīya (Chapter 3), where it leads to a quadratic equation. Instalment loans, in which a debt is paid off by equal payments at fixed intervals, also appear in the problem collections, though their treatment varies from author to author (Rangācārya 1912; Datta and Singh 1935).

    11.3 Buying, Selling and Repayment¶

    Problems of purchase and sale (krayavikraya) combine proportion with profit and loss. Goods are bought at one rate and sold at another, often with prices quoted as “so many for so much”—the commonest form of a price in a market without fixed units of packaging—and the question is the profit, the capital needed for a given profit, or the quantity to be traded. Such problems require the merchant to bring two rates to a common measure, which the rule of five does automatically.

    Worked example: buying and selling at quoted rates

    A merchant buys mangoes at 8 for 5 paṇas and sells them at 6 for 5 paṇas. How many must he trade to make a profit of 25 paṇas?
    One mango costs \(\tfrac58\) and sells for \(\tfrac56\), a profit of \(\tfrac56 - \tfrac58 = \tfrac{20 - 15}{24} = \tfrac{5}{24}\) paṇa each. A profit of 25 needs \(25 \div \tfrac{5}{24} = 120\) mangoes, bought for 75 and sold for 100 paṇas.

    Money itself had to be converted. Coins of different issues, mints and weights circulated together, and their value depended on their metal content, not only on their names. Money-changers (Volume 19) exchanged them at a discount that reflected both the difference in fineness and their own charge. The arithmetic is a chain of proportions: the coins are first valued in a standard unit, and the changer’s commission is then deducted as a fraction of the result.

    Worked example: exchanging coins

    A trader brings 240 coins of an issue reckoned at \(\tfrac{15}{16}\) of the standard coin. The changer charges 1 in every 75 for the exchange. How many standard coins does the trader receive?
    The coins are worth \(240 \times \tfrac{15}{16} = 225\) standard coins; the commission is \(\tfrac{225}{75} = 3\); the trader receives 222.

    Repayment of a debt by instalments that grow in a regular way leads to arithmetic progressions, and so to the rules for series. If the first payment and the increase are known, the number of payments needed to clear a given sum is the number of terms of a progression with that sum, found by Āryabhaṭa’s rule (Chapter 3). The same rules served for donations made on successive days of a festival, for the daily journeys of travellers and for courses of bricks that grow narrower towards the top of a stepped structure.

    Worked example: payments in progression

    A debt of 425 paṇas is repaid by monthly payments of 20 in the first month, 25 in the second, 30 in the third, and so on. How many months are needed?
    With first term \(a = 20\), difference \(d = 5\) and sum \(S = 425\), Āryabhaṭa’s rule gives

    \[\begin{align*} n &= \frac12\left[\frac{\sqrt{8dS + (2a - d)^2} - 2a}{d} + 1\right] = \frac12\left[\frac{\sqrt{17000 + 1225} - 40}{5} + 1\right]\\ &= \frac12\left[\frac{135 - 40}{5} + 1\right] = 10 . \end{align*}\]

    Check: the payments run from 20 to 65, and \(\tfrac{10}{2}(20 + 65) = 425\).

    11.4 Partnership and Proportional Division¶

    When several merchants pool their capital, the profit is divided in proportion to what each contributed. The rule is called prakṣepaka, from the word for a contribution or “throw-in”. The same rule divides an inheritance in given ratios, a wage among workers by the days each worked, or a cost among users in proportion to their use. When the contributions were made for different lengths of time, each is multiplied by its time before the division.

    Worked example: partnership

    Equal times. Three merchants contribute 40, 60 and 100 niṣkas, and the profit is 250. The shares are \(250 \times \tfrac{40}{200} = 50\), \(75\) and \(125\).
    Unequal times. Three partners contribute 40 for 12 months, 60 for 8 months and 100 for 3 months, and the profit is 420. The weighted contributions are \(480\), \(480\) and \(300\), with sum 1260, so the shares are \(420 \times \tfrac{480}{1260} = 160\), \(160\) and \(100\).

    Proportional division was also the mathematics of distribution: of a king’s grant among the recipients, of grain among households, of a fine among offenders. The texts treat all these cases with the same rule.

    11.5 Gold, Alloys and Mixtures¶

    11.5.1 The touch of gold¶

    Gold was assayed by its “colour” or “touch” (varṇa), judged by rubbing it on a touchstone and comparing the streak with that of samples of known fineness. Pure gold had sixteen varṇas, and lower fineness was expressed as fewer varṇas; gold of 12 varṇas, on this scale, is three-quarters pure. The arithmetic books have a section on gold (suvarṇagaṇita) in which the touch of a mixture, the weight of an unknown component or the loss in refining is found. The mathematics is that of weighted averages, and the same rules apply to mixtures of grain or oil at different prices. The working practice of goldsmiths and assayers, and the treatises on the testing of coins and metals, are described in Volumes 14 (Rasāyana) and 17 (Śilpa–Kāru).

    Worked example: the touch of an alloy

    Ten māṣas of gold of 13 varṇas, 4 of 12 varṇas and 2 of 14 varṇas are melted together. What is the touch of the alloy?
    Multiply each weight by its touch and divide the sum by the total weight:

    \[\frac{10\cdot 13 + 4\cdot 12 + 2\cdot 14}{10 + 4 + 2} = \frac{130 + 48 + 28}{16} = \frac{206}{16} = 12\tfrac78 \text{ }\mathit{varṇa}\text{s}.\]

    Worked example: an unknown weight, and refining

    Unknown weight. How much pure gold (16 varṇas) must be added to 12 māṣas of 10 varṇas to make gold of 12 varṇas?
    If \(x\) is added, \(12 \cdot 10 + 16x = 12(12 + x)\), so \(4x = 24\) and \(x = 6\). The texts state the rule without the unknown: the deficit of the base gold below the desired touch, multiplied by its weight, divided by the excess of the added gold over the desired touch, \(\tfrac{(12 - 10)\times 12}{16 - 12} = 6\).
    Refining. Twenty māṣas of gold of 12 varṇas are refined and weigh 16 māṣas afterwards. Since the amount of pure gold, \(20 \times 12\) in units of varṇa-weight, is unchanged, the new touch is \(240/16 = 15\) varṇas.

    11.5.2 Combinations of goods¶

    The section on mixtures also contains the combinatorial problems mentioned in Chapter 10: how many different dishes can be made from six tastes taken one, two, three or more at a time; how many necklaces from gems of several kinds. These belong to the same practical world as the alloys—the cook’s and the jeweller’s—and they show the arithmetic books treating counting as one of the merchant’s tools (Chapter 8).

    11.6 Work, Wages, Channels and Journeys¶

    A group of problems concerns rates of work. Workers who complete a task in different times, channels that fill a tank at different rates, and travellers who move at different speeds are all treated by adding rates, not times. The “filling of a tank” (vāpīpūraṇa) is a standard example: if several channels, running alone, would fill a tank in given times, the fractions of the tank filled by each in a unit of time are added, and the reciprocal of the sum is the time taken by all together.

    Worked example: channels and workers

    Channels. Three channels would fill a tank in 3, 4 and 6 hours respectively. In one hour together they fill \(\tfrac13 + \tfrac14 + \tfrac16 = \tfrac34\) of the tank, so together they fill it in \(\tfrac43\) hours, that is 1 hour and 20 minutes.
    Workers. Five labourers dig a channel 40 cubits long in 8 days. How long will 4 labourers take to dig 60 cubits? The time varies inversely with the number of workers and directly with the length: \(8 \times \tfrac54 \times \tfrac{60}{40} = 15\) days. In the Indian layout this is a rule of five with the workers placed in the opposite columns.

    Wage problems were often combined with partnership: when a team of workers of different skills is paid a lump sum, the payment is divided in proportion to each worker’s daily rate multiplied by the days worked. These problems give glimpses of the labour market, with its differential rates for skilled and unskilled work, although the numbers are those of the classroom, not of any one place and time.

    11.7 Mensuration for Builders and Surveyors¶

    The rules of plane and solid mensuration were collected in Chapter 9 (Table 9.3). The arithmetic books give these rules a practical setting. They distinguish “practical” or “gross” rules, good enough for the field and easy to apply, from “accurate” rules, and they sometimes give both for the same figure. The pairing is instructive: it shows that the authors knew the gross rules to be approximate, and it lets us see the trade-off they made between simplicity and accuracy.

    11.7.1 Fields¶

    For a quadrilateral field the gross rule multiplies the half-sums of opposite sides. This is the “surveyor’s rule” found in many ancient and medieval traditions, from Mesopotamia and Egypt to the Roman land surveyors; it is exact for a rectangle and too large for every other quadrilateral. Brahmagupta’s accurate rule, \(\sqrt{(s-a)(s-b)(s-c)(s-d)}\), is exact for a quadrilateral inscribed in a circle (Chapter 4); Bhāskara II warned that for other quadrilaterals the sides alone do not fix the area, so that a diagonal or a perpendicular must also be measured (Chapter 5).

    Worked example: the gross and accurate areas of a field

    A field is a trapezium with parallel sides of 12 and 8 rods and the other two sides each 5 rods.
    Gross rule. \(\tfrac{12 + 8}{2} \times \tfrac{5 + 5}{2} = 10 \times 5 = 50\) square rods.
    Accurate rule. The trapezium is symmetrical, and so can be inscribed in a circle. With \(s = \tfrac12(12 + 8 + 5 + 5) = 15\), Brahmagupta’s rule gives \(\sqrt{3 \cdot 7 \cdot 10 \cdot 10} = \sqrt{2100} \approx 45.83\) square rods.
    Check. The height of the trapezium is \(\sqrt{5^2 - 2^2} = \sqrt{21} \approx 4.583\), and its area is \(10 \times 4.583 = 45.83\). The gross rule is about 9% too large.

    In practice, land was often measured by quantities other than area. Copper-plate grants of the Gupta period and later from Bengal, for example, measure land in kulyavāpas and droṇavāpas, units defined by the quantity of seed needed to sow it. Elsewhere, standard rods and ropes of locally defined length were used. The texts’ rules of mensuration had to be applied within these systems of measure, whose values varied from place to place (Chapter 1).

    11.7.2 Excavations¶

    A pit, a tank or a foundation trench whose top and bottom are rectangles of different sizes has the shape of a frustum. Brahmagupta computed its volume in three steps. The “practical” volume \(V_p\) is found from the mean dimensions: the average of the top and bottom lengths times the average of the widths, times the depth. The “gross” volume \(V_g\) is the average of the top and bottom areas times the depth. The “accurate” volume is then

    \[V = V_p + \frac{V_g - V_p}{3} .\]

    Mahāvīra gives the same procedure, with his own names for the two preliminary volumes. The result is exact, as the following example shows. Bhāskara II’s single formula for the same volume was given in Chapter 5 (Colebrooke 1817; Rangācārya 1912; Sarasvati Amma 1979).

    Worked example: a tank with sloping sides

    A tank is square, 10 cubits on a side at the top and 6 at the bottom, and 3 cubits deep.
    Practical volume. The mean side is 8, so \(V_p = 8^2 \times 3 = 192\).
    Gross volume. The mean area is \(\tfrac12(100 + 36) = 68\), so \(V_g = 68 \times 3 = 204\).
    Accurate volume. \(V = 192 + \tfrac{204 - 192}{3} = 196\) cubic cubits.
    Check. The exact volume of the frustum is \(\tfrac{h}{3}(a^2 + ab + b^2) = 1 \times (100 + 60 + 36) = 196\). In general \(V_p + \tfrac13(V_g - V_p) = \tfrac13(2V_p + V_g)\), and expanding \(2\left(\tfrac{a+b}{2}\right)^2 + \tfrac{a^2 + b^2}{2}\) gives \(a^2 + ab + b^2\), so the rule is exact for every such frustum.

    For a pit of irregular depth the texts take the average of the depths measured at several places and multiply it by the area: a gross rule, but a sensible one. Wells were computed as cylinders, with the area of the circular section found by whatever value of the ratio of circumference to diameter the author used.

    Worked example: a well

    A circular well has a diameter of 4 cubits and a depth of 10 cubits. Its volume is the area of the circle times the depth. With the circumference taken as three times the diameter, the area is \(\tfrac14 \times 12 \times 4 = 12\) and the volume 120 cubic cubits; with \(\sqrt{10}\) the volume is \(10\sqrt{10} \times 4 \approx 126.5\); the true volume is \(40\pi \approx 125.7\). The “gross” value is about 4.5% too small, the Jain value about 0.7% too large.

    11.7.3 Bricks and stones¶

    The section on piles (citi) computes the number of bricks in a wall or platform: the volume of the structure divided by the volume of one brick. The number of courses is the height divided by the thickness of a brick. The rules are trivial in themselves; what they show is the use of arithmetic in planning and costing a building, a theme that the vāstu texts confirm (Volume 16).

    Worked example: a brick wall

    A wall is 20 cubits long, \(1\tfrac12\) cubits thick and 6 cubits high. Each brick measures 1 cubit by \(\tfrac12\) cubit by \(\tfrac14\) cubit. The wall contains \(20 \times 1\tfrac12 \times 6 = 180\) cubic cubits, and each brick \(\tfrac18\) cubic cubit, so \(180 \times 8 = 1440\) bricks are needed, laid in \(6 \div \tfrac14 = 24\) courses of 60 bricks each.

    11.7.4 Sawing¶

    The sawyer was paid by the area of the cuts he made (Chapter 4): each lengthwise cut through a log has an area equal to the length of the log times its thickness, and the total area is that of one cut times the number of cuts. A rate per unit of area then gives the wage by the rule of three. For a log that tapers, the thickness is averaged.

    Worked example: the sawyer’s wage

    A log 16 cubits long and \(1\tfrac12\) cubits thick is to be cut lengthwise into six planks. Five cuts are needed, each of area \(16 \times 1\tfrac12 = 24\) square cubits, so the total is 120 square cubits. If the rate is 2 paṇas for every 8 square cubits, the wage is \(120 \times \tfrac28 = 30\) paṇas.

    11.7.5 Heaps against walls and in corners¶

    The rule for a conical heap of grain on open ground, \((C/6)^2 h\) for circumference \(C\) and height \(h\), was given in Chapter 4. Grain was often heaped against a wall or in the corner of a storeroom, so that only part of the base circumference could be measured. The texts handle these cases by completing the heap in imagination. For a heap against a wall the measured arc is half the full circumference: the arc is doubled, the volume of the full heap is computed, and the result is halved. A heap in an inside corner is a quarter of a full heap, and one around an outside corner three-quarters, with the arc multiplied by four, or by four-thirds, accordingly.

    Worked example: a heap in a corner

    A heap of grain stands in the inside corner of a storeroom. Its curved edge measures 15 cubits and its height is 4 cubits. The full heap would have a circumference of \(4 \times 15 = 60\) and a volume of \((60/6)^2 \times 4 = 400\) cubic cubits; the heap in the corner is a quarter of this, 100 cubic cubits.
    The same reasoning gives, for an arc \(c\) against a wall, \(\tfrac12 (2c/6)^2 h = c^2 h/18\), and for an arc \(c\) in an inside corner, \(\tfrac14 (4c/6)^2 h = c^2 h/9\).

    11.7.6 Heights and distances¶

    Shadows and gnomons (chāyā) form the last determination. Their mathematics—similar triangles—was described in Chapters 3 and 9, and their use in astronomy and in the orientation of buildings belongs to Volumes 12 and 16. The astronomers also described simple sighting instruments; Bhāskara II’s “instrument of the intellect” (dhīyantra), a graduated staff used to find the height and distance of an object from a single position, is described in Volume 12, Khagola Jyotiṣa. In the arithmetic books the shadow problems are mostly exercises in proportion: a lamp, a gnomon and its shadow; two gnomons and their shadows; the height of a tree from the length of its shadow and that of a staff.

    11.8 How Accurate Were the Practical Rules?¶

    Because the texts so often give a “gross” and an “accurate” rule side by side, it is possible to ask how large the errors of the gross rules were, and whether they mattered for the purposes in hand. Table 11.1 collects the examples worked in this chapter and in earlier ones, with the error of each rule computed against the exact value.

    Table 11.1. Errors of some practical rules, computed for the examples in this volume
    Rule Example Error
    Circumference \(= 3 \times\) diameter any circle \(-4.5\%\)
    Circumference \(= \sqrt{10} \times\) diameter (Jain; Brahmagupta’s “accurate”) any circle \(+0.66\%\)
    Āryabhaṭa’s \(\pi \approx 3.1416\) any circle \(+0.0002\%\)
    Arc of a segment \(\approx \sqrt{6h^2 + c^2}\) (Jain) \(d = 10\), \(h = 2\) (Chapter 2) \(+1.2\%\)
    Quadrilateral: product of half-sums of opposite sides trapezium 12, 8, 5, 5 \(+9.1\%\)
    Frustum: “practical” volume from mean sides top 10, bottom 6, depth 3 \(-2.0\%\)
    Frustum: “gross” volume from mean areas same \(+4.1\%\)
    Conical heap: \((C/6)^2 h\) any cone \(+4.7\%\)
    Oval perimeter \(\approx \sqrt{6B^2 + 4L^2}\) (Mahāvīra) ellipse \(10 \times 6\) (Chapter 10) \(-2.8\%\)

    Three points stand out. First, the gross rules were wrong by a few per cent, an error comparable to the uncertainty of measuring a field with a rope or the depth of a pit with a rod, and smaller than the variation between local units of measure; for most practical purposes they were adequate. Second, the two gross rules for the frustum err in opposite directions, and Brahmagupta’s accurate rule combines them in exactly the proportion that cancels the errors. It is hard to believe that this was found by chance, although the texts do not say how it was found. Third, the largest error in the table is that of the surveyor’s rule for quadrilaterals, which systematically overstates the area of any field that is not a rectangle. Where land was taxed by area, such an error had economic consequences, whichever party it favoured; Bhāskara II’s warning that the area of a quadrilateral cannot be found from its sides alone shows that the mathematicians, at least, were aware of the problem.

    11.9 What the Problems Reveal¶

    Read together, the determinations give a picture of the economy that the arithmetic books served—a picture that is real but must be read with care. The commodities are those of a commercial society: rice and other grain, cloth, spices and perfumes, gold, gems, horses, elephants, cattle and slaves. Money is used everywhere, in a hierarchy of units from the cowrie to the gold niṣka (Chapter 1), and credit at interest is taken for granted. Partnerships, wage labour and contract work for building and sawing all appear. The texts thus confirm, from an unusual angle, what inscriptions and legal texts say about a monetised economy with merchants’ associations and moneylenders.

    The limits of this evidence are equally clear. The numbers are chosen to make the arithmetic come out, not to record prices; the same problems pass from book to book across centuries and regions; and the poetic framing, with its kings and lovers, is literary. The problems tell us what kinds of calculation were expected of an educated man of affairs, not what any particular merchant paid. Women appear in them mostly as addressees or as figures in the stories, and the labourers, sawyers and slaves of the examples are objects of calculation, not its practitioners. The people who actually did most of this arithmetic—merchants’ clerks, village accountants, masons—learned it in the schools described in Chapter 13, and mostly in the regional languages.

    11.10 Assessment¶

    The practical mathematics of the arithmetic books is not where Indian mathematics was most original; proportion, interest, alloys and areas were calculated in every literate commercial society. What is distinctive is the way the material was organised. Problems were sorted into named classes, each with a rule stated in memorable verse; the rule of three was extended in a uniform way to any number of terms; and gross and accurate rules were set side by side, so that a user could choose and a reader could see the difference. Some results, such as Brahmagupta’s accurate volume of a frustum and the treatment of heaps in corners, show care in turning geometry into usable procedure. This organisation travelled: the rules of proportion and the problem types of the Indian books passed to the Islamic world and, through it, to the merchant arithmetics of Europe.

    Exercises¶

    1. If 8 cubits of cloth cost 36 paṇas, what do 30 cubits cost?

    2. If 12 workers build a wall in 15 days, how long will 20 workers take?

    3. A principal of 400 is lent at 3 per hundred a month. After how many months will principal and interest together amount to 568?

    4. Two merchants contribute 120 for 5 months and 150 for 6 months. Divide a profit of 330 between them.

    5. How many māṣas of gold of 9 varṇas must be added to 8 māṣas of 15 varṇas to produce gold of 13 varṇas?

    6. A pit is 12 cubits square at the top and 8 at the bottom, and 6 cubits deep. Find its practical, gross and accurate volumes.

    7. A heap of grain against a wall has a curved edge of 18 cubits and a height of 3 cubits. Find its volume by the texts’ rule.

    Answers. (1) \(36 \times 30/8 = 135\). (2) \(15 \times 12/20 = 9\) days. (3) The interest is 168; a month’s interest is 12, so 14 months. (4) Weighted contributions 600 and 900: shares 132 and 198. (5) \(x\) satisfies \(9x + 120 = 13(8 + x)\), so \(4x = 16\) and \(x = 4\). (6) Practical \(10^2 \times 6 = 600\); gross \(\tfrac12(144 + 64) \times 6 = 624\); accurate \(600 + 8 = 608\), which is the exact volume \(\tfrac63(144 + 96 + 64) = 608\). (7) \(18^2 \times 3/18 = 54\) cubic cubits.
