    Preface¶

    This is Volume 14 of the series Bhāratīya Jñāna Paramparā: Rasāyana: Chemistry and Metallurgy. It surveys the history of chemical and metallurgical knowledge in the Indian subcontinent. It opens with the sources and methods of the subject and with the alchemical and iatrochemical literature of rasaśāstra, which grew up from about the end of the first millennium CE, heard where possible in its own words. It then turns to the mines from which the metals came and the archaeometallurgical methods by which their remains are studied, before following the metals themselves: the iron of the Delhi pillar and of the village smelters, the crucible steel known in Europe as “wootz”, the distillation of zinc at Zawar, and the precious and base metals, with the assay and refining of the Delhi and Mughal mints. Later chapters treat calcined metal medicines (bhasma) as history and as a problem of toxicology; gems; dyes and pigments; glass, ceramics and building materials; the salts, alkalis and acids—common salt, saltpetre, alum, sal ammoniac, borax—that were the everyday reagents of the crafts, together with sugar; and the Islamic alchemy (kīmiyā) of the Persianate courts, its meeting with rasaśāstra, and the history of distillation, from arrack to attar. A last chapter considers the legacy of the tradition.

    The volume is an edited and corrected version of an earlier draft. In revision, verses that could not be verified as genuine, with a correct reference, were removed rather than repaired; the short passages from the Rasārṇava, the Rasahṛdayatantra and the Rasaratnasamuccaya now quoted in Chapters 2 and 3 were checked against the electronic texts of printed editions named in the bibliography. Invented “studies” and unsourced modern measurements were deleted; dates are given as the range accepted in current scholarship (with traditional dates labelled as such); and claims that ancient India was “first” in this or that field were checked against the archaeological and historical literature and corrected or removed. Where the classical texts make claims about rejuvenation, transmutation or cures, these are reported as what the texts say, not as established facts.

    Two cautions apply throughout. First, this is a book of history. It describes what the rasa texts say about the processing of mercury, lead, arsenic, copper and other metals and minerals, but it deliberately gives no doses, proportions, durations or working recipes. Products made by these methods have repeatedly been found to contain toxic quantities of lead, mercury and arsenic, and they have caused serious poisoning; the evidence, including studies that defend these products, is set out in Chapter 9. Nothing in this volume is medical advice. Second, the dating of many Sanskrit technical texts is uncertain, and the attribution of alchemical works to famous names (Nāgārjuna above all) is a matter of tradition rather than history.

    The work of P. C. Ray, David Gordon White, G. J. Meulenbeld and Dominik Wujastyk on the rasa literature, of R. Balasubramaniam on the Delhi pillar, of Sharada Srinivasan and S. Ranganathan on wootz, and of Paul Craddock and his colleagues on Zawar has been drawn on throughout and is listed in the select bibliography, together with a glossary, a chronology and a guide to further reading. Every quotation and every technical claim should be checked against the editions and studies cited before scholarly use.

    Nikhil Kumar Rajput
