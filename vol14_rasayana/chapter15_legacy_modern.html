    Chapter 15: Legacy: From Rasāyana to Modern Chemistry¶

    15.1 Taking Stock¶

    The preceding chapters have described a long and varied history. Its most secure achievements, established by archaeology and laboratory analysis, are practical ones:

    • the copper, bronze, faience, stoneware and bead technologies of the Harappan cities;

    • an early, probably independent, iron industry, and the forge-welding of massive iron objects such as the Delhi pillar, whose slow corrosion is now well understood;

    • crucible steel, made in south India from at least the last centuries BCE, and later in Sri Lanka, and traded across western Asia;

    • the earliest known industrial-scale distillation of zinc, at Zawar;

    • high-tin bronzes, lost-wax casting and the Chola bronzes;

    • mineral-soda-alumina glass and the Indo-Pacific bead industry;

    • indigo vat dyeing and mordant printing and painting of cotton, which shaped world trade in textiles;

    • the production and refining of salts—saltpetre above all, but also alkalis, alum, sal ammoniac and salt itself—on which much of the craft economy, and for two centuries much of Europe’s gunpowder, depended.

    Alongside these stands the Sanskrit literature of rasaśāstra, with its laboratory, apparatus, classifications of minerals and long sequences of operations on mercury and metals. It is an important chapter in the global history of alchemy, comparable to the Chinese and Arabic traditions. Its theoretical goals—transmutation and bodily immortality—were unattainable, and its medical legacy is, as earlier chapters have shown, a serious contemporary safety problem.

    Two cautions follow. First, the achievements were mostly those of craft communities, not of textual scholars; the people who smelted the iron and dyed the cloth were often of low social status and left no writings. Second, much popular writing on these subjects is marred by exaggeration—claims of absolute priority, of anticipations of modern science, of “lost” secrets. The record is impressive enough without them.

    15.2 P. C. Ray and the Historiography of Indian Chemistry¶

    The modern study of Indian chemistry began with Prafulla Chandra Ray (1861–1944), a chemist at Presidency College, Calcutta, who founded the firm that became Bengal Chemical and Pharmaceutical Works (incorporated 1901) and is remembered in chemistry for his work on mercurous nitrite and related compounds. His History of Hindu Chemistry (two volumes, 1902 and 1909) surveyed Sanskrit medical and alchemical literature, with extracts and translations, and argued that India had possessed a genuine chemical tradition (Ray 1902–1909). A revised edition, edited by Priyadaranjan Ray, appeared in 1956 (Ray 1956).

    Ray’s work was written in a nationalist spirit, but it was not uncritical. He attributed the decline of Indian chemistry in part to the caste system, which separated learning from manual work and left practical knowledge to groups whom the learned despised, and to philosophical attitudes that devalued the study of the material world. Some of his datings of rasa texts were too early and have since been revised, and some of his attributions (notably of a Rasaratnākara to Nāgārjuna) have been shown to be mistaken (Wujastyk 1984; Meulenbeld 1999–2002). His work nevertheless remains the starting point for the subject, and his insistence on the social conditions of science is as relevant now as it was then.

    Later scholarship has moved in several directions: the bibliographical and philological work of Meulenbeld and others on the rasa texts (Meulenbeld 1999–2002); the study of rasaśāstra as part of Tantric religion, above all by David Gordon White (White 1996); the archaeometallurgical work of D. P. Agrawal, R. Balasubramaniam, Sharada Srinivasan, S. Ranganathan, Paul Craddock, Gill Juleff, Thilo Rehren and many others (Agrawal 2000; Balasubramaniam 2002; Srinivasan and Ranganathan 2004; Craddock 2017; Juleff 1998); and the economic and social history of Indian crafts and trade (Riello 2013).

    15.3 Modern Chemistry and Metallurgy in India¶

    Modern chemistry came to India in the 19th century through colonial education and administration: chemistry was taught at the medical colleges and the new universities of Calcutta, Bombay and Madras (founded 1857), and the Geological Survey of India (1851) and the colonial mints and arsenals employed European chemists and metallurgists. Indian chemists of the first generation, P. C. Ray foremost among them, combined research with the conviction that chemistry should serve Indian industry; Ray’s Bengal Chemical and Pharmaceutical Works was one of the first Indian-owned chemical firms.

    The early 20th century saw the foundation of the Indian Institute of Science in Bangalore (1909), whose creation owed much to the initiative of J. N. Tata, and of the Tata Iron and Steel Company at Jamshedpur, which began production in 1912 (Chapter 5). Indian chemists such as Nil Ratan Dhar, Jnan Chandra Ghosh, Shanti Swarup Bhatnagar and T. R. Seshadri built research schools; Bhatnagar became the first director-general of the Council of Scientific and Industrial Research (CSIR), founded in 1942, whose national laboratories—among them the National Metallurgical Laboratory at Jamshedpur and the National Chemical Laboratory at Pune—shaped the post-independence development of chemistry and materials science.

    15.3.1 Ray’s Laboratory and the Calcutta School¶

    Ray’s own career shows how the new science was made Indian. Born in 1861 in a landholding family of Khulna district in eastern Bengal, he studied in Calcutta and then, with a scholarship, at the University of Edinburgh, where he took his doctorate in chemistry in 1887. On his return he found the senior grade of the Indian Educational Service effectively closed to Indians, and he joined Presidency College in 1889 in a junior post, an experience of discrimination that he recalled with some bitterness in his autobiography (Ray 1932; Arnold 2000). At Presidency College he built up a research laboratory with very limited means. His work on the nitrites, beginning with the preparation of mercurous nitrite in 1896, was published in Calcutta and in London and gave him an international reputation.

    Ray also set out to show that Indians could manufacture the chemicals and medicines that India imported. In 1892 he began making pharmaceutical preparations, with a small capital, in his own lodgings in Calcutta; the venture was incorporated in 1901 as the Bengal Chemical and Pharmaceutical Works, which moved to a factory in the suburbs and grew rapidly in the years of the Swadeshi movement after 1905, when Indian-made goods were deliberately preferred. It made medicines from Indian plants as well as standard pharmacopoeial preparations, laboratory chemicals and household products. Ray’s purpose was as much social as commercial: he repeatedly urged the educated Bengali middle class to turn from government service and the law to industry, and he saw the firm as a demonstration that chemistry could be the basis of Indian enterprise (Ray 1932).

    His most lasting creation was a school of chemists. Among his students at Presidency College and, after 1916, at the new University College of Science in Calcutta, where he became the first Palit Professor of Chemistry, were Nil Ratan Dhar, Jnan Chandra Ghosh, Jnanendra Nath Mukherjee and Priyadaranjan Ray, who between them founded research traditions in physical, colloid and inorganic chemistry at Calcutta, Dacca, Allahabad and Bangalore; the physicist Meghnad Saha also attended his lectures. In 1924 Ray became the first president of the Indian Chemical Society, whose journal gave Indian chemists a forum of their own. The History of Hindu Chemistry belongs to the same project. It was written, in part, to give Indian students of a European science a history of their own, and it presented the Sanskrit tradition both as evidence of past achievement and, through its account of caste and the contempt for manual work, as an explanation of later decline (Ray 1902–1909; Prakash 1999).

    The Calcutta school shaped the teaching of chemistry in India for half a century. Its graduates staffed the chemistry departments of the new universities, and the institutions in which they worked—the University College of Science, the Indian Institute of Science, the national laboratories of the CSIR—trained the chemists and metallurgists who, after 1947, carried out much of the analytical work on Indian iron, steel, zinc, glass and medicines on which this volume relies (Kumar 1995; Arnold 2000).

    This modern history is linked to the older one in two ways. Some of the earliest work in the history of Indian chemistry was done by modern Indian chemists, notably Ray, who saw it as part of a national scientific revival. And it was modern laboratory science, including Indian laboratories, that made possible the archaeometallurgical research on which much of this volume depends.

    15.4 From Salts to Soda Ash: The Modern Chemical Industry¶

    The substances discussed in Chapter 13—saltpetre, borax, alum, alkalis, salt—were, in the 19th century, among India’s exports of raw materials, along with indigo, lac and the myrobalans used in European tanning. Manufactured chemicals, by contrast, were largely imported: soda ash, caustic soda, acids, synthetic dyes, drugs and fine chemicals came from Britain and, increasingly, Germany. Indian chemical manufacture in the modern sense began on a small scale at the end of the century, with ventures such as Ray’s Bengal Chemical and Pharmaceutical Works, and with government laboratories that made vaccines and sera, such as the Plague Research Laboratory set up in Bombay in 1899 under Waldemar Haffkine (Arnold 2000).

    The modern successors of the old salt economy grew out of this dependence. The First World War, which cut off German dyes and drugs, led the Indian Industrial Commission (1916–1918) to recommend government support for industrial research (Arnold 2000; Kumar 1995), and the inter-war decades saw the first Indian firms in several branches of the industry: factory soap made from vegetable oils and caustic soda gradually replaced the washermen’s sajjī, reh and soapnut (Chapter 13). From 1939 Tata Chemicals built a works at Mithapur in Gujarat to make soda ash by the ammonia–soda (Solvay) process from the two cheapest raw materials of the old salt economy, brine and limestone. The successor of Bihar saltpetre was synthetic nitrogen: the public-sector fertiliser factory at Sindri, producing from 1951, was a showpiece of the first Five-Year Plans, and synthetic ammonia later underpinned the Green Revolution (Volume 15). The pharmaceutical industry that grew after Independence, and the generic manufacturing made possible by the Patents Act of 1970, belong to the economic history of modern India rather than to this volume.

    The modern industry also inherited, on a far larger scale, the dangers that run through this volume. The release of methyl isocyanate from the Union Carbide pesticide plant at Bhopal on the night of 2–3 December 1984, which killed thousands within days, remains the worst disaster in the history of the chemical industry; less dramatic, but widespread, are chromium from tannery effluents, mercury from older chlor-alkali plants and pesticide residues. From the mercury of the rasa laboratory and the lead of the cupelling hearth to Bhopal, the history of Indian chemistry is also a history of the people who bore its costs.

    15.5 The Evidence and How It Is Weighed¶

    The kinds of evidence used in this volume, and what each can show, are summarised in Table 15.1.

    Table 15.1. Kinds of Evidence for the History of Indian Chemistry and Metallurgy
    Kind Examples What it can show
    Artefacts Iron pillars, steel ingots, bronzes, beads, textiles What could be made; composition and structure
    Production debris Slag, crucibles, retorts, kiln remains How and on what scale things were made; dates
    Mining remains Workings at Zawar, Rajpura–Dariba, Khetri, Hutti Sources of raw materials; dates
    Texts Arthaśāstra, gem treatises, rasa texts Classifications, terminology, some processes, ideas
    Observers’ accounts Xuanzang, al-Bīrūnī, Tavernier, Buchanan, Voysey Practices at particular times and places
    Ethnography Elwin on the Agaria; studies of living crafts Practices surviving into modern times
    Laboratory analysis Metallography, chemical and isotopic analysis Composition, microstructure, provenance, date

    The methods by which production debris, mine workings and objects are analysed are explained in Section 4.5. The strongest conclusions come where several kinds of evidence agree—for example, where travellers’ descriptions of crucible steel making, excavated crucibles and the analysis of steel ingots tell the same story. Texts alone are a weak guide to practice, and analysis alone cannot recover the meanings that materials had for their makers.

    15.6 Connections to Modern Chemistry¶

    Did Indian chemical knowledge contribute to modern chemistry? The honest answer is: in specific ways, and mostly indirectly.

    • European study of wootz in the early 19th century contributed to the understanding of carbon in steel (Chapter 6).

    • European attempts to reproduce Indian mordant-dyed cottons stimulated the growth of textile printing and dye chemistry in the 18th century (Chapter 11).

    • Indian indigo was the most important natural dye of the 19th century, and the research that led to its synthesis was central to the German dye industry.

    • Indian zinc, steel, saltpetre, borax and other materials were traded internationally and studied by European chemists; Bihar saltpetre in particular was a principal source of nitre for Britain in the period when European chemists made nitre central to their study of combustion (Chapter 13).

    Modern chemistry, however, developed in 17th–19th-century Europe from its own sources, including European alchemy and pharmacy. There is no evidence that Sanskrit alchemical texts influenced it. Claims that the rasa texts anticipated the conservation of mass, stoichiometry, catalysis or coordination chemistry rest on reading modern concepts into texts that do not contain them.

    15.7 Against Presentism¶

    Several modern ways of describing the Indian tradition deserve particular caution.

    “Ancient nanotechnology”
    Bhasmas and Damascus blades contain very small particles or structures, but their makers did not and could not aim at nanostructures. Calling them nanotechnology misdescribes both the past and the present (Chapters 6 and 9).
    “Green chemistry”
    Traditional processes used renewable fuels and plant materials because those were what was available, and some were highly polluting or dangerous to workers (mercury vapour, lead fumes, charcoal-driven deforestation). They are not models of sustainable industry merely because they are old.
    “Validated by science”
    When modern analysis confirms that the Delhi pillar resists corrosion, it validates a material, not a theory. When analysis shows that a bhasma contains fine particles, it does not validate the claims made for it as a medicine.
    “First in the world”
    Priority claims are often wrong, and always provisional. They also distract attention from what is genuinely interesting: how particular communities solved particular problems with the means available to them.

    15.8 Rasaśāstra Today¶

    The present status of rasaśāstra—as a college subject, an industry and a regulated class of medicines—and the toxicological evidence about its products were set out in Chapter 9. Research in Indian institutions has focused on the characterisation and standardisation of these products and on their safety in animals (Kumar et al. 2006; Chaudhari et al. 2016), and some of it has been critical of quality.

    Health and Safety Note

    Metal-containing traditional medicines remain a recognised cause of lead, mercury and arsenic poisoning in India and abroad (Kales and Saper 2009). Nothing in this book is a recommendation to use them; see the full note in Section 1.1.

    The contemporary debate is often framed as a conflict between “tradition” and “Western science”. It need not be. The authors of the rasa texts were themselves concerned with the dangers of metals and devised tests to guard against them; modern toxicology offers better tests. Practitioners and regulators in India have increasingly accepted that metal-containing products must meet modern safety standards, and that claims of efficacy require properly designed clinical trials. Respect for the tradition is compatible with—indeed requires—subjecting it to the best available evidence.

    15.9 Caste, Gender and the Ownership of Knowledge¶

    The history of Indian chemistry and metallurgy is also a history of who was allowed to know, to write and to profit. The Sanskrit texts were composed by and for literate men of high status: physicians, ascetics, officials, connoisseurs. The smelters, smiths, miners, dyers, potters and bead-makers who carried out most of the processes described in this volume belonged to hereditary occupational groups whose status in the Brahmanical order ranged from respectable to untouchable. Some iron-smelting and leather-working communities were treated as polluting; forest communities such as the Agaria and Asur were marginal to the agrarian order altogether. As P. C. Ray observed, the separation of learning from manual work was one reason why practical knowledge so rarely entered the learned literature (Ray 1902–1909).

    Women’s work is especially poorly recorded. Women took part in many crafts—in mining and ore-dressing (Tavernier saw women and children in the diamond diggings), in bead-making and pottery decoration, in spinning and dyeing, in bangle-making—usually as unpaid family labour or as low-paid workers (Tavernier 1889). In the rasa texts women appear chiefly in ritual roles, and the texts’ theory of sulphur as the Goddess’s generative fluid reflects a male-authored symbolism rather than women’s own knowledge. The modern glass bangle industry of Firozabad, with its home-based women and child workers, shows that these patterns are not only historical.

    Recognising this does not diminish the achievements described in this book; it attributes them to the people who made them. A history of Indian chemistry that celebrated the Delhi pillar while ignoring the smelters who made the iron, or the kalamkari while ignoring the dyers, would repeat the very separation of knowledge from labour that Ray regretted.

    15.10 Craft Knowledge, Colonial Rule and Decline¶

    Many of the industries discussed in this volume declined in the 19th century: iron smelting, crucible steel, zinc distillation, natural indigo, hand-printed cottons. Colonial rule played a large part—through tariffs and market policies favouring British manufactures, forest laws restricting charcoal, the coercive organisation of indigo cultivation, and the general subordination of the Indian economy. Other causes were global: the industrial production of iron, steel and zinc, the synthesis of dyes, and changes in warfare and fashion affected traditional producers everywhere (Riello 2013; Balfour-Paul 1998; Srinivasan and Ranganathan 2004).

    Colonial officials and scientists also produced much of our documentation of these industries: Buchanan, Voysey and Heath on steel; the gazetteers and economic surveys; the first chemical analyses. Earlier still, the Mughal administration had recorded the work of its mints and workshops in the Āʾīn-i Akbarī (Chapter 8), a reminder that the documentation of Indian crafts did not begin with colonial rule. Their records are indispensable, even when written from the perspective of a ruling power interested in Indian resources.

    15.11 Traditional Knowledge and Intellectual Property¶

    Since the 1990s, Indian institutions have sought to protect traditional knowledge from inappropriate patenting. Two cases became famous: a United States patent on the use of turmeric for wound healing, revoked in 1997 after the Council of Scientific and Industrial Research (CSIR) showed that the use was documented in old Indian texts; and a European patent on a neem-based fungicide, revoked after opposition proceedings. In response the Government of India created the Traditional Knowledge Digital Library (TKDL), which records formulations from classical Āyurvedic, Unani, Siddha and Yoga texts in a form that patent examiners can consult as prior art. Documenting a formulation as traditional knowledge, it should be noted, does not imply that it is safe or effective.

    15.12 Teaching the History of Indian Chemistry¶

    The National Education Policy of 2020 encourages the inclusion of Indian knowledge traditions in education. The history of chemistry and metallurgy is a natural subject for this: it connects the laboratory with the museum and the archaeological site, and it shows students how scientific methods can be used to investigate the past. Good teaching in this field would:

    • use case studies with strong evidence—the Delhi pillar, Zawar, crucible steel, Indo-Pacific beads, indigo and kalamkari;

    • distinguish clearly between what texts claim and what evidence shows;

    • place Indian developments in a comparative frame, alongside Chinese, Islamic and European ones;

    • include the craftspeople, and the social hierarchies that shaped who was allowed to know and to write;

    • treat the medical uses of metals with the safety warnings they require.

    15.13 Where to See the Evidence¶

    Much of the material discussed in this volume can be seen in museums and at sites in India and abroad.

    Sites
    The iron pillar stands in the Qutb complex at Mehrauli, Delhi. The Dhar pillar fragments are at Dhar in Madhya Pradesh. Ancient mine workings and retort heaps survive at Zawar near Udaipur. Harappan sites with museums include Lothal and Dholavira in Gujarat; Kodumanal and Keezhadi in Tamil Nadu have been the subject of recent excavations by the Tamil Nadu State Department of Archaeology, whose reports have dated the urban levels of Keezhadi to the 6th century BCE and later (Sivanantham and Seran 2019).
    Indian museums
    The National Museum, New Delhi, holds Harappan material, including the bronze “dancing girl” from Mohenjo-daro, and a major collection of bronzes and arms. The Government Museum, Chennai, has one of the finest collections of South Indian bronzes. The Chhatrapati Shivaji Maharaj Vastu Sangrahalaya in Mumbai, the Indian Museum in Kolkata and the Salar Jung Museum in Hyderabad hold metalwork, arms, Bidri, jade and textiles. The Calico Museum of Textiles in Ahmedabad has outstanding painted and printed cottons.
    Museums abroad
    The British Museum, the Victoria and Albert Museum (which holds the jade wine cup of Shāh Jahān and large textile and metalwork collections) and Birmingham Museum and Art Gallery (the Sultanganj Buddha) in Britain, and many museums in Europe, North America and Southeast Asia, hold Indian metalwork, arms and textiles, many of them acquired in the colonial period.

    The presence of so many objects abroad raises questions of history and ownership that are now widely discussed. For the historian of technology, wherever they are, the objects themselves—examined with the methods described in this volume—remain the primary evidence.

    15.14 Open Questions¶

    1. The beginnings of iron. How early, and where, did regular iron smelting begin in South Asia? The claims of very early dates from Tamil Nadu (Chapter 5) need full publication and independent testing.

    2. Crucible steel. When did crucible steel first appear in South Asia, and how were the South Asian and Central Asian traditions related?

    3. The rasa texts. Few rasa texts have critical editions or reliable translations, and their chronology remains uncertain. Philological work is needed before their history can be written with confidence.

    4. Transmission. Were the Indian, Chinese and Islamic alchemical traditions historically connected, and if so how? Did Indian zinc technology influence China or Europe?

    5. Indo-Persian sources. The Persian literature on alchemy, pharmacy and materials written or copied in India is largely unstudied. Its relationship to the rasa texts, and the history of Unani kushtas, could change our picture of how the two traditions met (Chapter 14).

    6. Salts and acids. When and where were mineral acids first prepared in India, and what were the drāvakas of the late texts? The 19th-century descriptions of saltpetre, alum, sal ammoniac and borax production deserve to be compared with surviving practice and with archaeological evidence (Chapter 13).

    7. Craft documentation. Many traditional metallurgical, ceramic and dyeing practices survive only among elderly practitioners and should be recorded before they disappear.

    8. Safety. The heavy-metal content of traditional medicines and cosmetics, and its health effects, need continued monitoring; any therapeutic claims require properly designed trials.

    15.15 Concluding Reflections¶

    The history surveyed in this volume is one of skill, ingenuity and persistence: of smelters who forged a six-tonne pillar that has stood for sixteen centuries, of zinc distillers at Zawar who produced the metal on an industrial scale some centuries before the earliest dated Chinese production and long before European, of dyers whose cottons clothed much of the world, and of alchemists who handled mercury and sulphur with remarkable practical command. It is also a history of hierarchy, in which those who did the work were seldom those who wrote the books, and of danger, in which toxic metals were taken as medicines and continue to be taken today.

    To study this history well is to hold both sides in view. The achievements need no exaggeration, and the dangers need no concealment. The materials themselves—the slag heaps of Zawar, the crucibles of the Deccan, the iron of the Delhi pillar, the faded kalamkari in museum stores—remain the most reliable witnesses, and modern science, used carefully, allows them to tell their story.

    What Is Secure and What Is Debated

    Secure: the practical achievements listed at the start of this chapter, each attested by archaeology and analysis; P. C. Ray’s foundation of the subject; and the specific, mostly indirect routes by which Indian materials entered the history of modern chemistry. Debated: the causes of the 19th-century decline of the crafts, and the weight of colonial policy among them; the safety of metal medicines; and how the knowledge of craftspeople—often of low caste status, and often women—can be recovered from a record written by others.
