    Chapter 1: Introduction: Chemistry and Metallurgy in Indian History¶

    raso vai saḥ. rasaṃ hy evāyaṃ labdhvānandī bhavati.
    “That, truly, is essence (rasa); having obtained the essence one becomes blissful.”

    — Taittirīya Upaniṣad 2.7

    1.1 Words and Scope¶

    The Sanskrit word rasa has a wide range of meanings: sap or juice, the liquid essence of a thing, taste, the first bodily tissue produced from digested food, aesthetic emotion in poetics, and—in the alchemical literature that is one of the subjects of this book—mercury. Rasāyana is usually analysed as rasa + ayana, “the path (or abode) of rasa”. In classical Āyurveda it is the name of one of the eight branches of medicine (aṣṭāṅga āyurveda): rejuvenation therapy, intended to restore vigour and prolong life, and in the early compendia it is overwhelmingly a matter of herbal and dietary regimens (Wujastyk 2003; Meulenbeld 1999–2002).

    From about the end of the first millennium CE a new body of Sanskrit literature appears whose central substance is mercury and whose subject is the transformation of metals and of the human body by means of processed mercury, sulphur, metals and minerals. Its authors called it rasaśāstra, rasavidyā or rasatantra, “the science (or teaching) of mercury”; historians call it Indian alchemy and, in its medical aspect, iatrochemistry (Ray 1956; White 1996). In modern Indian languages rasāyana (Hindi rasāyan) has come to mean simply “chemistry”.

    This volume uses the word in its broadest, modern sense. It is a history of chemical and metallurgical knowledge in the Indian subcontinent, from the copper and faience of the Harappan cities to the crucible steel and distilled zinc of the medieval period, and from the alchemical laboratories of the rasa texts to the dyer’s vat and the glass-bead workshop. Much of this knowledge was never written down: it lived in the hands of smiths, potters, dyers and miners, and is known to us through archaeology, the observations of travellers and colonial officials, and modern laboratory analysis. Part of it was written down, in Sanskrit treatises on statecraft, medicine, gems and alchemy. The two kinds of evidence do not always meet, and one aim of this book is to keep them distinct.

    Three Kinds of “Rasāyana”

    • Rasāyana in Āyurveda: rejuvenation therapy, one of the eight branches of classical medicine, mainly herbal.

    • Rasaśāstra: the medieval literature on mercury, metals and minerals, with two declared aims—the transmutation of metals (dhātuvāda or lohavāda) and the perfection or immortality of the body (dehavāda)—and a growing third, medicinal one.

    • Chemistry and metallurgy: the practical knowledge of ores, metals, alloys, glasses, pigments and dyes, most of it held by craft communities rather than by Sanskrit authors.

    Health and Safety Note

    Several chapters of this volume describe what the rasa texts say about processing mercury, lead, arsenic, copper and other toxic metals and minerals. They are described as part of the history of chemistry. The book deliberately gives no doses, proportions, durations or working procedures, and nothing in it is medical advice. Independent laboratory studies have repeatedly found lead, mercury and arsenic in Ayurvedic products sold in India and abroad: about one in five of the products tested by Saper and colleagues contained detectable levels, and products of the rasaśāstra type were the most likely to contain them (Saper et al. 2004, 2008). Serious poisoning from such products is documented (Centers for Disease Control and Prevention 2004; Kales and Saper 2009). The texts hold that “purification” (śodhana) and calcination (māraṇa) make these substances safe. Toxicology has not confirmed that view; the studies on both sides are reviewed in Chapter 9. This note applies to every chapter of the book, and the shorter notes in later chapters refer back to it.

    1.2 An Outline Chronology¶

    The chronology of Indian chemical and metallurgical history rests on two very different kinds of dating. Archaeological sites are dated, with varying precision, by radiocarbon and other scientific methods; Sanskrit technical texts are dated mainly by cross-references and quotations, and many dates are uncertain by a century or more. Table 1.1 gives an outline; a fuller chronology appears at the end of the volume.

    Table 1.1. Outline Chronology of Chemistry and Metallurgy in South Asia
    Period Developments
    c. 2600–1900 BCE Mature Harappan civilisation: copper and tin bronze, lost-wax casting, gold and silver work, faience, stoneware bangles, etched carnelian beads
    c. 1500–500 BCE Vedic texts name gold, ayas (metal, later iron), lead and tin; iron in the Ganga plain and the Deccan from the late 2nd millennium BCE (earlier dates claimed and debated)
    c. 600 BCE–300 CE Punch-marked silver coinage; Northern Black Polished Ware; crucible steel in south India; Arthaśāstra (composite, c. 50 BCE–300 CE) on mines, metals and the mint
    c. 300–700 CE Gupta gold coinage; the iron pillar now at Delhi (c. 400 CE); gem chapters of the Bṛhatsaṃhitā
    c. 900–1300 CE Zinc distillation at Zawar (earliest retorts c. 9th–10th century); early rasa texts (Rasahṛdayatantra, Rasārṇava); Chola bronzes
    c. 1300–1700 CE Rasaratnasamuccaya and later compendia; large-scale zinc production; Golconda diamonds; export of Indian cottons, dyes and steel
    c. 1700–1900 CE European study of wootz and Indian dyeing; decline of traditional iron, steel and indigo industries under colonial rule and industrial competition

    1.3 Metals in the Vedic Texts¶

    The Ṛgveda (core c. 1500–1200 BCE) knows gold (hiraṇya) and a metal called ayas. Since the Ṛgveda predates the general use of iron in north India, ayas there is usually understood as copper or bronze; the word later comes to mean iron (Chakrabarti 1992). Gold is everywhere in the hymns as ornament and as an image of brilliance; the cosmogonic hymn Ṛgveda 10.121 opens with the “golden embryo”:

    Ṛgveda 10.121.1

    hiraṇyagarbhaḥ sam avartatāgre bhūtasya jātaḥ patir eka āsīt
    In the beginning the Golden Embryo arose; once born, he was the one lord of all that exists.

    The hymn is cosmology, not metallurgy, but it shows the symbolic weight that gold already carried. In the later Vedic texts (c. 1200–500 BCE) the vocabulary of metals grows. A list of substances in the Vājasaneyi Saṃhitā (18.13) names, after stone, clay, hills, sand and plants, hiraṇya (gold), ayas, śyāma (“the dark one”, usually taken as iron), loha (“the red one”, usually taken as copper), sīsa (lead) and trapu (tin). Later Vedic texts, beginning with the Atharvaveda, distinguish a “dark” metal (śyāma ayas, later also kṛṣṇāyas, “black metal”) from a “red” one (lohita), most naturally iron and copper. These identifications are inferences from usage and from archaeology, not definitions given in the texts, and some remain debated.

    1.4 The Arthaśāstra: Mines, Metals and the Mint¶

    The Arthaśāstra, the Sanskrit treatise on statecraft traditionally attributed to Kauṭilya (Cāṇakya), minister of Candragupta Maurya (c. 300 BCE), is in its present form a composite work compiled between about 50 BCE and 300 CE (Olivelle 2013). Its second book, on the duties of superintendents, contains the fullest early account of the state’s interest in minerals and metals. A superintendent of mines (ākarādhyakṣa) must know the science of ores and the techniques of smelting, and recognises ores by their colour, weight, smell and taste (Arthaśāstra 2.12). Beside him stand a superintendent of metals (lohādhyakṣa), who runs workshops for copper, lead, tin, iron, brass, bronze and other metals, and a superintendent of the mint (lakṣaṇādhyakṣa), who issues silver and copper coins of fixed composition and is checked by an examiner of coins (rūpadarśaka). The chapters on the state goldsmith describe the examination of gold and silver by touchstone, colour and fire (2.13–14), and list at length the ways in which artisans may cheat by alloying or substitution; gems, pearls and other precious goods are to be examined before they are received into the treasury (2.11).

    The treatise also sets out punishments for debasing coin and for fraud by goldsmiths. Its interest is fiscal and administrative: mines and metal workshops are sources of revenue to be controlled, and its technical content is a series of lists rather than a description of processes. It is nonetheless good evidence that by the early centuries CE the state recognised mining, smelting, alloying, assaying and coinage as distinct specialisms (Olivelle 2013; Biswas and Biswas 1996).

    1.5 Weights, Measures and Assay¶

    Quantitative work with materials depends on reliable weights. The Harappans used a remarkably uniform system of cubical weights, usually of chert, found across the whole area of the civilisation. The smaller weights double in a binary series (1, 2, 4, 8, 16, 32, 64 units), and the larger ones follow a decimal pattern; the most commonly found weight is about 13.7 g (Kenoyer 1998). Such weights would have served for trade in precious materials such as beads, metals and spices.

    The Arthaśāstra has a superintendent of standards of weights and measures, who is to have balances and weights made of iron or of stone from particular regions and to inspect those used in the market; it gives tables of units, from the māṣa and suvarṇa upwards, used for gold, silver and other goods (Olivelle 2013). The goldsmith’s touchstone and the assayer’s balance, together with fixed weight standards, allowed the state and merchants to check the fineness and weight of coin and bullion. Later Indian systems of weights, with units such as the ratti (the seed of Abrus precatorius), māśā and tolā, continued in use in jewellery and medicine into modern times.

    1.6 Chemical Knowledge in the Medical Compendia¶

    Long before the rasa literature, the classical medical compendia of Caraka and Suśruta (Caraka’s redaction c. 100 BCE–200 CE; the Suśruta-saṃhitā in its present form by c. 500 CE) and the later synthesis of Vāgbhaṭa (c. 600 CE) recorded a considerable amount of practical chemistry, embedded in pharmacy and surgery (Wujastyk 2003; Meulenbeld 1999–2002). Both compendia are available in standard Sanskrit editions and in English translation (Trikamji Acharya 1941; Sharma 1981–1994; Trikamji Acharya and Acharya 1938; Bhishagratna 1907–1916).

    1.6.1 Alkalis¶

    The Suśrutasaṃhitā devotes a chapter of its first book (Sūtrasthāna 11) to caustic alkali (kṣāra), which it ranks above surgical instruments for some purposes because it can cut, cauterise and scrape. The alkali is made from the ashes of particular plants, leached with water and concentrated by boiling, and a milder form for internal use is distinguished from a stronger, caustic form for external application (Trikamji Acharya and Acharya 1938; Bhishagratna 1907–1916). It is one of the clearest descriptions of a deliberate chemical preparation in ancient Indian literature; its chemistry is discussed in Section 13.2.

    1.6.2 Salts¶

    The compendia classify salts (lavaṇa) into several kinds, commonly five: rock salt (saindhava, “from Sindh”), sauvarcala, viḍa, sea salt (sāmudra) and salt obtained from saline earth (audbhida). Some of these were prepared by heating common salt with other materials, and modern authors have tried to identify them with particular mixtures of sodium chloride with sulphides, carbonates or other salts; the identifications are uncertain. Other mineral substances used in the compendia include alum, red ochre, collyrium (añjana), orpiment and realgar (for external use), and iron and other metals.

    1.6.3 Fermentation¶

    The compendia describe many fermented medicinal liquors (āsava and ariṣṭa), made by allowing decoctions or juices with sugar or honey to ferment, often with the flowers of dhātakī (Woodfordia fruticosa), which are generally thought to have supplied the yeasts that start the fermentation. The Arthaśāstra similarly lists the kinds of liquor (medaka, prasannā, āsava, ariṣṭa, maireya and others) whose production was supervised by the state (Olivelle 2013). Fermentation, the oldest of the chemical arts, was thus thoroughly familiar to Indian physicians and administrators.

    1.6.4 Metals in Early Medicine¶

    Caraka and Suśruta prescribe iron, and to a lesser extent gold, silver, copper, tin and lead, usually in the form of thin leaves heated and quenched in plant decoctions, ground to powder, or used to prepare medicated waters, and they use some minerals externally. Mercury plays almost no part. The great expansion of metallic and mineral medicine, and the internal use of mercury, belong to the rasa literature of the following centuries (Chapter 2).

    1.7 Philosophical Frameworks¶

    Indian thinkers did not develop a theory of chemical composition in the modern sense, but several philosophical schools offered frameworks within which material change could be discussed.

    1.7.1 The Five Elements¶

    Most Indian schools accepted that the material world is made of five “great elements” (pañcamahābhūta): earth, water, fire, air and ether (ākāśa). Āyurveda used this scheme to explain the qualities of foods and drugs, and the rasa texts often describe metals and minerals in its terms. The scheme is classificatory; it does not predict what will happen when two substances are heated together.

    1.7.2 Vaiśeṣika Atomism¶

    The Vaiśeṣika school, whose foundational Vaiśeṣikasūtra (attributed to Kaṇāda) is usually dated between the last centuries BCE and the early centuries CE, held that earth, water, fire and air are composed of eternal, partless atoms (paramāṇu); ether is not atomic. Two atoms form a dyad (dvyaṇuka), which is still imperceptible; three dyads form a triad (tryaṇuka), the smallest perceptible unit. The qualities of compound substances derive from those of their atoms.

    Pīlupāka and Piṭharapāka

    A famous debate concerned what happens when a clay pot is baked and turns red. The Vaiśeṣikas (from Praśastapāda, c. 6th century CE, onwards) held the pīlupāka (“atom-baking”) view: heat breaks the pot down to its atoms, changes their colour, and the pot is re-formed, all in an imperceptibly short time. The Naiyāyikas held the piṭharapāka (“pot-baking”) view: heat penetrates the whole pot, which changes colour without being destroyed. The debate is a genuine attempt to explain a physical change in atomic terms; it is not, however, a theory of chemical reaction, and neither side proposed experiments to settle it.

    1.7.3 Alchemical Correspondences¶

    The rasa texts have their own framework, drawn from Tantric Śaivism. Mercury is the seed (semen) of Śiva and sulphur the uterine blood of the Goddess; their union in the laboratory reproduces divine union. The texts assume that a mercury capable of transmuting base metal into gold will, taken into the body, make the body imperishable (White 1996). The Rasaratnasamuccaya builds the assumption into one of the names of mercury: it is called sūta because “it brings forth (sūte) perfection in body and in metal” (dehalohamayīṃ siddhiṃ sūte, 1.78; Chapter 3) (GRETIL 2020). This analogical reasoning, not atomism, is what governed alchemical practice.

    1.8 Who Knew What: Craft Knowledge and Textual Knowledge¶

    Most of the achievements discussed in this volume—the Delhi pillar, wootz steel, Zawar zinc, Chola bronzes, Indian dyeing—were the work of hereditary craft communities: smiths (lohār, karmakāra), the five-craft Viśvakarma communities of the south (Tamil kammāḷar, Kannada pāñcāḷa), bronze-casters (sthapati), dyers and printers (rangrez, chhīpā), potters, bead-makers, miners, and forest communities such as the Agaria who smelted iron (Mukherjee 1978; Elwin 1942). Their knowledge was passed on orally and by apprenticeship within families and caste groups.

    In the hierarchy of the Brahmanical law books, manual crafts ranked low, and some of the groups who smelted iron or tanned leather were treated as untouchable. Sanskrit authors wrote about metals mainly as administrators (Arthaśāstra), physicians (Āyurveda), connoisseurs (the gem treatises) and alchemists (rasaśāstra). Their texts preserve names, classifications and some processes, but rarely the practical details of a working furnace. The historian therefore has to read texts, archaeology and ethnography together, while keeping in mind that the people who made the steel and the people who wrote the books were seldom the same.

    1.9 Classifying Substances in the Rasa Texts¶

    The rasa texts developed elaborate classifications of metals and minerals. They differ from text to text; the most influential scheme is that of the Rasaratnasamuccaya (late 13th or 14th century), which places mercury (rasa, pārada) in a class of its own and groups other substances as in Table 1.2: eight mahārasas, eight uparasas, eight sādhāraṇa rasas, the gems and the metals.

    Table 1.2. The Mineral Classes of the Rasaratnasamuccaya (with Usual Modern Identifications)
    Class Members (usual identification; “?” = uncertain)
    Mahārasa abhraka (mica), vaikrānta (?), mākṣika (copper/iron pyrites), vimala (pyrite variety?), śilājatu (shilajit), sasyaka (blue vitriol), capala (?), rasaka (calamine, a zinc ore)
    Uparasa gandhaka (sulphur), gairika (red ochre), kāsīsa (green vitriol), kāṅkṣī (alum), haritāla (orpiment), manaḥśilā (realgar), añjana (stibnite or galena), kaṅkuṣṭha (?)
    Sādhāraṇa rasa kampillaka (powder of Mallotus fruit), gaurīpāṣāṇa (white arsenic?), navasādara (sal ammoniac), kapardikā (cowrie shell), agnijāra (?), girisindūra (?), hiṅgula (cinnabar), mṛddāraśṛṅga (litharge)

    Several of these identifications are uncertain, and the same Sanskrit name may denote different substances in different texts and regions. Many members of the lists—orpiment, realgar, white arsenic, cinnabar, litharge, lead and the copper salts—are highly toxic. The texts also describe in detail the laboratory (rasaśālā) in which such substances were to be processed, with its shrine, furnaces, heating pits and apparatus; these are discussed in Chapter 2.

    1.10 Nāgārjuna and the Attribution of Alchemical Works¶

    No name is more often attached to Indian alchemy than Nāgārjuna’s, and none is more confused. At least three figures must be distinguished (White 1996; Wujastyk 1984; Meulenbeld 1999–2002).

    1. Nāgārjuna the Buddhist philosopher (c. 2nd–3rd century CE), founder of the Madhyamaka school and author of the Mūlamadhyamakakārikā. Nothing in his securely attributed philosophical works concerns alchemy (on the philosopher, see Volume 4, Buddha–Jina Prabhā).

    2. Nāgārjuna of legend. By the 7th century the philosopher had attracted alchemical legends: Xuanzang, on his journey of c. 629–645, records a story that Nāgārjuna prepared a drug of long life for a Sātavāhana king and could turn stone into gold (Beal 1884). Tibetan tradition later identified the philosopher with the alchemist and the tantric adept.

    3. Nāgārjuna the alchemist or alchemists. Al-Bīrūnī, writing c. 1030, mentions a Nāgārjuna from a fort near Somnāth who excelled in alchemy, wrote a book on it and lived about a century before al-Bīrūnī’s time (Sachau 1888). A Sanskrit alchemical text, the Rasendramaṅgala, is attributed to a Nāgārjuna; its date is uncertain, and estimates range from the 8th to the 12th century. A siddha named Nāgārjuna also appears in the lists of the Nāth and Siddha traditions, and the 12th-century commentator Ḍalhaṇa names a Nāgārjuna as the redactor of the Suśrutasaṃhitā.

    Modern scholarship treats these as different people, or as a legendary name attached to later works, and does not identify the Madhyamaka philosopher with any alchemical author. The often-cited “Rasaratnākara of Nāgārjuna”, described by P. C. Ray as one of the oldest rasa texts, is an instance of how such attributions arise: Wujastyk showed that it is a “ghost”, and that the work cited under that name is in fact the Rasendramaṅgala (Wujastyk 1984). The well-known Rasaratnākara is a later work by Nityanātha (13th–14th century). Statements that Nāgārjuna “systematised” Indian alchemy, or that the eighteen saṃskāras go back to him, therefore have no secure basis.

    1.11 The Harappan Legacy¶

    The Indus or Harappan civilisation (mature phase c. 2600–1900 BCE) provides the earliest large body of evidence for chemical crafts in South Asia (Kenoyer 1998; Possehl 2002).

    1.11.1 Metals¶

    Harappan smiths worked copper, tin bronze, arsenical copper, lead, silver, gold and electrum. Most copper objects are of nearly pure or arsenical copper; tin bronze is less common, and tin must have been imported. Objects were cast in open and closed moulds and by the lost-wax method—the famous “dancing girl” from Mohenjo-daro is a lost-wax casting in copper alloy—and sheet metal was raised, riveted and soldered (Kenoyer and Miller 1999; Agrawal 2000). Copper ores were probably obtained from the Aravalli hills (the Ganeshwar–Jodhpura region) and from Baluchistan and Oman.

    1.11.2 Vitreous Materials and Beads¶

    The Harappans also commanded a high-temperature chemistry of non-metallic materials: faience, fired and glazed steatite, partly vitrified “stoneware” bangles fired in sealed containers, and stone beads whose colour and surface were altered by heat and by alkaline pastes (Kenoyer 1998). These crafts are described in Section 12.1, and the carnelian bead industry in Section 10.7.

    1.12 The Textual Corpus¶

    The oldest of the written sources used in this volume is the Arthaśāstra (composite, c. 50 BCE–300 CE), on mines, metals, the mint and gems (Olivelle 2013). The classical Āyurvedic compendia of Caraka, Suśruta and Vāgbhaṭa (c. 100 BCE to c. 600 CE) use some metals and minerals, mainly externally or in small quantities, and describe rejuvenation (rasāyana) therapy (Wujastyk 2003). A separate literature on gems runs from the gem chapters of Varāhamihira’s Bṛhatsaṃhitā (6th century) to Buddhabhaṭṭa’s Ratnaparīkṣā and the Agastimata (Bhat 1981–1982; Finot 1896).

    The rasa literature proper (Chapter 2) begins with the Rasahṛdayatantra of Govinda (c. 10th–11th century) and the Rasārṇava (c. 11th–12th century), continues with the Rasendracūḍāmaṇi of Somadeva (12th–13th century), the Ānandakanda (13th century) and the Rasaprakāśasudhākara of Yaśodhara (13th century), and reaches its most influential form in the Rasaratnasamuccaya attributed to Vāgbhaṭa (late 13th or 14th century) and Nityanātha’s Rasaratnākara (13th–14th century); all these dates are approximate (White 1996; Meulenbeld 1999–2002). Later compendia such as the Āyurvedaprakāśa of Mādhava (17th century) and the Rasataraṅgiṇī of Sadānanda Śarmā (20th century) remain the basis of modern rasaśāstra teaching. Travellers and observers, from Xuanzang to al-Bīrūnī, Tavernier and Francis Buchanan, supply what the texts leave out.

    1.12.1 Indo-Islamic and Early Modern Sources¶

    The Sanskrit texts are only part of the written record. From the 11th century, Arabic and Persian authors wrote about Indian materials and techniques, and from the 13th century the courts of the Delhi Sultanate and its successors produced their own administrative and technical literature.

    Al-Bīrūnī (973–after 1050), who spent years in north-west India under the Ghaznavids, described Indian rasāyana in his India (c. 1030) and wrote on gems and on drugs (Chapter 14) (Sachau 1888). Ṭhakkura Pherū, a Jain official of the Khaljī treasury and mint, wrote in the early 14th century short treatises in Apabhraṃśa (a late literary Prakrit) on gems, coins and metals (Section 8.12). The Āʾīn-i Akbarī of Abuʾl Faẓl (completed c. 1598), the great gazetteer of Akbar’s empire, describes the imperial mint, the manufacture of arms, the cooling of water with saltpetre, the distillation of spirits and the perfumery of the court (Abuʾl-Faẓl ʿAllāmī 1873); its account of the mint is discussed in Section 8.13. European travellers and officials—Tavernier on the diamond mines, Buchanan and Voysey on steel, the authors of district gazetteers and economic surveys—recorded processes in the 17th–19th centuries, often because they wished to profit from or imitate them.

    These sources are a valuable corrective to a narrowly Sanskritic view. Much of the metallurgy, minting, perfumery, dyeing and glass-making of medieval and early modern India took place in a Persianate court and urban culture, and was practised by Muslim as well as Hindu and Jain artisans. The Islamic alchemical tradition in India and its contacts with rasaśāstra are discussed in Chapter 14.

    1.13 India in the Wider History of Technology¶

    Several Indian products and processes had a significance far beyond the subcontinent. They are treated in detail later; here it is enough to state them with the qualifications that current scholarship requires.

    Crucible steel
    High-carbon steel made by melting iron with carbonaceous material in sealed crucibles was produced in south India from at least the last centuries BCE, and in Sri Lanka in the first millennium CE. It was exported as ingots to Iran and the Arab world, where some was forged into the patterned “Damascus” blades. Crucible steel was also made in Central Asia and Iran, so South Asia was one of several centres of the technology, not the only one (Srinivasan and Ranganathan 2004; Rehren and Papachristou 2003).
    Zinc distillation
    Zawar in Rajasthan has the earliest securely dated evidence for the production of metallic zinc by distillation on an industrial scale, from about the 9th–10th century CE and on a very large scale from about the 14th century (Craddock et al. 1983; Craddock 2017).
    The Delhi iron pillar
    A forge-welded wrought-iron pillar of c. 400 CE, more than six tonnes in weight, whose slow corrosion has been explained by Balasubramaniam’s work on its phosphorus-rich protective film (Balasubramaniam 2000).
    Indigo and mordant dyeing
    India was for many centuries a leading producer and exporter of indigo, and Indian mordant-dyed cottons were prized across the Indian Ocean and later in Europe. The English word “indigo” comes, through Spanish or Portuguese and Latin indicum, from Greek indikon, “Indian (substance)” (Balfour-Paul 1998; Riello 2013).
    Diamonds
    Until the discovery of Brazilian deposits in the 1720s, India was the world’s main source of diamonds (Tavernier 1889).

    Older books often go further and call India “the first” to do many things. Such claims are usually unnecessary and often wrong: technologies were developed in several places, evidence is incomplete, and new discoveries regularly move the dates. This volume describes what the evidence shows and leaves aside contests of priority.

    1.14 How the History Has Been Written¶

    The history of Indian chemistry and metallurgy has been written from several standpoints, each of which has left its mark on the literature.

    Colonial
    Many 19th-century European writers assumed that India had produced philosophy and religion but not science, and that its crafts, however skilful, were matters of unreflective custom. At the same time colonial officials collected much valuable information about Indian crafts, often with an eye to their commercial exploitation or replacement.
    Nationalist
    In response, Indian scholars of the late 19th and early 20th centuries set out to show that ancient India had possessed genuine sciences. P. C. Ray’s History of Hindu Chemistry (Ray 1902–1909) and Brajendranath Seal’s The Positive Sciences of the Ancient Hindus (1915) (Seal 1915) are the classic works. They were careful and learned, but they sometimes dated texts too early and read modern ideas into ancient ones—tendencies that popular writing has since greatly exaggerated.
    Marxist and social
    Later historians, such as Debiprasad Chattopadhyaya, asked why empirical science had flourished in some periods and declined in others, and related the fate of science to social structure, especially caste and the devaluation of manual labour (Chattopadhyaya 1986).
    Institutional surveys
    After independence the Indian National Science Academy sponsored A Concise History of Science in India (1971), and the multi-volume History of Science, Philosophy and Culture in Indian Civilization devoted a volume to chemistry and chemical techniques, edited by B. V. Subbarayappa (1999); both gather textual and technical material and remain useful guides (Bose et al. 1971; Subbarayappa 1999).
    Archaeometric
    Since the 1970s, the most important new evidence has come from archaeology and laboratory analysis—the work of Agrawal, Balasubramaniam, Srinivasan, Craddock, Juleff, Kenoyer and many others—which has made it possible to test claims against material evidence (Agrawal 2000; Craddock 1995).
    Religious-historical
    Scholars of religion, especially David Gordon White, have placed rasaśāstra in its Tantric context, showing that it cannot be understood simply as proto-chemistry (White 1996).

    This volume draws on all of these, but especially on the last two, and tries to avoid both the colonial dismissal and the nationalist exaggeration.

    1.15 A Comparative Frame¶

    Indian chemical history is best understood alongside the histories of other regions, with which it was often in contact.

    China
    Chinese metallurgy developed cast iron early (by the mid-first millennium BCE), using furnaces hot enough to melt iron, whereas South Asia relied on bloomery iron and crucible steel. Chinese alchemy, with its elixirs of cinnabar and mercury, flourished from the Han dynasty, and gunpowder was discovered in China, probably in the 9th century, in the course of alchemical experimentation. Porcelain, a high-fired white ceramic, was a Chinese achievement without an Indian counterpart.
    The Islamic world
    From the 8th century, Arabic-writing chemists inherited Greek, Syriac, Persian and possibly Indian knowledge. The writings attributed to Jābir ibn Ḥayyān and those of the physician al-Rāzī (Rhazes, d. c. 925) classified substances into minerals, plants and animal products, described laboratory apparatus such as the alembic and the aludel, and developed the theory that metals are formed from sulphur and mercury. Al-Bīrūnī’s work shows that learned Muslims were interested in Indian sciences, including rasāyana (Sachau 1888; Principe 2013).
    Europe
    Latin Europe received alchemy from Arabic in the 12th century. In the 16th century Paracelsus promoted chemical medicines, including preparations of mercury and antimony—a development parallel in some ways to the medical turn of Indian rasaśāstra. Modern chemistry emerged in Europe from the 17th century, through the work of Boyle, Lavoisier, Dalton and others, out of this alchemical and pharmaceutical background (Principe 2013).

    Seen in this frame, India’s distinctive contributions lie in particular technologies—crucible steel, zinc distillation, high-tin bronze, mineral-soda-alumina glass, mordant dyeing of cotton, crystalline sugar—and in a rich alchemical literature, rather than in a single path towards modern chemistry.

    1.16 Methodological Considerations¶

    1.16.1 Dating the Rasa Texts¶

    Many rasa texts are attributed to legendary or semi-legendary authors, were expanded by later hands, and survive in manuscripts much younger than the works themselves. Relative dates can be established from quotations (a text that quotes another is later), and absolute dates from a few datable authors and manuscripts. The result is a framework accurate to perhaps a century; P. C. Ray’s early dates, still repeated in popular books, have generally been revised downwards (Meulenbeld 1999–2002; White 1996).

    1.16.2 Reading Processes in Texts¶

    Technical texts in Sanskrit verse are compressed and often deliberately obscure. Their names for substances may not correspond to modern mineral species, and the same procedure may be described differently in different works. Modern rasaśāstra textbooks often present a single standardised reading; the historian has to remember that this is a modern interpretation.

    1.16.3 Laboratory Analysis¶

    Modern analytical methods—X-ray diffraction, electron microscopy, X-ray fluorescence, mass spectrometry, radiocarbon and other dating techniques—have transformed the study of ancient materials. They have shown, for example, how the Delhi pillar resists corrosion and when Zawar’s zinc retorts were used. When the same methods are applied to modern “traditional” medicines, they show what those products contain—including, in many cases, toxic levels of heavy metals—but they do not by themselves show that a product is safe or effective. Those are questions for toxicology and for properly conducted clinical trials. Table 1.3 lists the main techniques; what archaeometallurgists use them to find out is explained in Section 4.5, at the start of the chapters on metals, and Figure 4.1 shows where the principal sites lie.

    1.17 Scope and Structure of this Volume¶

    The volume is organised in fifteen chapters. After this introduction, Chapter 2 surveys the rasa literature, its texts, apparatus, classifications and social world, quoting a few of its programmatic verses, and Chapter 3 describes what the texts say about mercury and its eighteen saṃskāras and what modern toxicology says about mercury. Chapter 4 turns to the mines from which the metals came and explains the methods of archaeometallurgy on which the following chapters rely. Chapters 5–8 treat the metals: iron and the Delhi pillar; crucible steel; zinc and Zawar; and gold, silver, copper, lead, tin and their alloys, with the Delhi and Mughal mints. Chapter 9 discusses calcined metal preparations (bhasma) as a historical tradition and as a contemporary safety problem.

    The non-metallic crafts follow: gems and minerals (Chapter 10); dyes, pigments and inks (Chapter 11); glass, ceramics and building materials (Chapter 12); and the salts, alkalis and acids—kṣāra, common salt, saltpetre, alum, the vitriols, sal ammoniac and borax—that were the everyday reagents of pre-modern chemistry, together with sugar (Chapter 13). Chapter 14 turns to Islamic alchemy (kīmiyā) in India, its meeting with rasaśāstra, and the history and chemistry of distillation, from spirits to attar. Chapter 15 considers the legacy of this tradition, its place in the history of world chemistry, and the questions that remain open.

    What Is Secure and What Is Debated

    Secure: the three senses of rasāyana; the state’s interest in mines, metals and the mint in the Arthaśāstra; the alkalis, salts and fermented liquors of the medical compendia; and the distinction between the Madhyamaka philosopher Nāgārjuna and the alchemists who bore his name. Debated: the dates of almost every rasa text, which are relative and uncertain by a century or more; the identity of many minerals named in the texts; and how far the craft knowledge that made Indian steel, zinc and dyes ever reached the authors of Sanskrit treatises.
