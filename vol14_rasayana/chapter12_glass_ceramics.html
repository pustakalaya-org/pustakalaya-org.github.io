    Chapter 12: Kāca and Mṛttikā: Glass, Ceramics and Building Materials¶

    12.1 Vitreous Materials before Glass¶

    Glass (Sanskrit kāca) was preceded in South Asia, as in Egypt and Mesopotamia, by other vitreous materials. The Harappans made faience on a large scale: a body of crushed quartz, bound with a little alkali and colourant, shaped into beads, bangles, small vessels and figurines and fired so that a glassy surface formed, usually blue or green from copper. They also made high-fired “stoneware” bangles, fired in sealed containers until partly vitrified, and they glazed steatite beads (Kenoyer 1998). These crafts, which have been studied through workshop debris, experimental replication and laboratory analysis, are the earliest body of chemical technology in South Asia, and they deserve a closer look (Kenoyer 1998; Vidale 2000).

    12.1.1 Faience¶

    Faience is not a glass and not a pottery, but a material between the two. Its body is made of finely ground quartz, mixed with a small proportion of alkali—from plant ash or natural soda—and usually some lime, moistened, shaped by hand or in moulds, and fired. During firing the alkali reacts with the surfaces of the quartz grains to form a little glass, which binds the grains together, and a glassy layer forms on the surface. Copper compounds added to the body or to the glazing mixture give the characteristic turquoise and blue-green colours. Studies of Egyptian and Near Eastern faience have identified three ways of producing the surface glaze: applying a glazing slurry; “efflorescence”, in which soluble alkali salts in the moist body migrate to the surface as it dries and form the glaze on firing; and “cementation”, in which the object is fired buried in a glazing powder. Which of these the Harappans used, and in what combination, is still being investigated. Much Harappan faience is dense and contains a high proportion of glassy phase through its body, more than is usual in Egyptian faience, and some archaeologists have preferred to call it a vitreous paste. Harappan craftsmen made from it beads, bangles, small vessels, inlays, figurines of animals and moulded tablets bearing short inscriptions in the Indus script (Kenoyer 1998).

    12.1.2 Fired and Glazed Steatite¶

    Steatite (soapstone), a rock composed largely of talc, is so soft that it can be carved with copper tools. When it is heated to about 900–1000 °C it loses its water and recrystallises into a white, much harder material composed largely of a magnesium silicate (enstatite) and silica. Harappan seal-cutters exploited this transformation: they carved the square stamp seals in the soft stone and then fired them, and many seals and beads were coated with an alkaline glaze before firing, which gave them a white or bluish surface. The crisp edges of many Indus seals have survived for four thousand years largely because of this treatment. Steatite was also made into enormous numbers of tiny disc beads, some only about a millimetre across; whether some of these “microbeads” were made from a paste of powdered steatite rather than cut from the stone has been debated (Kenoyer 1998; Vidale 2000).

    12.1.3 Stoneware Bangles¶

    The most demanding Harappan ceramic was the stoneware bangle, found mainly at Mohenjo-daro and Harappa. These thin bangles, dark grey to black and highly polished, were made of an extremely fine, carefully prepared clay and fired at temperatures well above those of ordinary Harappan pottery, so that the body partly vitrified and became dense and hard. They were fired stacked inside closed clay containers (saggars) sealed with clay, which protected them from the fuel and created the reducing atmosphere that produced their dark colour; the containers were broken open after firing. Many bangles bear tiny inscriptions in the Indus script, and the sealed containers were sometimes stamped. The difficulty of the process, the limited number of places where its debris has been found, and the inscriptions suggest that production was controlled by a small group, perhaps under some form of civic or elite supervision (Kenoyer 1998; Vidale 2000).

    12.1.4 A Shared Pyrotechnology¶

    Faience, fired steatite and stoneware, together with the heat-treatment of carnelian and the alkaline etching of carnelian beads (Section 10.7), depended on a common stock of skills: grinding and mixing minerals, the use of alkalis as fluxes and of copper as a colourant, and above all the control of temperature and atmosphere in kilns and sealed containers. Kenoyer and Vidale have emphasised that these crafts were technically interlinked and that techniques passed between them—alkaline glazes between steatite and faience, sealed firing between stoneware and other products (Kenoyer 1998; Vidale 2000). There is no convincing evidence that the Harappans made glass in the strict sense, and glass-making proper appears in South Asia only in the first millennium BCE (below); but the Harappan vitreous crafts show a command of high-temperature chemistry that is remarkable for its date.

    12.2 Glass in South Asia¶

    The earliest glass objects from South Asia are beads and small ornaments from sites of the early first millennium BCE in the north and the megalithic sites of the south. By the last centuries BCE glass was being made and worked on a large scale, and South Asia became one of the major glass-producing regions of the ancient world (Henderson 2013; Francis 2002). Table 12.1 gives an outline.

    Table 12.1. Outline of Glass in South Asia (approximate)
    Period Sites (examples) Evidence
    c. 2600–1900 BCE Harappan cities Faience; glazed steatite; stoneware bangles
    early 1st mill. BCE Painted Grey Ware and megalithic sites Early glass beads
    c. 3rd c. BCE–3rd c. CE Arikamedu, Taxila, Kodumanal, Mantai (Sri Lanka) Drawn glass beads in quantity; glass working; Roman vessel glass imported

    early modern Kapadvanj, Patna, Firozabad Glass vessels, bottles, huqqa bases, bangles

    12.2.1 Arikamedu and the Indo-Pacific Beads¶

    Arikamedu, on the coast near Puducherry, was excavated by Mortimer Wheeler in 1945 and by Vimala Begley’s team in 1989–1992. It was a port in contact with the Roman world in the early centuries CE: Wheeler found Mediterranean amphorae and Italian red-gloss pottery (Arretine ware), which allowed him to date the site, and Begley’s excavations showed that the settlement began before the Roman contact and continued after it. It was also a major centre for making small, monochrome glass beads drawn from glass tubes, and for working semi-precious stones (Begley et al. 1996). These “Indo-Pacific” beads, as Peter Francis called them, were made at several South Asian and later Southeast Asian sites and traded from East Africa to Korea; they are among the most widely distributed artefacts of the ancient world (Francis 2002). At Papanaidupet in Andhra Pradesh drawn glass beads were still made by related methods in the 20th century.

    12.2.2 Composition¶

    Laboratory analyses show that much South Asian glass belongs to a distinctive type known as mineral-soda-alumina glass: a soda glass with a relatively high alumina content, probably made from local sands rich in feldspar and from a mineral (not plant-ash) source of soda, such as the alkaline efflorescences (Hindi reh) of north Indian soils. This glass differs from the natron glass of the Roman Mediterranean and from the plant-ash glasses of western Asia, and its distinctive composition allows Indian glass to be identified at distant sites (Dussubieux et al. 2010; Henderson 2013). Potash glasses and plant-ash soda glasses also occur in South and Southeast Asia, and the picture is still being refined by new analyses.

    12.2.3 Colour¶

    South Asian glass-makers coloured glass with metal oxides: copper for turquoise and green (and, under reducing conditions, for opaque red), cobalt for deep blue, iron for greens and browns, manganese for purple and black, and lead–tin compounds for opaque yellow. The orange-red opaque glass beads of the Indo-Pacific type, coloured with cuprite, required careful control of the furnace atmosphere (Francis 2002; Henderson 2013).

    12.2.4 Bead-Making Techniques¶

    1. Drawing: a hollow tube of glass is drawn from a gather on a pointed tool; the tube is cut into short segments and reheated to round the edges. This allowed mass production of small beads.

    2. Winding: glass is wound round a wire or rod to form individual beads.

    3. Folding, moulding and drilling: used for larger or special beads.

    12.3 Glass at the Mughal and Rajput Courts¶

    Glass vessels, though less common in India than in the Islamic Middle East or Europe, were made and used at the courts and in the cities of medieval and early modern India. Mughal and Deccani glass includes huqqa bases, bottles, flasks and cups, sometimes gilded or enamelled; some were imported from Iran, Venice or later Britain, and some were made locally, at centres such as Patna and Kapadvanj in Gujarat. Mirror glass was used for decoration in the “mirror palaces” (shīsh maḥal) of the Mughal and Rajput courts, such as those of the Agra fort and of Amber, whose walls and ceilings are set with countless small pieces of convex mirror in stucco. In the 18th and 19th centuries, imported European glass, including chandeliers and coloured glass, became fashionable among Indian elites and gradually displaced local production of fine glassware.

    12.4 Glass Bangles¶

    Glass bangles have been worn in South Asia since at least the early historic period and carry social meanings—in many communities they mark married status, and their breaking marks widowhood. In medieval and modern times bangle-making became concentrated in a few centres, above all Firozabad in Uttar Pradesh, where the industry grew from the late 19th century. Glass is melted in furnaces, drawn into coils on rotating rods, cut, joined and decorated, largely in small workshops and homes. The industry has long been associated with hazardous working conditions—heat, fumes and burns—and with the use of child labour, which has been the subject of repeated official and non-governmental intervention.

    12.5 Pottery¶

    Pottery is among the oldest crafts of South Asia. At Mehrgarh in Baluchistan pottery appears in the later Neolithic levels, after an earlier aceramic phase. Table 12.2 gives a very brief outline of some of the principal wares.

    Table 12.2. Some Major Ceramic Traditions of South Asia
    Period Ware Characteristics
    c. 6th mill. BCE onwards Mehrgarh and early village pottery Hand-made, later wheel-made painted wares
    c. 2600–1900 BCE Harappan Wheel-thrown red ware with black painting; standardised forms; kilns
    c. 1200–600 BCE Painted Grey Ware Fine grey ware with black painting, Ganga–Yamuna plain
    c. 700–200 BCE Northern Black Polished Ware Fine ware with a glossy black (or other coloured) surface
    early centuries CE Rouletted Ware, Red Polished Ware Fine wares, partly connected with maritime trade
    from c. 13th c. CE Glazed wares and tiles Lead- and alkali-glazed pottery and tiles under the Sultanates and Mughals

    12.5.1 Northern Black Polished Ware¶

    Northern Black Polished Ware (NBPW), the fine ware of the early historic cities of the Ganga plain, has a thin, lustrous surface, usually black but sometimes golden, red or steel-blue. Its surface appears to be a very fine slip, fired in a reducing atmosphere and then cooled in a way that preserved its dark colour and sheen; the exact technique is still debated. NBPW is a technically demanding ware, comparable in fineness to the best Mediterranean wares of the period.

    12.5.2 Terracotta¶

    Terracotta, low-fired unglazed clay, has been used in South Asia for figurines from the Harappan period, for the moulded plaques and figures of the early historic period, and for architectural decoration. The brick temples of Bengal, especially those of Bishnupur (17th–18th centuries), are covered with terracotta panels showing scenes from the epics, the Kṛṣṇa legend and contemporary life.

    12.5.3 Glazes and Tiles¶

    True glazes on pottery appear in South Asia mainly with the Islamic period. Glazed tiles adorn the tombs and mosques of Multan, Uch and Thatta in the Indus valley (14th–18th centuries), the monuments of the Delhi Sultanate, and Mughal buildings such as the Lahore Fort, where mosaics and painted tiles (kāshīkārī) in Persian style were used on a large scale. The “blue pottery” of Jaipur, made from a body of ground quartz and glass with very little clay and painted in cobalt and copper colours, is a 19th-century development of Persianate traditions.

    12.5.4 Kilns¶

    Indian potters used open firings (pots stacked with fuel in the open or in a shallow pit) for much everyday ware, and updraught kilns of various kinds for higher-quality ware and bricks. Controlling the atmosphere (oxidising or reducing) and the cooling, as much as the maximum temperature, determined the colour and finish of the product.

    12.6 Bricks¶

    Fired brick is one of the great ceramic achievements of the Harappan civilisation. The Harappan cities used sun-dried and kiln-fired bricks on a huge scale, made in moulds to standard proportions—the length, breadth and thickness of most bricks are in the ratio 4:2:1, whatever their absolute size—and laid in bonds that are still standard today. Fired bricks were used where durability against water mattered: for drains, wells, bathing platforms and the Great Bath of Mohenjo-daro, which was made watertight with carefully fitted bricks, gypsum mortar and a layer of bitumen (Kenoyer 1998; Possehl 2002).

    Fired brick returned to large-scale use in the cities of the early historic period and remained the main building material of the Ganga plain, where stone is scarce, through the Buddhist stūpas and monasteries, the brick temples of the Gupta period (such as Bhitargaon) and of Bengal, and the Sultanate and Mughal buildings of the north. Brick-making requires the selection and preparation of clay, the control of shrinkage in drying, and the firing of very large quantities in clamps or kilns—a major consumer of fuel and of labour.

    12.7 Crucibles and Refractories¶

    Some of the most demanding ceramics were made not for use in the home but for metallurgy: the crucibles of crucible steel production (Chapter 6), the retorts and furnace plates of Zawar (Chapter 7), the clay tuyères of iron-smelting furnaces, the cupels used for refining silver and gold, and the sealed earthen dishes of the rasa laboratory. These had to withstand high temperatures for long periods, resist thermal shock and chemical attack, and be cheap enough to be discarded after one use. Potters achieved these properties by selecting refractory clays and adding temper—rice husk, sand, crushed potsherd—whose combination of porosity and refractoriness suited each purpose (Srinivasan and Ranganathan 2004; Rehren and Papachristou 2003; Craddock 2017). These utilitarian ceramics are a little-noticed but essential part of the history of Indian technology.

    12.8 Lime, Mortars and Plasters¶

    Indian builders used a variety of binding materials, the main ones of which are listed in Table 12.3.

    Table 12.3. Some Traditional Binding Materials
    Material Names Composition Uses
    Mud mortar — Clay and silt Common walling
    Lime mortar sudhā, cūnā Lime with sand Masonry, plaster
    Surkhī mortar surkhī Lime with crushed burnt brick (a hydraulic additive) Masonry, waterworks
    Shell lime — Lime burnt from shells Coastal building, plaster
    Polished lime plaster ārāish (Rajasthan) Fine lime, polished, sometimes with additives Wall surfaces with a marble-like finish
    Vajralepa vajralepa Plant materials and resins boiled to a glue (Bṛhatsaṃhitā) Adhesive and coating

    Lime mortars set by reacting with carbon dioxide from the air; the addition of crushed brick (surkhī) makes them partly hydraulic, able to set in damp conditions, which made surkhī mortar valuable for tanks, wells and bridges. Many Indian builders also added organic materials to lime—jaggery, plant gums, fruit pulps, pulses—and modern studies suggest that some of these modify the setting and workability of the mortar.

    The Bṛhatsaṃhitā has a short chapter on vajralepa, “adamantine plaster”, a glue made by boiling various fruits, barks, resins and other substances, which it recommends for temples, images, walls and wells (Bhat 1981–1982). Popular claims that vajralepa was a “super-cement” stronger than modern concrete have no basis in tests of the recipes; it was an adhesive and coating rather than a structural cement.

    12.8.1 Gypsum¶

    The second mineral binder of South Asia is gypsum, hydrated calcium sulphate, which occurs in large beds in the arid north-west; Rajasthan supplies most of India’s gypsum today. Its chemistry is simpler than that of lime. Heated gently, to little more than the boiling point of water, gypsum loses three-quarters of its water and becomes a fine white powder, the hemihydrate known as plaster of Paris:

    \[\text{CaSO}_4\cdot 2\,\text{H}_2\text{O} \longrightarrow \text{CaSO}_4\cdot \tfrac{1}{2}\,\text{H}_2\text{O} + 1\tfrac{1}{2}\,\text{H}_2\text{O} .\]

    Mixed with water, the powder takes it up again and sets within minutes as a mass of interlocking gypsum crystals, expanding slightly as it does so. Overheated, it loses all its water and becomes “dead-burnt” anhydrite, which sets only very slowly. Because it sets quickly and fills fine detail, plaster is ideal for moulds and casts; because gypsum is appreciably soluble in water, it is less durable than lime in exposed walls, and was used mainly where it could be kept dry. The Harappan builders used gypsum mortar in the Great Bath of Mohenjo-daro (Section 12.6).

    Gypsum has two modern uses in India that connect with other chapters of this volume. It is the standard amendment for reclaiming sodic soils, the barren ūsar lands of the Ganga plain whose alkaline salts were once collected as reh (Section 13.3): calcium from the gypsum displaces the sodium held by the clay, and the sodium is washed out as soluble sulphate (Volume 15, Kṛṣi and Jīva Vijñāna). And plaster of Paris has largely replaced clay for the festival images immersed in rivers and lakes, with consequences for water quality discussed in Volume 17, Śilpa–Kāru.

    12.9 Stone: Weathering, Polish and Conservation¶

    Mortars and plasters were applied to stone and brick, and the chemistry of the stone itself—how it was chosen, finished and preserved, and how it decays—belongs in the same story. The architecture and sculpture made from stone are the subject of Volume 16, Vāstu–Śilpa; this section is concerned with the materials.

    12.9.1 Building Stones¶

    India’s builders and sculptors used the stone of their regions, and the chemistry of each stone shaped what could be done with it. The sandstones of the Vindhyan ranges—the buff stone of Chunar near Varanasi used for the Mauryan pillars, the red stone of the Agra, Fatehpur Sikri and Dholpur region used for Mughal buildings—consist of grains of quartz bound by a natural cement, and their durability depends on that cement: sandstones bound by silica are hard and lasting, those bound by calcite are vulnerable to acid, and those rich in clay swell and flake with repeated wetting and drying. Marble, such as the white marble of Makrana in Rajasthan used for the Taj Mahal, is recrystallised calcium carbonate: it takes a fine polish and can be carved in great detail, but it is attacked by acids. The granites and charnockites of Tamil Nadu and Karnataka, made of quartz, feldspars and dark silicate minerals, are among the most durable building stones, though hard to carve; they weather slowly as the feldspars are converted to clay. The basalt of the Deccan Traps, into which the caves of Ellora were cut, and the soft schists of the Hoysaḷa temples and of Gandhāra each had their own working properties.

    The colours that give Indian buildings much of their character are also chemical. The red of the Mughal sandstones comes from thin films of iron oxide (haematite) coating the quartz grains; where the iron was removed by ground water, or reduced to its paler ferrous state around decaying organic matter, the stone is buff, white or greenish, and the spotted and banded stones prized by builders record such differences in the rock. The black of the polished basalt and of the dark chloritic stones used for images comes from iron- and magnesium-rich silicate minerals, and the green and grey tones of many schists from chlorite. Builders exploited these differences deliberately, as in the inlay of white marble into red sandstone that distinguishes the façades of Mughal Delhi and Agra (Volume 16, Vāstu–Śilpa). The same iron oxides that colour the stone are responsible for the rusty stains that disfigure pale marble and sandstone when iron in or near the surface weathers.

    Laterite, the red, iron-rich rock of the Western Ghats, the Konkan, Kerala and Odisha, is a product of tropical weathering, in which rain leaches silica and other soluble components from the parent rock and leaves a residue rich in the oxides and hydroxides of iron and aluminium. Fresh laterite is soft enough to be cut into blocks with a pick, and it hardens on exposure to the air as it dries out and its iron compounds crystallise. Francis Buchanan, travelling in Malabar in 1800–1801, described this property and gave the rock its name, from the Latin later, “brick”, in the account he published in 1807. Laterite blocks, often plastered with lime, are the traditional walling material of coastal Karnataka, Goa and Kerala.

    12.9.2 The Mauryan Polish¶

    The pillars and animal capitals set up in the reign of Aśoka (3rd century BCE), and the interiors of the rock-cut Barabar caves in Bihar, have a lustrous, almost glassy polish that has impressed observers since they were first described. How it was produced is not known with certainty. The usual view is that it was obtained by prolonged grinding and burnishing with progressively finer abrasives; others have proposed that a coating or surface treatment was applied. The few published scientific examinations have not settled the question, and the polish remains one of the open problems of early Indian stone technology. Claims that the Mauryan craftsmen possessed a lost chemical secret go well beyond the evidence.

    12.9.3 Decay and Repair¶

    Stone decays by physical, chemical and biological processes acting together. Rain made weakly acid by carbon dioxide slowly dissolves marble and calcareous sandstone, and sulphur dioxide from burning coal and oil turns calcite into the more soluble gypsum, which washes away or forms blackened crusts; soluble salts carried in by ground water crystallise in the pores and break the stone from within; and iron cramps rust and swell, cracking the blocks they were meant to hold. The best-known Indian case is the Taj Mahal. A Supreme Court order of 1996 moved or converted polluting industries in the Taj Trapezium Zone around it, but the marble has continued to discolour, and a study published in 2015 attributed the discoloration mainly to deposited black and brown carbon particles and dust rather than to chemical attack on the stone (Bergin et al. 2015). The chemical lesson of 20th-century conservation, learned at some cost with Portland cement, is that repairs should be softer and more permeable than the stone they protect, as traditional lime plasters were.

    12.10 Living Traditions¶

    Many of the crafts discussed in this chapter survive: village potters work in every region of India; terracotta figures are made for shrines and festivals; Khurja in Uttar Pradesh and Jaipur produce glazed wares; and Firozabad remains the centre of glass bangles. These traditions are under pressure from industrial products and changing tastes, and many of their practitioners are poor. Their history is part of the history of Indian chemistry: the control of materials, temperature and atmosphere in kilns and furnaces was as much an empirical chemical skill as anything practised in the alchemist’s laboratory.

    What Is Secure and What Is Debated

    Secure: the Harappan vitreous crafts formed an interlinked high-temperature technology; South Asian mineral-soda-alumina glass and the drawn Indo-Pacific beads were traded from East Africa to Korea; and the chemistry of lime, gypsum and the common building stones is well understood. Debated: which glazing methods the Harappans used; when glass proper was first made in South Asia; how the Northern Black Polished Ware surface and the Mauryan polish were produced; and the composition and purpose of vajralepa.
