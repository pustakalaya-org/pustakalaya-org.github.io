    Chapter 3: Pārada: Mercury in Alchemy, Medicine and Toxicology¶

    3.1 Mercury in the Rasa Tradition¶

    Mercury is the centre of rasaśāstra. The rasa texts identify it with Śiva, and more particularly with Śiva’s semen; they praise it in hymns, worship it in the form of a liṅga, and promise that mercury properly processed and “bound” will transmute metals and confer an imperishable body (White 1996). Its many names were explained in the same spirit. The Rasaratnasamuccaya derives four of them from what mercury does:

    Rasaratnasamuccaya 1.77–79a

    rasanāt sarvadhātūnāṃ rasa ity abhidhīyate
    jarāruṅmṛtyunāśāya rasyate vā raso mataḥ
    rasoparasarājatvād rasendra iti kīrtitaḥ
    dehalohamayīṃ siddhiṃ sūte sūtas tataḥ smṛtaḥ
    rogapaṅkābdhimagnānāṃ pāradānāc ca pāradaḥ
    It is called rasa because it “tastes” (that is, consumes) all the metals; or it is held to be rasa because it is tasted to destroy old age, disease and death. Because it is king of the rasas and uparasas it is celebrated as rasendra. Because it brings forth (sūte) perfection in body and in metal it is known as sūta; and because it gives the far shore (pāra-da) to those sunk in the ocean of the mire of disease, it is pārada.

    The etymologies are not philology but doctrine: in four words they state the tradition’s claims over metals, over the body, and over disease. The passage is quoted from the GRETIL e-text of the Ānandāśrama edition, whose verse numbers may differ slightly from those of other printings (GRETIL 2020).

    The properties that made mercury so fascinating are real. It is the only metal that is liquid at ordinary temperatures; it is very dense (about 13.5 times as dense as water), so that iron floats on it; it dissolves gold, silver, tin and other metals to form amalgams; it combines readily with sulphur, forming a black sulphide on grinding and a brilliant red one (vermilion) on sublimation; and it vaporises on heating, apparently vanishing, and reappears as droplets on a cool surface. To a pre-modern observer, a substance that could “eat” gold, change colour from silver to black to red, and escape and return through fire must have seemed uniquely alive.

    3.1.1 Sources of Mercury¶

    The principal ore of mercury is cinnabar (mercuric sulphide), Sanskrit hiṅgula or darada. The second name connects it with the Daradas, a people of the north-western mountains, and the rasa texts tell a myth of mercury welling up from a deep well in the far north-west (White 1996). India has few workable deposits of cinnabar, and most of the mercury used in Indian alchemy and medicine must have been imported—from Central Asia, from China, and in the early modern period from Spain (Almadén) via European traders. The rasa texts describe obtaining mercury from cinnabar by heating it and collecting the mercury in a cooled vessel, a process known in the Mediterranean world since antiquity.

    Health and Safety Note

    Mercury is a poison in all its forms: its vapour damages the brain, nerves and kidneys, its soluble salts are corrosive, and even mercuric sulphide is absorbed to some extent and accumulates with long use (Liu et al. 2008). No procedures or doses are given; see the full note in Section 1.1.

    3.2 The Eighteen Saṃskāras¶

    The rasa texts subject mercury to a long sequence of operations, usually counted as eighteen; the usual list was given in Table 2.3 (Chapter 2). The list and its order vary somewhat between works (White 1996; Ray 1956). The first eight are described as preparing mercury for medicinal use; the remainder belong to transmutational and bodily alchemy.

    3.2.1 What the Operations Were¶

    The texts describe the operations in compressed verse, with long lists of plant juices, mineral powders and other materials. Read historically, they fall into a few types.

    Washing and grinding (1–2).
    Mercury is heated in plant decoctions and ground with plant juices and mineral or brick powders. Such treatment would remove dirt and some surface oxides, and abrasive powders would carry off solid impurities.
    “Swooning” and “raising” (3–4).
    Mercury is ground until it loses its fluidity and lustre and becomes a dull paste—a dispersion of fine droplets in the grinding medium—and is then recovered as liquid metal.
    Sublimation and distillation (5).
    Mercury is driven off by heat and condensed in another vessel, upwards, downwards or sideways. Distillation separates mercury from less volatile metals such as lead and tin and is the one operation in the list that genuinely purifies the metal.
    Further “awakening” and “kindling” (6–8).
    Operations intended to restore the “vigour” and “appetite” of mercury after the preceding treatments, so that it will “eat” other substances.
    “Feeding” and “digestion” (9–16).
    Mercury is made to absorb mica, metals (gold above all), sulphur and other substances, to dissolve them and to be “coloured” by them. In chemical terms this involves the formation of amalgams and sulphides; in the texts’ terms, the mercury grows in strength with each substance it consumes.
    Application (17–18).
    The perfected mercury “pierces” base metals, transmuting them into gold, and is consumed to perfect the body.

    The later operations, and especially vedha, rest on the alchemical theory that transmutation is possible. It is not: gold cannot be made from base metals by chemical means. Apparent transmutations in alchemical practice can be explained by gilding, by colouring alloys, or by the recovery of gold already present in the materials.

    3.3 Binding Mercury and the Mercury Liṅga¶

    “Binding” (bandha) mercury—making it stable in fire and no longer fluid—is the alchemist’s central goal, and the texts distinguish many kinds of binding by the substances and methods used. A solid, “bound” mercury could be shaped into a liṅga and worshipped, or into beads and pills. Chemically, mercury can be made solid at room temperature only by combining it: with sulphur (as mercuric sulphide) or with other metals as an amalgam. Objects sold in India today as “mercury liṅgas” (pārad śivliṅg) are generally amalgams; handling or heating them can release mercury vapour.

    3.4 Mercury and Sulphur: Kajjalī and Rasasindūra¶

    Many mercurial medicines of the rasa texts are based on two mercury–sulphur products.

    • Kajjalī (“lamp-black”) is a black powder made by prolonged grinding of mercury with sulphur. Modern analyses identify it as black mercuric sulphide (metacinnabar), usually with some unreacted mercury and sulphur.

    • Rasasindūra (“mercury vermilion”) and related products are made by heating mercury–sulphur mixtures in a clay-coated glass bottle embedded in a sand-bath (the kūpīpakva, “bottle-cooked”, method) until a red sublimate collects in the neck. This is red mercuric sulphide (cinnabar or vermilion).

    • Makaradhvaja and similar preparations are sublimed mercuric sulphide products, in some recipes made with gold, which the texts praise as rejuvenatives and aphrodisiacs.

    Vermilion had long been made in this way in many parts of the world as a pigment; its production by sublimation was known in China and in medieval Europe. What is distinctive in the rasa texts is its internal medicinal use.

    The texts claim that these products, prepared from properly processed mercury and sulphur, are not only harmless but powerfully therapeutic. Modern work has shown that mercuric sulphide is much less soluble, and much less readily absorbed from the gut, than mercury salts or methylmercury. It has not shown that it is harmless. Experimental studies find that cinnabar is absorbed to some degree, accumulates in the kidney and brain with prolonged use, and can cause toxicity; very small particle size and organic coatings may alter its absorption in ways that are not well understood (Liu et al. 2008). Products sold as mercury–sulphur preparations can also contain free mercury and other heavy metals.

    3.5 Mercurial Preparations in the Pharmacopoeia¶

    From the 13th century onwards the rasa compendia include hundreds of compound medicines containing mercury, usually in the form of kajjalī or rasasindūra, often combined with other minerals (mica, copper, iron, arsenic sulphides, aconite) and herbs. The texts name them after their claimed actions or ingredients—“fever-destroying”, “lord of the elixirs” and so on—and recommend them for fevers, digestive complaints, skin diseases, respiratory diseases, impotence and as general rejuvenatives. Many such preparations are still manufactured. Their claimed benefits rest on the authority of the texts and on practitioners’ experience; they have not been established by adequate clinical trials, while their content of mercury and other heavy metals is well documented (Saper et al. 2008; Ernst 2002).

    3.6 What the Texts Knew of Mercury’s Dangers¶

    The rasa authors knew that mercury was dangerous. They warn that impure or improperly processed mercury causes a list of diseases and can kill, and they built the doctrine of mercury’s “defects” (doṣa) and their removal on this knowledge (Chapter 2). Their response, however, was to multiply the processing steps rather than to abandon internal use. From the standpoint of modern toxicology, the defects that matter are not the “poison”, “fire” and “dirt” of the texts but the mercury itself, and no traditional operation removes the toxicity of a mercury compound taken into the body. The tradition was not unusual in this: European medicine, too, gave patients mercury for centuries (Section 3.8), and what has changed is the toxicological knowledge now available to every tradition. The Tibetan form of mercury processing, tsotel, which raises the same questions, is described in Chapter 2.

    3.7 A Modern Chemical Reading¶

    From the standpoint of modern chemistry, the rasa operations on mercury may be summarised as follows:

    1. mechanical cleaning by washing, grinding and filtering through cloth;

    2. separation of mercury from less volatile metals by distillation;

    3. formation of mercury compounds, especially the black and red sulphides, and of amalgams with gold, silver, tin and other metals;

    4. a large number of treatments with plant materials whose chemical effect, if any, is unknown.

    The first three are real chemical operations and show genuine practical skill in handling a difficult and dangerous substance. They were embedded, however, in a theory—the progressive perfecting of mercury until it could transmute metals and the body—that has no chemical basis.

    3.8 Mercury in World History¶

    Mercury and cinnabar were known and used across the ancient world long before the rasa texts. Cinnabar was used as a red pigment in Neolithic Anatolia and in China, where it was also placed in tombs. The Greek philosopher Theophrastus (c. 300 BCE) describes the preparation of quicksilver by grinding cinnabar with vinegar in a copper mortar, and the Roman writers Vitruvius, Dioscorides and Pliny describe both the extraction of mercury from cinnabar and its use for gilding and for recovering gold. The great mines of Almadén in Spain were worked in Roman times and supplied much of the world’s mercury until the 20th century (Craddock 1995).

    Two uses of mercury were especially important in pre-modern technology:

    • Fire-gilding. An amalgam of gold and mercury is spread on a copper or silver object and heated; the mercury evaporates, leaving a thin layer of gold. Fire-gilding was used in South Asia as elsewhere, for example on images and on metalwork, and was extremely dangerous to the gilders, who breathed the mercury vapour.

    • Amalgamation. Mercury dissolves fine gold and silver, and can be used to collect them from crushed ore or river sand; the mercury is then driven off by heat. From the 16th century the Spanish used amalgamation on a huge scale to extract silver in the Americas, with terrible consequences for the miners.

    In China the pursuit of elixirs of immortality made from cinnabar and mercury caused many deaths, including those of several emperors of the Tang dynasty. In Europe mercury was a standard medicine from the 16th to the early 20th century, especially for syphilis, and mercurous chloride (calomel) remained in use as a purgative and in teething powders into the 20th century, when it was found to cause “pink disease” (acrodynia) in infants. The harm to patients was very serious. The rasa tradition’s medicinal use of mercury is therefore part of a widespread pre-modern pattern.

    3.9 Vermilion¶

    Red mercuric sulphide, made by subliming mercury with sulphur or by grinding natural cinnabar, has been prized as a pigment for millennia. In India vermilion was used in painting, in manuscript illumination, and—with red lead, red ochre and, in modern times, synthetic dyes—in the sindūr applied to the hair parting by married Hindu women and to images of deities. The name sindūra covers several red powders, and its composition in any particular case can only be determined by analysis. Lead- and mercury-containing sindūr products are a source of exposure to these metals (Chapter 11).

    3.10 Mercury Today¶

    Mercury is now recognised as a global pollutant. Released into the environment by coal burning, small-scale gold mining, industry and waste, it is converted by micro-organisms into methylmercury, which accumulates in fish and is highly toxic to the developing brain—as the disaster at Minamata in Japan, from the 1950s, showed. The Minamata Convention on Mercury, adopted in 2013 and in force since 2017, commits its parties, including India, to reduce the use and release of mercury, to phase out mercury-containing products such as many thermometers and batteries, and to control mercury trade. Traditional medicines are not its main target, but the convention reflects a global consensus that human exposure to mercury should be minimised. The continued use of mercury as a medicine sits uneasily with that consensus.

    3.11 Comparative Mercury Alchemy¶

    Table 3.1 sets the Indian treatment of mercury beside those of Chinese and Arabic alchemy.

    Table 3.1. Mercury in Three Alchemical Traditions (simplified)
    Feature Indian rasaśāstra Chinese alchemy Arabic alchemy
    Main aims Transmutation; bodily immortality; medicine Elixirs of immortality (waidan); later meditative neidan Transmutation; also medicine
    Key substances Mercury, sulphur, mica, gold Cinnabar, mercury, lead Mercury, sulphur, sal ammoniac
    Theory of metals Mercury as Śiva’s seed; sulphur as the Goddess’s fluid Yin–yang and the five phases Metals formed from mercury and sulphur
    Processing Eighteen saṃskāras Repeated cyclical transformations (“nine-cycled elixir”) Distillation, sublimation, calcination
    Fate Absorbed into Āyurvedic pharmacy Displaced by neidan Transmitted to Latin Europe

    The resemblances are close, and all three traditions handled mercury with skill. The Indian tradition is distinctive in its Tantric religious framework and in the way its medicinal branch survived into modern institutional medicine. Whether the traditions were historically connected remains an open question (White 1996; Principe 2013).

    What Is Secure and What Is Debated

    Secure: the chemistry behind the main operations—washing and grinding, distillation, and the formation of amalgams and of black and red mercuric sulphide—and the fact that most mercury used in India was imported. Secure, too, is that the texts knew raw mercury to be dangerous. Debated: how far the mercury–sulphur products of the pharmacopoeia are absorbed and what harm they do in the doses used, a question on which the studies cited in Chapter 9 disagree; and whether the Indian, Chinese and Arabic mercury alchemies were historically connected.
