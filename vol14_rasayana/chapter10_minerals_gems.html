    Chapter 10: Ratnaparīkṣā: Gems and Minerals¶

    10.1 The Indian Literature on Gems¶

    South Asia was for many centuries a principal source of diamonds, pearls, corundum (ruby and sapphire, especially from Sri Lanka and Burma), beryl, garnet, agate and carnelian, and a centre of gem cutting and trade. A specialised literature on the examination of gems (ratnaparīkṣā) grew up around this trade. Its main texts are (Finot 1896; Bhat 1981–1982):

    • the Arthaśāstra (composite, c. 50 BCE–300 CE), whose chapter on articles to be received into the treasury (2.11) describes pearls, gems and diamonds, their sources, qualities and defects (Olivelle 2013);

    • the gem chapters of Varāhamihira’s Bṛhatsaṃhitā (6th century), on gems in general and diamonds, on pearls, on rubies and on emeralds (Bhat 1981–1982);

    • the Ratnaparīkṣā of Buddhabhaṭṭa, the Agastimata and other lapidary treatises, of uncertain date, collected and translated by Louis Finot (Finot 1896); similar material appears in the Garuḍa Purāṇa;

    • the Ratnaparīkṣā of Ṭhakkura Pherū (early 14th century; on its author see Section 8.12);

    • the rasa texts, which treat gems as materials for medicines.

    Outside Sanskrit, al-Bīrūnī’s Arabic book on precious stones (Section 10.10) and the 17th-century French jeweller Jean-Baptiste Tavernier’s eyewitness account of the diamond mines and markets of the Deccan are the most important sources (Tavernier 1889).

    The gem texts are practical manuals for connoisseurs and merchants: they describe the sources of each stone, its colours, its qualities and defects, the methods of distinguishing genuine stones from imitations, and how to value it. They are also full of myth (for instance, of gems formed from the body of a slain demon) and of beliefs about the good or ill fortune that stones bring their owners.

    10.2 The Nine Gems¶

    In medieval and modern Indian usage nine gems (navaratna) form a canonical set, associated in astrology with the nine “planets” (navagraha). The association of particular stones with particular planets is a later astrological development; it is not found in the early gem literature, and it has no basis in mineralogy. Table 10.1 gives the usual set.

    Table 10.1. The Nine Gems (Navaratna): Mineral Identity and Astrological Association
    Sanskrit English Mineral Planet (astrological)
    māṇikya, padmarāga Ruby Corundum (red, coloured by chromium) Sun
    muktā, mauktika Pearl Calcium carbonate (biogenic) Moon
    pravāla Coral Calcium carbonate (biogenic) Mars
    marakata Emerald Beryl (green) Mercury
    puṣparāga Yellow sapphire or topaz Corundum (yellow) or topaz Jupiter
    vajra Diamond Carbon Venus
    nīla, indranīla Blue sapphire Corundum (blue) Saturn
    gomeda Hessonite Garnet (grossular) Rāhu
    vaidūrya Cat’s eye Chrysoberyl (chatoyant); earlier texts may mean beryl Ketu

    The identifications of several Sanskrit names, especially puṣparāga and vaidūrya, varied over time; vaidūrya in particular is thought to have denoted beryl in early usage before it came to mean cat’s eye.

    10.3 Diamonds¶

    10.3.1 Sources¶

    Until diamonds were discovered in Brazil in the 1720s, India was the world’s principal source of diamonds; smaller quantities came from Borneo. The most famous Indian diamonds came from alluvial deposits along the Krishna river and its tributaries in the eastern Deccan, worked most intensively in the 16th and 17th centuries and marketed through the kingdom of Golconda—hence “Golconda diamonds”. The mines at Kollur, visited by Tavernier, were among the most productive. Diamonds were also obtained in central India around Panna, where the Majhgawan kimberlite pipe is still mined, and in eastern India (Tavernier 1889; Biswas and Biswas 1996).

    10.3.2 The Diamond in Indian Thought¶

    The Sanskrit word for diamond, vajra, also denotes the thunderbolt of Indra, and the two meanings reinforce each other: the diamond is the hardest of substances, unbreakable and able to cut all others. In Buddhism the vajra became a central symbol of the indestructible nature of enlightenment, giving its name to the Vajrayāna, the “diamond” or “thunderbolt” vehicle of Tantric Buddhism, and to texts such as the Vajracchedikā (“Diamond-Cutter”) sūtra. Poets used the diamond as an image of hardness and brilliance, and the gem texts surrounded it with stories of its divine origin. These associations help to explain the diamond’s prestige, but they are a matter of symbolism rather than of mineralogy.

    10.3.3 Tavernier at the Mines¶

    Jean-Baptiste Tavernier, a French gem merchant who made six journeys to the East between 1631 and 1668, visited the diamond mines of the Deccan and described them in his Travels (Tavernier 1889). He describes large numbers of labourers—men, women and children—digging the diamond-bearing gravels, washing and sieving them, and searching the residue under close supervision; the miners’ wages were very low, and the stones belonged to the merchants who rented the ground and to the king, who reserved the largest stones. He also describes the merchants’ methods of bargaining and payment, and the Indian practice of cutting stones to preserve weight. Tavernier’s account is a rare view of the labour behind the gems celebrated in courtly literature, and it shows how the diamond trade linked Golconda with Mughal, Persian and European markets.

    10.3.4 Classification and Valuation¶

    The gem texts classify diamonds by colour, transparency, shape, size and defects. The ideal diamond is colourless, transparent, sharp-edged and naturally well-formed (octahedral crystals were prized); defects include spots, lines, cracks, cloudiness and irregular shape. The texts also assign diamonds of different colours to the four social classes (varṇa)—white to the Brahmin, reddish to the Kṣatriya, yellowish to the Vaiśya, dark to the Śūdra—and prescribe that each should wear the diamond of his own class (Finot 1896; Bhat 1981–1982). This classification projects the social hierarchy of the law books onto the mineral world; it tells us more about Brahmanical ideology than about diamonds.

    Indian jewellers traditionally preserved weight rather than cutting stones to maximise brilliance, and polished the natural crystal faces; European visitors often criticised Indian cutting for this reason. The modern “brilliant” cut is a European development of the 17th–20th centuries, and the modern grading by colour, clarity, cut and carat weight was codified in the 20th century. The Indian texts’ attention to colour, clarity and weight reflects the practical concerns of any diamond trade rather than an anticipation of modern grading.

    Historical Note: The Koh-i-Noor

    Popular accounts trace the Koh-i-Noor to the Kākatīya kingdom of Warangal and the Kollur mines, and identify it with diamonds mentioned in earlier chronicles. Dalrymple and Anand argue that its history is secure only from the 18th century: it was among the treasures carried off from Delhi by Nādir Shāh in 1739, passed to the Afghan Durrānī rulers and then to Ranjit Singh of the Sikh empire, and was surrendered to the East India Company under the Treaty of Lahore in 1849. Recut in London in 1852, it was set in successive British crowns and is now in the crown made for Queen Elizabeth the Queen Mother. Its return has been requested by India and by other countries (Dalrymple and Anand 2017).

    10.4 Corundum: Ruby and Sapphire¶

    Ruby and sapphire are both corundum (aluminium oxide), coloured respectively by chromium and by iron and titanium. The gem texts discuss rubies (padmarāga, māṇikya) at length, classify them by shade, and distinguish them from red spinel and garnet. Historically, the finest rubies reaching India came from Burma (the Mogok region) and many sapphires and other gem-gravel stones from Sri Lanka; India’s own famous sapphire deposit, in the Paddar area of the Zanskar range in Kashmir, was discovered only in the 1880s.     Yellow sapphire puṣparāga (in part) Iron Sri Lanka
    Star ruby/sapphire — Rutile inclusions (asterism) Sri Lanka, Burma

    10.5 Emerald and Beryl¶

    Emerald (marakata) is green beryl coloured by chromium or vanadium. India has only minor emerald deposits, in Rajasthan, which were found in the 20th century. In antiquity emeralds reached India from Egypt; the large emeralds of Mughal jewellery came mainly from Colombia, mined by the Spanish from the 16th century and traded to India through European and Middle Eastern merchants. Mughal craftsmen carved and engraved them, and many famous “Mughal emeralds” are Colombian stones. Aquamarine and other beryls occur in Indian pegmatites.

    10.6 Pearls and Coral¶

    The pearl fisheries of the Gulf of Mannar, between south India and Sri Lanka, were among the most important in the world from antiquity: the Periplus mentions them, and Marco Polo described the divers and the royal control of the fishery in the late 13th century (Casson 1989; Yule and Cordier 1903). Pearls also came from the Persian Gulf. The Bṛhatsaṃhitā lists the sources of pearls as including not only oysters but also legendary sources—elephants, serpents, clouds, bamboo, fish, boars and conches—and grades pearls by size, roundness, lustre, colour and weight (Bhat 1981–1982). Coral (pravāla) was imported from the Mediterranean and the Red Sea.

    10.7 Agate, Carnelian and the Bead Industry¶

    Beyond the nine gems, the texts and the trade recognised many “secondary” stones (uparatna): agate, carnelian, onyx, rock crystal (sphaṭika), garnet, amethyst, lapis lazuli, turquoise and others. Some were local; lapis lazuli came from Badakhshan in Afghanistan, and turquoise from Iran.

    The agate and carnelian bead industry is one of the oldest continuous crafts of South Asia. Harappan bead-makers heated carnelian to deepen its red colour—the heat converts iron hydroxides in the stone to red iron oxide—and bored long beads with specially made drills of a hard stone that archaeologists call “Ernestite” (Kenoyer 1998). Their beads reached Mesopotamia. Some carry white lines and patterns made by painting the surface with an alkaline paste and then heating it, which whitens the treated areas; these etched carnelian beads, found in Mesopotamia as well as in the Indus cities, are among the earliest known deliberate chemical treatments of a stone surface, and the technique continued in South Asia long after the Harappan period (Kenoyer 1998). In historical times the centre of the industry has been Khambhat (Cambay) in Gujarat, using stones from the Ratanpur area of the Rajpipla hills, and Khambhat beads were traded across the Indian Ocean for centuries (Francis 2002).

    10.8 Shell¶

    Shell was one of the most important ornamental materials of South Asia. Harappan craftsmen worked the large marine gastropod Turbinella pyrum (the sacred conch, śaṅkha) and other shells from the coasts of Gujarat and Baluchistan into bangles, beads, inlays and ladles, sawing the heavy shells with bronze saws in specialised workshops at sites such as Nageshwar and Balakot (Kenoyer 1998). The tradition of conch-shell bangles continued through the historical period and survives in Bengal, where white shell bangles (śākhā) are worn by married women. Shell, pearl and coral are chemically similar—mostly calcium carbonate—and the rasa texts treat them together as materials for calcined preparations.

    10.9 Testing Gems¶

    The gem texts describe practical tests, some of them effective and some fanciful:

    • examination of colour in different lights and against different backgrounds;

    • hardness, tested by scratching—the diamond scratches every other stone;

    • weight in relation to size (a crude estimate of density);

    • examination of transparency, inclusions and flaws;

    • tests by fire and by water, some of which would damage genuine stones or distinguish them from glass imitations.

    The texts also mention optical properties that modern gemology recognises—the “fire” of diamond, the moving line of light on cat’s eye (chatoyancy), the star of some sapphires and rubies—without, of course, explaining them. Claims that a description of diamond as displaying “seven colours” anticipates Newton’s analysis of the spectrum read too much into a conventional poetic number.

    10.10 Gem Knowledge at the Sultanate and Mughal Courts¶

    From the 11th century, Arabic and Persian learning added another layer to the knowledge of gems in India. Al-Bīrūnī’s book on precious stones and metals (Kitāb al-jamāhir fī maʿrifat al-jawāhir), written in Ghazna in the 1040s and available in an English translation (al-Bīrūnī, Abū Rayḥān 1989), discusses gems, pearls and metals, their sources, prices, tests and imitations, drawing on Indian as well as Greek, Persian and Arabic informants and recording Indian names and lore. It also records the specific gravities of many stones and metals, which al-Bīrūnī measured himself with remarkable accuracy; the table later reproduced in the Āʾīn-i Akbarī is discussed in Section 8.13. In the early 14th century Ṭhakkura Pherū (Section 8.12) wrote a Ratnaparīkṣā that describes the gems handled in the Delhi treasury, their sources (many outside India) and their prices in contemporary coin.

    The Mughal workshops also excelled in carving hardstones. Rock crystal, carved into cups and ornaments, had a long history in India; under Jahāngīr and Shāh Jahān jade (nephrite, imported from Central Asia) was carved into wine cups, dagger hilts and boxes of great delicacy, often inlaid with gold and gems—the wine cup of Shāh Jahān in the Victoria and Albert Museum is the best-known example. Hardstone inlay (pietra dura, Persian parchīnkārī) decorated imperial buildings such as the Tāj Mahal.

    The Mughal emperors were among the greatest gem collectors in history. Their treasuries held diamonds from the Deccan, spinels from Badakhshan (often inscribed with the names of successive owners), Burmese rubies, Colombian emeralds and Gulf pearls; the court employed lapidaries who carved and engraved stones and goldsmiths who set them in the kundan technique, using highly refined gold foil. Much of this treasure was carried away by Nādir Shāh after his sack of Delhi in 1739.

    10.11 Minerals in the Rasa Texts¶

    The rasa texts’ classes of minerals were introduced in Chapter 1. Three minerals deserve brief further mention.

    Śilājatu (shilajit)
    A dark, tarry exudate found on rocks in the Himalaya and other mountains, formed from decomposed plant matter. The rasa texts class it among the mahārasas and praise it as a rejuvenative. It contains humic and fulvic substances and minerals. Commercial shilajit products have been found to contain heavy metals, and raw or poorly processed material may be contaminated.
    Gandhaka (sulphur)
    Second only to mercury in rasaśāstra, as the “female” partner of mercury in the formation of mercuric sulphide. India has few deposits of native sulphur, and much of the sulphur used must have been imported.
    Abhraka (mica)
    Mica, abundant in Indian pegmatites (Bihar and Jharkhand later became the world’s leading producers), is classed by the texts into varieties by colour and by its behaviour in fire. Black mica (biotite) is preferred for medicine. Mica dust is a respiratory hazard to miners.

    10.12 Gems in Medicine and Astrology¶

    The rasa and later Āyurvedic texts prescribe preparations of pearl, coral, shell and gems: fine pastes ground with rose water or other liquids (piṣṭi) and calcined powders (bhasma). Pearl, coral and shell preparations consist essentially of calcium carbonate, which is an antacid; other claimed effects, for example on the heart, have not been established. Calcined diamond and ruby preparations are prescribed by the texts as rejuvenatives; they consist of carbon residues or aluminium oxide together with whatever other materials were used in processing.

    The wearing of gems to counteract the influence of planets (ratna remedies in jyotiṣa) is a widespread practice in India. It is a matter of belief; there is no evidence that gems worn on the body have physical effects on health or fortune. Claims that gems “absorb and transmit planetary vibrations” are pseudo-scientific.

    Health and Safety Note

    Gem and mineral preparations are often processed with mercury, sulphur or arsenic compounds. No doses or methods are given, and no therapeutic claim here is established; see the full note in Section 1.1.

    10.13 Minerals and the State in the Arthaśāstra¶

    The Arthaśāstra’s treatment of gems belongs to its larger concern with state revenue. The superintendent of the treasury was to receive gems, pearls and diamonds only after examination by experts, and the text lists the sources (rivers, mines, seas and named regions), types and qualities of each kind, with an emphasis on detecting inferior or false stones (Olivelle 2013). The superintendent of mines controlled the extraction of metal ores and gems, and the state claimed a share of all mineral production. These chapters are among the earliest systematic descriptions of gems in any literature, alongside the Greek and Roman lapidary tradition of Theophrastus and Pliny.

    What Is Secure and What Is Debated

    Secure: India was the main source of diamonds until the 1720s, from the alluvial mines of the Krishna valley; many gems prized in India came from Burma, Sri Lanka, Badakhshan and Colombia; the gem texts are practical manuals mixed with myth and social ideology; and the bead industry runs from the Harappan cities to Khambhat. Debated: the dates of most Sanskrit lapidaries; the identification of terms such as puṣparāga and vaidūrya; and the early history of famous stones such as the Koh-i-Noor.
