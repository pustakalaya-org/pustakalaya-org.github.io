    Chapter 13: Kṣāra and Lavaṇa: Salts, Alkalis, Acids and Sugar¶

    13.1 The Reagents of Pre-Modern Chemistry¶

    Metals and gems attract the attention of historians because they survive and because they are beautiful. The everyday work of pre-modern chemistry, however, was done with salts. Washermen scoured cloth with alkaline earth, dyers made their indigo vats alkaline and fixed their reds with alum, glass-makers fluxed sand with soda, goldsmiths cleaned and joined metal with borax and sal ammoniac, tinsmiths coated copper pots with the help of sal ammoniac, surgeons cauterised with caustic alkali, refiners parted gold from silver with common salt, and the makers of gunpowder depended on saltpetre. The rasa texts, too, are full of salts: their lists of reagents include the “five salts”, the “three alkalis”, alum, the vitriols, borax and sal ammoniac, and many of their operations on mercury and metals are carried out in the presence of one or more of them.

    Health and Safety Note

    This chapter describes historical methods in outline only, and gives no quantities or procedures. Caustic alkalis cause severe chemical burns, especially to the eyes; the vitriols, particularly copper sulphate, are poisonous if swallowed; mineral acids are corrosive and give off toxic fumes; and saltpetre mixtures are fire and explosion hazards. Nothing here should be attempted outside a properly equipped and supervised laboratory. Medical uses of kṣāra and of mineral salts are described as history and are not recommendations (see also Section 1.1).

    This chapter describes these substances: what they were, how they were obtained or made, how they were named and classified, and what they were used for. Several of them have been mentioned in earlier chapters, in connection with medicine (Chapter 1), the mint (Chapter 8), dyeing (Chapter 11) and glass (Chapter 12); here they are treated together, as a chemical industry in their own right. The social and fiscal history of salt, which in India is a large subject, belongs to Volume 19, Vāṇijya and Samudra, and gunpowder as a weapon to Volume 20, Yuddha. This chapter is concerned with the chemistry.

    Pre-modern writers had no concept of a “salt” as the product of an acid and a base. Sanskrit distinguishes lavaṇa, a salty substance (above all common salt), from kṣāra, a caustic or alkaline one, and names many other substances individually. Arabic and Persian likewise distinguish milḥ (salt) from qily, the alkaline ash of certain plants, from which, through medieval Latin, comes the English word “alkali”. Table 13.1 lists the principal substances discussed in this chapter, with their usual modern identifications. As with the minerals of the rasa texts (Table 1.2), the same name can denote different substances in different texts and regions, and identifications based on modern usage may not hold for early texts.

    13.2 Alkalis from Plants¶

    13.2.1 Kṣāra in Surgery¶

    The oldest detailed account of a manufactured alkali in Indian literature is the chapter on caustic alkali (kṣāra) in the first book of the Suśrutasaṃhitā (Sūtrasthāna 11). Its medical use—the grading of the alkali as mild, medium or strong, its external and internal application, and the “alkaline thread” (kṣārasūtra) developed from Suśruta’s brief mention of a thread smeared with alkali—is described in Volume 13, Āyurveda (Trikamji Acharya and Acharya 1938; Bhishagratna 1907–1916). What concerns the historian of chemistry is the logic of the process. The ashes of selected plants were steeped in water, the liquor was strained, and the clear lye was boiled down; to make the caustic grade, further mineral materials were added during the boiling. The text describes the signs of correct and incorrect application, and prescribes sour liquids to counteract an alkali burn, which is, in modern terms, neutralisation with weak acids.

    Plant ash consists largely of the carbonates of potassium and calcium, with smaller amounts of other salts; leaching dissolves the potassium carbonate and leaves the insoluble calcium compounds behind. The materials added to sharpen the lye included burnt shell and stone, that is, quicklime. Lime, made by burning limestone, nodular kankar or shells, was also used for mortar and plaster (Section 12.8) and in many craft processes. Lime reacts with a solution of potassium carbonate to precipitate calcium carbonate and leave potassium hydroxide in solution, a much more corrosive base. This “causticising” reaction underlies the making of strong lyes everywhere before the industrial age, including the lyes of European soap-boilers. Suśruta’s text does not explain the reaction, and its other additives (shell, various plant and mineral substances) were chosen on medical rather than chemical grounds; but the procedure it describes would have produced a genuinely caustic alkali, and the text’s warnings about its dangers show that its authors knew it.

    13.2.2 The Three Alkalis¶

    The later rasa literature, for example the Rasaratnasamuccaya, names a group of “three alkalis” (kṣāratraya): yavakṣāra, sarjikākṣāra and borax (ṭaṅkaṇa). Yavakṣāra, “barley alkali”, is the alkali obtained by burning and leaching the dried plants of barley; it is essentially potash, potassium carbonate. Sarjikākṣāra or svarjikā corresponds to the sajjī of the north-western crafts, a crude sodium carbonate (described below). Borax, which is chemically quite different, was included because it behaved in the laboratory and the clinic like the others: it was bitter-saline, it dissolved readily, and it made other substances fuse. The group is a practical classification, based on use and on qualities perceptible to the senses, not a chemical one. The rasa texts also used the ashes of many individual plants, each named after its source, so that “alkali” in these texts denotes a family of related preparations rather than a single substance (Ray 1956; Meulenbeld 1999–2002).

    The word kṣāra was not confined to alkalis in the modern sense. Its root meaning has to do with flowing, melting or corroding, and in the storehouse chapter of the Arthaśāstra (2.15) the heading kṣāra covers the products of sugarcane: thickened juice, jaggery, granular and crystalline sugar (Olivelle 2013; Kangle 1965). The overlap of terms is a reminder that pre-modern vocabularies grouped substances by their behaviour and use rather than by their composition.

    13.2.3 Sajjī¶

    In the arid and saline lands of the Punjab, Sindh, Rajasthan and the Deccan grow shrubs adapted to salty soils, among them species of Haloxylon, Salsola and Suaeda, whose tissues accumulate sodium. When they are burned the ash is rich in sodium carbonate rather than potassium carbonate, as in the Mediterranean “barilla” plants from which European soap- and glass-makers obtained their soda until the Leblanc process replaced them in the early 19th century. Nineteenth-century accounts describe how the plants were cut in the cold season, dried and burned in pits, and how the ash partly fused into a hard, porous, greyish mass, the sajjī of the market, which was broken up and sold by weight (Watt 1889). It was used by washermen and dyers, by glass-makers and bangle-makers, by soap-makers, and in cooking (it is still used in the dough of some crisp breads). Crude sajjī was variable in strength and contained much salt and sulphate as well as carbonate, and users judged it by taste, by its “bite” on the tongue and by its behaviour in use.

    13.3 Natural Soda and Washing¶

    Alkalis were among the most widely used chemicals of pre-modern India. The caustic alkali (kṣāra) of the Suśrutasaṃhitā, made from plant ashes, has been described above. In the crafts two sources of alkali were important. One was plant ash, especially sajjī. The other was the white efflorescence (reh) that forms on poorly drained saline soils in the Ganga plain and contains sodium carbonate and sulphate. Washermen used such alkalis to scour cloth; dyers used them to prepare cotton and to make indigo vats alkaline (Chapter 11); glass-makers used them as the flux that lowered the melting point of sand, and the mineral-soda composition of much South Asian glass may reflect the use of reh-type soda (Section 12.2); and they were the basis of soap-making.

    Reh forms where ground water rich in dissolved salts rises by capillary action through the soil and evaporates at the surface, leaving the salts behind as a white crust after the rains. The lands affected, known in northern India as ūsar, are barren or nearly so, and their extent grew in the 19th century where canal irrigation raised the water table without adequate drainage—a problem that colonial engineers and soil scientists studied closely. The composition of the crust varies from place to place: in some it is mainly common salt and sodium sulphate, in others it contains much sodium carbonate and bicarbonate, and only the latter is useful as an alkali. Collectors therefore chose their ground by experience, and the Sanskrit name for salt from saline earth, audbhida (“sprung from the ground”), shows that the medical authors were aware of this source (Chapter 1). In the Deccan, 19th-century accounts describe the collection of natural soda from the shores of the saline crater lake of Lonar, in Buldhana district of Maharashtra.

    Washing in India did not depend on alkali alone. Soapnut (rīṭhā, the fruit of Sapindus species) and the pods of śikākāī (Acacia concinna) contain saponins, plant compounds that foam in water and act as mild detergents, and they were used for washing hair, fine textiles and jewellery. Soap in the modern sense, made by boiling oil or fat with a caustic lye so that the fat is split into glycerol and the salts of fatty acids, carries an Arabic name in Hindi and Urdu (sābun). Washermen’s practice combined these materials: soaking in alkaline solutions, beating, boiling and bleaching in the sun.

    13.4 Common Salt¶

    Salt was produced in South Asia in several ways: by mining rock salt in the Salt Range of the Punjab (the source of saindhava, “salt from Sindh”), by evaporating the brine of inland lakes such as Sambhar in Rajasthan, by evaporating sea water in coastal pans in Gujarat, the Konkan, the Coromandel coast and Bengal, and by leaching saline earths. The Arthaśāstra places salt under a superintendent and taxes it (Olivelle 2013). Salt remained a major source of state revenue under later rulers and under the British, whose salt monopoly and tax became the target of Gandhi’s Salt March to Dandi in 1930. The medical compendia’s classification of salts (Chapter 1) reflects this variety of sources. The organisation of salt-making under the East India Company, the salt-boilers of the Bengal delta, the inland customs line and the Salt Act are described in Volume 19, Vāṇijya and Samudra.

    13.4.1 Rock, Lake and Sea¶

    The three main sources differ in their chemistry. Rock salt is halite, sodium chloride deposited by the evaporation of ancient seas and later buried. That of the Salt Range is relatively pure, and its pink and red varieties owe their colour to small amounts of iron oxide; the rock salt of Mandi in the western Himalaya, worked at Drang and Guma, is darker and contains more clay. Rock salt needs no refining, which made it a premium product, and the medical texts rank saindhava as the best salt.

    Lake salt is obtained by evaporating the brine of shallow, closed lakes in which salt brought down by rivers and wind has accumulated. Sambhar, whose name derives from Śākambharī, the goddess and the early medieval capital of the Cāhamānas, is the largest; Didwana and Pachpadra in Rajasthan are smaller sources. The brine is led into shallow pans and left to evaporate in the sun, and the crystals are raked up.

    Sea salt is made by evaporating sea water, which contains about 3.5 per cent dissolved salts, of which roughly three-quarters by weight is sodium chloride. On the coasts of Gujarat and the south, sea water or the sub-surface brine of salt flats (as in the Little Rann of Kutch) is led through a series of shallow pans and evaporated by the sun; in the humid Bengal delta, where solar evaporation was too slow, brine was concentrated by boiling.

    13.4.2 The Chemistry of the Salt Pan¶

    Solar salt-making depends on a sequence of precipitations that salt-makers learned to control by experience. As sea water evaporates, the least soluble salts come out first: calcium carbonate when the brine has been concentrated about twofold, then gypsum (calcium sulphate) at about four- or fivefold concentration. Sodium chloride begins to crystallise only when the brine has been reduced to about a tenth of its original volume. If evaporation goes further, the remaining liquor—the “bittern”—deposits the sulphates and chlorides of magnesium and potassium, which give salt a bitter taste and make it absorb moisture. Good salt was therefore made by moving the brine from pan to pan, so that the gypsum was left behind in the first pans, and by drawing off the bittern before it could contaminate the crop. Salt-workers judged the stages by the look and feel of the brine; in the 19th century official salt works introduced the hydrometer to measure its density.

    13.4.3 Manufactured Salts¶

    Some of the salts of the medical texts were manufactured from common salt. The best known in modern practice is the “black salt” (kālā namak) of northern India, a dark, pinkish-grey salt with a strong smell of sulphur that is used in food and in household medicine. It is made by heating salt strongly with plant materials, such as the fruits of the myrobalan, in a closed furnace. Under these reducing conditions the sulphates present in the salt are converted into sulphides, which account for its smell, and iron compounds give it its colour. Modern authors have tried to identify the sauvarcala and viḍa of the medical compendia with salts of this kind; as noted in Chapter 1, the identifications are plausible but uncertain, and the early texts do not describe the processes.

    13.4.4 Salt as a Reagent¶

    In the crafts and the laboratory, common salt was a reagent as well as a foodstuff. Refiners used it in the cementation process for separating silver from gold, in which gold is heated with salt and brick-dust and the silver is drawn off as its chloride (Chapter 8). The rasa texts add salts to many of their grinding and heating operations on mercury and metals. Their purposes were stated in the texts’ own terms, but chlorides do attack metals and their oxides when heated, forming chlorides of which some are volatile, and it is likely that the alchemists’ observations of metals “eaten” or “dissolved” in the presence of salts rested on such reactions. Mercury and its compounds react readily with chlorides; one of the mercurial preparations of the later pharmacopoeias, rasakarpūra, is usually identified as a mercury chloride (Chapter 14).

    13.5 Saltpetre¶

    Saltpetre (potassium nitrate; Hindi shorā) forms naturally in the soils of the Ganga plain, especially around villages, where organic wastes are broken down by bacteria. It was extracted by specialist communities—in Bihar notably the Nonia—who scraped the nitrous earth, leached it with water, boiled down the liquor and crystallised the crude salt, which was then refined by re-dissolving and recrystallising. The Āʾīn-i Akbarī gives the same outline: the saline earth is put in a perforated vessel, water is poured over it, and the liquid that drips through is boiled, cleaned and allowed to crystallise (Āʾīn 1.22) (Abuʾl-Faẓl ʿAllāmī 1873). The process is a straightforward but skilled application of differences in solubility.

    13.5.1 How Nitre Forms¶

    The nitrogen in saltpetre comes from the urine, dung and other organic refuse that accumulate around human and animal habitation. Soil bacteria convert the ammonia released by decay first into nitrite and then into nitrate, and in a hot climate with a long dry season the nitrates, which are very soluble, are drawn up to the surface with the evaporating soil water and deposited as a whitish efflorescence. The cattle-pens, refuse heaps and old village mounds of Bihar, with their warm, alternately damp and dry soils, were ideal for this process, and the nitrate formed there was often already largely potassium nitrate, because the soils and wood ash supplied potassium. Elsewhere, and in the artificial “nitre beds” that European states built in the 17th and 18th centuries in an attempt to free themselves from imported saltpetre, much of the nitrate formed as calcium nitrate, which absorbs moisture from the air and is useless for gunpowder. It had to be converted by adding wood ash: the potassium carbonate of the ash reacts with calcium nitrate to precipitate calcium carbonate and leave potassium nitrate in solution. Indian saltpetre-makers also used wood ash in their leaching vats where the earth required it.

    13.5.2 Refining¶

    The crude product of the village workings (Hindi kaccā, “raw”) contained common salt, sulphates and other impurities. Refining exploited the fact that the solubility of potassium nitrate rises steeply with temperature, whereas that of common salt hardly changes: when a hot, concentrated solution is allowed to cool, saltpetre crystallises out in long prisms and most of the salt stays in the liquor. Repeated recrystallisation gave the clean, crystalline grade known in the trade as kalmī shorā. The European companies bought both crude and refined saltpetre, and refined part of their purchases themselves at Patna and elsewhere (Prakash 1985).

    13.5.3 Uses¶

    Saltpetre had several uses. The Āʾīn-i Akbarī describes its use at the Mughal court for cooling water: dissolving saltpetre in water absorbs heat, and a flask of drinking water swirled in such a solution is chilled (Abuʾl-Faẓl ʿAllāmī 1873). Its most important use, however, was in gunpowder, a mixture of saltpetre, sulphur and charcoal. Gunpowder was invented in China, and knowledge of it reached India, probably through the Mongols and the Islamic world, by the 13th or 14th century; firearms were in use in several Indian states by the later 15th century, and Bābur’s artillery at Pānīpat in 1526 was decisive. Claims that Vedic or epic texts describe gunpowder weapons are not supported by the evidence (Khan 2004). The history of firearms and rockets in India is treated in Volume 20, Yuddha.

    Chemically, gunpowder is a solid mixture that carries its own oxidant. When it is ignited, the nitrate releases oxygen to burn the charcoal, and the reaction produces a large volume of hot gas—chiefly carbon dioxide and nitrogen—in a very short time, together with solid potassium salts that make up the dense white smoke. Sulphur lowers the temperature at which the mixture ignites and speeds the reaction. The performance of powder depended on the purity of the saltpetre (salt and other deliquescent impurities made it damp and sluggish), on the kind of charcoal, and on how intimately the ingredients were ground and mixed; these, rather than any secret formula, distinguished good powder from bad. Saltpetre was also the essential ingredient of the fireworks (Persian ātishbāzī) that became a feature of court festivals and weddings in the Sultanate and Mughal periods (Khan 2004).

    13.5.4 Bihar and the World Market¶

    From the 17th century Bihar became one of the world’s main sources of saltpetre. The Dutch and English East India Companies bought it in large quantities at Patna and shipped it to Europe, where it supplied the gunpowder of European armies and navies; its weight made it useful as ballast as well as cargo (Prakash 1985; Frey 2009). Control of the Bihar saltpetre trade was one of the strategic advantages that Britain gained from its conquest of eastern India in the 18th century. The trade is followed in Volume 19, Vāṇijya and Samudra.

    Indian saltpetre also had a place in the history of European chemistry. Nitre was one of the substances most intensively studied by 17th- and 18th-century chemists, because it was strategically important and because its behaviour—it made charcoal burn fiercely, and could be decomposed and “reintegrated”—seemed to hold a clue to combustion and to the nature of air. Robert Boyle’s essay on nitre (1661) and John Mayow’s theory of a “nitro-aerial spirit” (1674) belong to this line of enquiry, which led in the following century to the recognition of oxygen (Principe 2013). In the 1770s Antoine Lavoisier, as a commissioner of the French gunpowder administration, reorganised the production of saltpetre in France in order to reduce the country’s dependence on imports, at a time when Britain controlled the richest source, in Bihar. The Nonia’s nitre was thus part of the material background against which European chemists worked out the chemistry of combustion.

    The decline of Indian saltpetre was also a chemical story. From the mid-19th century the vast deposits of sodium nitrate in the Atacama desert of Chile were exploited, at first for fertiliser; sodium nitrate itself absorbs moisture too readily for good gunpowder, but it could be converted into potassium nitrate by reaction with potassium chloride from the newly opened German potash mines, and this “conversion saltpetre” undercut the Indian product. In the early 20th century the Haber–Bosch synthesis of ammonia from atmospheric nitrogen, followed by the oxidation of ammonia to nitric acid, made fixed nitrogen an industrial product and ended the strategic importance of natural nitre everywhere.

    13.6 Sugar: Crystallisation from Solution¶

    The refining of saltpetre depended on crystallisation from solution, and so did the oldest crystalline product of Indian chemistry, sugar—which the Arthaśāstra, as noted above, files under kṣāra. Sugarcane was domesticated in New Guinea, but it was in South Asia that the technique of making solid sugar was developed. Sanskrit texts distinguish several sugar products: guḍa (unrefined, jaggery-like sugar), khaṇḍa (sugar in pieces), śarkarā (granular sugar) and others; the Arthaśāstra lists several of them among the goods in the state storehouse (Olivelle 2013). The words travelled with the product: Sanskrit śarkarā lies behind Persian shakar, Arabic sukkar and English “sugar”, and khaṇḍa behind “candy”.

    Making sugar is a chemical process: the juice must be clarified, concentrated by boiling to the right point and allowed to crystallise, and the crystals separated from the molasses. Christian Daniels’s study of sugar technology in Asia shows that the Chinese learned methods of making crystalline sugar from India: in the 7th century the Tang emperor Taizong sent envoys to Magadha in eastern India to learn the technique (Daniels 1996). Indian sugar was exported to western Asia, and sugar-making spread from India and Iran across the Islamic world to the Mediterranean, and thence to the Atlantic plantations whose history is bound up with slavery.

    The stages of the process, and the crop itself, are described in Volume 15, Kṛṣi and Jīva Vijñāna. Their chemistry can be stated briefly. Cane juice contains, besides sucrose, acids, proteins, gums and colouring matter. Boiling with an alkaline clarifier—lime or plant ash—neutralises the acids and makes much of the impurity coagulate as a scum, which was skimmed off; vegetable mucilages and milk were added in some regions to help the scum gather. Neutralisation mattered for another reason: in hot, acidic juice sucrose is slowly split into glucose and fructose (“inversion”), which do not crystallise readily, so that juice boiled too long or left too acid yields more molasses and less sugar. Separating the crystals from the syrup by draining, and washing them by letting water percolate slowly through the crystal mass, as Indian refiners did by covering their draining pots with moist water-weeds, gave progressively whiter grades (Daniels 1996).

    13.7 Alum and the Vitriols¶

    13.7.1 Alum¶

    Alum (Sanskrit kāṅkṣī, sphaṭikā or sphaṭikārī; Hindi phiṭkarī; Arabic shabb) is a double sulphate of aluminium and potassium (or ammonium) that crystallises in clear, colourless octahedra. Its Sanskrit name sphaṭikā, “crystal-like”, invites confusion with sphaṭika, rock crystal (Chapter 10), and translators have sometimes mistaken one for the other. In the rasa texts alum is an uparasa (Table 1.2).

    Alum occurs naturally only in small quantities, as an efflorescence on certain rocks. Most alum was manufactured from alum shales, clays rich in finely divided pyrites (iron sulphide). When such shale is roasted, or simply left to weather in heaps for months, the pyrites oxidise and the sulphuric acid formed attacks the clay minerals, producing aluminium sulphate together with iron sulphate. The soluble salts are leached out with water and the liquor is concentrated; an alkali—potash from wood ash or, in some places, ammonia from stale urine—is then added, and alum crystallises out on cooling, leaving the more soluble iron salts in the mother liquor. The same method was used across the Old World, from China to the alum works of papal Italy and 17th-century England. In 19th-century India alum was made in this way at Kalabagh on the Indus, from the pyritic shales of the Salt Range, and in Kutch, and alum was also imported (Watt 1889).

    Alum had many uses, most of which depend on the aluminium ion. As a mordant it fixed the red of madder, chay and lac (Chapter 11). It was used in tanning and tawing leather. Dissolved in a little water and stirred into turbid river water, it causes the fine suspended clay to flocculate and settle—a use still common in Indian households and the principle of modern water treatment with aluminium salts. Its astringency made it a styptic, used by barbers after shaving and in folk medicine, and the medical texts used it externally.

    13.7.2 The Vitriols¶

    The “vitriols” of pre-modern chemistry were crystalline metal sulphates. Green vitriol (kāsīsa; Hindi hīrā kasīs), iron(II) sulphate, forms naturally where pyrites weather in moist air, and was also obtained as a by-product of alum-making. With the tannins of myrobalan, pomegranate rind or oak galls it gives a deep black, the basis of black dyes and of the iron-gall inks of the Islamic and later the Indian manuscript traditions; the black outlines of kalamkari depend on the same chemistry (Chapter 11). Iron-gall ink is permanent, but its acidity and the iron it contains slowly degrade paper, and many Indo-Persian manuscripts show the characteristic browning and cracking along the lines of writing. Kāsīsa already appears in the medical compendia among external applications, and the rasa texts class it as an uparasa.

    Blue vitriol (tuttha, also called sasyaka in the rasa texts; Hindi nīlā thothā), copper(II) sulphate, forms in the oxidised zones of copper deposits and in the water draining from copper mines, from which it can be crystallised. It was used as a caustic and astringent and, in the rasa texts, as a source of copper and as a mahārasa. Both vitriols are poisonous if swallowed; copper sulphate in particular has caused many deaths by accidental and deliberate ingestion.

    13.8 Sal Ammoniac¶

    Sal ammoniac, ammonium chloride, has the unusual property of passing directly from solid to vapour when heated and condensing again as a white crust on a cool surface. When hot, it attacks the oxide films on metals, converting them to chlorides that melt or volatilise and leaving a clean metal surface. For this reason it is the traditional flux of the tinsmith. In India the tinning (qalaʿī) of copper and brass cooking pots, to prevent the formation of poisonous copper salts in sour food, has been carried out for centuries by itinerant qalaʿīgars, who heat the pot, rub its inner surface with sal ammoniac (Hindi nausādar) and then wipe molten tin over it; the sharp, white fumes of the salt are characteristic of the work. Goldsmiths and braziers used it in soldering.

    Sal ammoniac was not known as a named substance in Greek alchemy, but it was one of the principal reagents of Arabic alchemy from the late 9th century, classed with mercury, sulphur and the arsenic sulphides among the “spirits” because it sublimes. Arab geographers of the 10th century describe its collection from vents in the mountains of Sogdiana, where underground coal fires released ammonium chloride that crystallised at the mouths of the fissures; Central Asian sal ammoniac was traded to the Islamic world and to China (Laufer 1919; Principe 2013). The Arabic nūshādir comes from Persian nawshādur, and the Sanskrit navasāra or navasādara, which appears in the rasa texts but not in the classical medical compendia, is generally regarded as a loan from the Persian (Chapter 14).

    Sal ammoniac could also be made. Wherever dung or other nitrogenous fuel was burned in a confined space—in brick kilns, in the hearths of houses fuelled with dung-cakes—ammonium chloride sublimed from the fire and collected in the soot. One of its Sanskrit synonyms, cūlikālavaṇa, “hearth salt”, points to this source. In the 19th century sal ammoniac was manufactured in northern India, especially in the districts around Delhi and Karnal, by subliming it from the deposits scraped from brick kilns that had been fired with refuse and dung, and it was sold widely to tinsmiths, dyers and druggists (Watt 1889).

    13.9 Borax¶

    Borax (Sanskrit ṭaṅkaṇa; Hindi suhāgā; Persian būra, whence Arabic būraq and English “borax”), sodium borate, was the goldsmith’s and metal-refiner’s flux. When heated it loses its water of crystallisation, swells, and then melts to a clear glass that dissolves metal oxides; a little borax on a joint keeps the metal clean so that solder or molten gold can flow and bond. The workmen of the Mughal mint used it, with an alkaline salt, to smelt silver-bearing ashes (Section 8.13), and the rasa texts list it among the materials of the alchemical laboratory and among the “three alkalis”. It was also used in glazes and enamels, to which it gives a lower melting point and a brilliant surface, and in medicine.

    India had almost no borax of its own. The crude salt came from the salt lakes and hot springs of the Tibetan plateau, where it crystallises from evaporating waters, and from the hot springs of the Puga valley in Ladakh. It was carried across the Himalaya on the backs of sheep and goats, each animal bearing a small load in a double bag, to markets in Kumaon, Bushahr and Nepal, and from there to the plains, where it was cleaned and recrystallised. Much was re-exported. In Europe crude borax was known as “tincal”, a word that came through Persian (tinkār) from Sanskrit ṭaṅkaṇa, and was refined in Venice and later in the Netherlands (Watt 1889, 1908). In the 19th century Tibetan borax was displaced by boric acid from the volcanic springs of Tuscany and, after 1870, by the borate deposits of California and Nevada.

    13.10 Lime¶

    Lime was the cheapest and most widely produced of all pre-modern chemicals. It is made by burning a form of calcium carbonate—limestone, shell or coral—at a red heat, which drives off carbon dioxide and leaves quicklime (calcium oxide). Quicklime reacts violently with water, swelling, cracking and giving off much heat, to form slaked lime (calcium hydroxide); left exposed to the air, slaked lime slowly takes up carbon dioxide and returns to calcium carbonate. This cycle of burning, slaking and recarbonation is the chemistry of lime mortar and plaster (Section 12.8), and the empirical control of each stage—how hard to burn the stone, how long to slake and age the lime, how much sand to add—was the core of the lime-burner’s and mason’s craft.

    The raw materials varied by region. Where stone was available, as in the Vindhyas, the Deccan and the Himalayan foothills, limestone was burned in kilns; in the stoneless alluvium of the Ganga plain the usual source was kankar, the hard calcareous nodules that form in the subsoil and were dug up, broken and burned in clamps; on the coasts lime was burned from sea-shells, especially in Bengal, Kerala and the Konkan, and shell lime was valued for fine plaster. The Sanskrit words sudhā and cūrṇa (Hindi cūnā) cover both the material and the plaster made from it, and since sudhā also means nectar, poets could play on the double sense when describing palaces gleaming with white plaster.

    Lime entered many other processes described in this volume. It sharpened alkaline lyes (Section 13.2), clarified cane juice in sugar-boiling (Section 13.6), and was an ingredient of some indigo vats (Chapter 11). Tanners soaked hides in lime-water for days to loosen the hair and swell the skin before tanning. Slaked lime, as a paste, is an essential ingredient of the betel quid (tāmbūla), whose history and health risks are discussed in Volume 15. The calcined shell, pearl, coral and cowrie preparations of the rasa texts (Chapters 9 and 10) are, chemically, forms of calcium carbonate or calcium oxide. Quicklime is itself hazardous: it burns the skin and can blind, and the heat of slaking has started fires in boats and storehouses where lime was carried or kept damp.

    13.11 Acids¶

    13.11.1 Sour Liquids¶

    Pre-modern chemistry, in India as elsewhere, had no strong acids until late in its history. The acids available were those of plants and fermentation: the tartaric acid of tamarind, the citric acid of lemons and limes (jambīra, nimbū), the acids of the āmalakī fruit, and the lactic and acetic acids of sour rice gruel (kāñjika, Hindi kāñjī) and of vinegar (Persian sirka). These weak acids dissolve metal oxides and carbonates, which is why tamarind has always been used in Indian kitchens to clean tarnished brass and copper, and why goldsmiths boiled finished jewellery in sour solutions to remove the oxides left by heating. The rasa texts group sour substances into a “sour class” (amlavarga) and use them constantly in their “purifications”; one effect of many such operations would have been to remove surface oxides and dissolve basic impurities. Sour liquids also served to neutralise alkalis, as in Suśruta’s treatment of alkali burns.

    13.11.2 “Dissolving Liquids”¶

    Mineral acids—sulphuric, nitric and hydrochloric acids—are made by distilling or heating salts: nitric acid, for example, by heating saltpetre with a vitriol or with alum, and hydrochloric acid by heating common salt with such materials. In Europe the first clear descriptions of such preparations appear in Latin alchemical texts of about 1300, and “aqua fortis” (nitric acid) became the standard means of parting gold from silver in the following centuries (Principe 2013). Whether, and when, Indian chemists prepared mineral acids has been much discussed. P. C. Ray drew attention to preparations called drāvaka (“solvent” or “liquefier”), among them a śaṅkhadrāvaka (“shell-dissolver”), which late Sanskrit works describe as made by distilling a mixture of salts such as alum, saltpetre, sal ammoniac and common salt, and which was said to dissolve shells and metals (Ray 1956). If the descriptions are taken at face value, such a distillate would have contained nitric and hydrochloric acids. But the texts in which these preparations appear are late, mostly of the 16th century or after, and their dates and possible debt to Persian or European practice are uncertain; they do not show that mineral acids were known in the classical rasa literature.

    The evidence of the Mughal mint points the same way. Abuʾl Faẓl’s account of refining relies on cementation with salt, cupellation and parting with sulphur, not on acids, and the substance rāsī that Blochmann translated as “aqua fortis” cannot be identified with confidence (Section 8.13). By the 19th century nitric acid, known in Hindi and Urdu as tezāb (Persian tīzāb, “sharp water”), was in common use among Indian goldsmiths and refiners for testing and parting gold, and it was by then largely an imported or industrially made chemical. Its arrival marks the boundary between the older chemistry of salts and the new chemistry of the laboratory. It changed even the oldest of assays. The touchstone test described in the Arthaśāstra and the Āʾīn-i Akbarī (Chapters 1 and 8) judged fineness by comparing the colour of a streak of the sample with streaks of touch-needles of known fineness. With acid, the assayer could also wet the streaks: base metals and low-grade gold dissolve or discolour, while fine gold remains, so that the test no longer depended on the eye alone. The “acid test” on the touchstone is still the everyday assay of the Indian jeweller’s shop.

    13.12 Workers of Salt and Nitre¶

    The salts described in this chapter were produced by some of the poorest workers in India. The Nonia (also Luniya or Noniyā, from non or lon, “salt”) of Bihar and eastern Uttar Pradesh were the saltpetre-makers of the Ganga plain; they scraped nitrous earth from village sites under licence from landholders and, in the colonial period, worked on advances from the Company’s agents and later from licensed refiners. Their hereditary occupation placed them low in the caste order, and in the 20th century, as the saltpetre industry declined, many turned to earthwork and labouring. The salt-boilers (malaṅgī) of the Bengal delta and the salt-pan workers (Agariya) of the Little Rann of Kutch, whose working conditions are discussed in Volume 19, worked in great heat and glare, and the pan-workers of the Rann suffered from the effects of brine on the skin and of reflected sunlight on the eyes. The sajjī-burners of the north-west, the reh-collectors of the Ganga plain and the miners and sorters of the Himalayan borax trade left almost no written trace.

    As elsewhere in this volume, the knowledge involved—when to scrape the earth, how strong to make the lye, when to move the brine, how to judge the purity of a salt by taste and crystal—was held by these workers and passed on within families. It appears in the written record mainly when officials wished to tax or monopolise its products.

    What Is Secure and What Is Debated

    Secure: the everyday reagents of pre-modern Indian chemistry were salts—plant-ash alkalis, natural soda, common salt, saltpetre, alum, the vitriols, sal ammoniac, borax and lime—obtained by leaching, evaporation, crystallisation and sublimation; Suśruta’s caustic alkali was a lime-sharpened lye; Bihar saltpetre was a world source for two centuries; and India developed the making of crystalline sugar. Debated: the identity of several salts of the medical texts; whether sal ammoniac and its name came into Sanskrit from Persian; and when, and from where, mineral acids such as the drāvakas of the late texts were first prepared in India.
