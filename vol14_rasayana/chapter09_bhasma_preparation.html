    Chapter 9: Bhasma: Calcined Metals in History and Toxicology¶

    9.1 The Idea of a Bhasma¶

    Bhasma means “ash”. In the rasa literature and in later Āyurveda it denotes a powder obtained from a metal, mineral, gem or shell by a sequence of treatments ending in repeated calcination, after which—according to the texts—the substance has lost its metallic nature and become fit to be taken as a medicine. The texts say that metals in their natural state are poisonous and cause disease, but that “killed” (mṛta, mārita) metals become wholesome and, in the case of gold, mica and iron, powerful rejuvenatives (Ray 1956; Dash 1986).

    Health and Safety Note

    This chapter gives no doses, proportions, durations, vehicles or step-by-step methods. Many bhasmas are made from lead, mercury, arsenic compounds or copper. Do not prepare or take such products; anyone who has taken them and has symptoms should see a doctor, who can test for heavy-metal poisoning. The evidence is reviewed in Section 9.5; see also the full note in Section 1.1.

    Bhasmas are still manufactured on a large scale in India and sold in India and abroad. This chapter treats them in two ways: as a historical tradition of chemical practice, described in the texts; and as a contemporary product whose safety has been examined by modern toxicology.

    9.2 The Stages Described in the Texts¶

    The rasa texts and the later compendia describe the making of a bhasma in three broad stages: a preliminary “purification” (śodhana), a “killing” (māraṇa) by repeated grinding and heating in sealed vessels, and an examination (parīkṣā) of the product, with the killing repeated until the tests are met. The details vary for each substance and from text to text.

    9.2.1 Śodhana¶

    “Purification” usually begins with a general treatment in which thin pieces of metal are heated and quenched in a series of liquids—oils, sour gruel, buttermilk, cow’s urine, plant decoctions—followed by treatments specific to each substance. In physical terms, repeated heating and quenching oxidises the metal surface and makes it brittle, so that it can be broken up and ground; some soluble or volatile impurities may also be removed. The texts, however, explain śodhana in terms of removing the “defects” of the substance, and they apply similar procedures to poisons such as aconite and to arsenic sulphides, for which no comparable physical rationale exists.

    9.2.2 Māraṇa¶

    “Killing” consists, in outline, of grinding the substance with plant juices or with other minerals (often sulphur, mercury or arsenic sulphides) and heating the product in sealed earthen vessels in a puṭa, a cycle that the texts repeat many times. In chemical terms the heating converts most metals to oxides, or, when sulphur is added, to sulphides, and the grinding breaks up the product into a fine powder. Gold behaves differently: it does not oxidise, and modern analyses of gold bhasma find fine particles of metallic gold.

    9.2.3 Traditional Tests¶

    The texts give a set of tests that a properly “killed” bhasma must pass (Table 9.1).

    Table 9.1. Traditional Tests for Bhasmas
    Test What the texts require What it can and cannot show
    rekhāpūrṇatā The powder fills the lines of the fingers Fineness of the powder
    vāritara A pinch floats on still water Fineness (surface tension supports fine, light particles)
    niścandratā No metallic lustre or glitter remains Absence of visible metal or unaltered mica
    apunarbhava, niruttha Heated with reducing materials, the powder does not revert to metal Absence of easily reducible metal; not absence of the element
    Colour, taste, texture Specified for each bhasma Consistency with the expected product

    These tests are a genuine attempt at quality control, and some correspond to measurable properties such as particle fineness. None of them, however, tests for toxicity. A powder can pass every traditional test and still consist largely of lead oxide or arsenic sulphide; the tests show that a metal has been converted into a compound, not that the compound is safe.

    9.3 The Principal Bhasmas and Their Claimed Uses¶

    The following summary describes what the texts claim. It is not a statement that these claims are true.

    Gold (svarṇa bhasma).
    Praised as the supreme rejuvenative, said to strengthen the body, heart and mind and to counter poisons. Analyses find fine particles of metallic gold, sometimes with other metals introduced during processing.
    Iron (loha or lauha bhasma).
    Prescribed for pallor (pāṇḍu), enlargement of the liver and spleen, and weakness; analyses find mainly iron oxides.
    Copper (tāmra bhasma).
    Prescribed for digestive, liver and skin disorders. The texts themselves warn of its dangers if insufficiently processed.
    Tin and lead (vaṅga and nāga bhasma).
    Prescribed especially for urinary and genital disorders and prameha. Lead preparations are inherently toxic.
    Zinc (yaśada bhasma).
    A later addition, prescribed for eye diseases and prameha.
    Mica (abhraka bhasma).
    One of the most praised rejuvenatives; prepared by especially long processing. Mica is a silicate of aluminium and potassium (with iron and magnesium in dark varieties); the product contains these elements in altered form.
    Mercurial and arsenical preparations.
    Discussed in Chapter 3; arsenic sulphides (orpiment, realgar) and white arsenic are also processed into “calcined” or sublimed products.

    The texts also prescribe the vehicle (anupāna) with which a medicine is taken—honey, ghee, milk, plant juices—and adjust it to the disease and to the patient’s constitution. This is a characteristic feature of Āyurvedic pharmacology; it does not alter the toxicity of a heavy metal.

    9.4 What Modern Analysis Shows¶

    Since the 1990s many studies, mostly in Indian laboratories, have characterised bhasmas with modern methods. An early survey analysed twenty bhasmas for up to eighteen elements by neutron activation and found, among other things, that a sample of the mercurial makaradhvaja was essentially stoichiometric mercuric sulphide (Kumar et al. 2006); a more recent study of a commercial rasasindūra found pure red mercuric sulphide in nanometre-sized particles (Mukhi et al. 2017). Taken together, such studies show that most metal bhasmas consist of oxides of the metal (iron, copper, tin or zinc oxides, for example), or of sulphides when sulphur or mercury has been used in processing, while gold bhasma contains metallic gold. The powders are fine, with primary particles that may be sub-micron in size, usually aggregated into larger clusters. They often contain elements other than the named metal, introduced from the raw material, the processing agents, the crucibles or contamination—including, in a number of studies, lead, mercury and arsenic—and their composition varies between manufacturers and between batches.

    Many of the authors of these studies regard their results as confirming that traditional processing yields a well-defined and, in their view, safer product, and some present the fine particle size as the key to the claimed effects (Pal et al. 2014). The analyses certainly show what the products contain and how consistently they are made. Whether a given product is effective or safe is a separate question, which analysis alone cannot answer.

    9.4.1 The “Nanomedicine” Argument¶

    It has become common to describe bhasmas as “ancient nanomedicine” and to suggest that their fine particle size explains claimed therapeutic effects or reduced toxicity (Pal et al. 2014). This argument should be treated with caution. Fine particle size is a consequence of repeated grinding and calcination; it was not, and could not have been, a goal understood by the authors of the texts. In modern toxicology, smaller particles are often absorbed more readily and can be more, not less, toxic than coarse ones; nanomaterials are subject to particular safety scrutiny for this reason. Finally, describing a product as containing nanoparticles says nothing about whether it works for any disease. The analogy with modern nanomedicine, where particles are designed and tested for specific purposes, is therefore misleading.

    9.5 Safety and Toxicology¶

    9.5.1 The Traditional View¶

    The rasa texts knew that metals could be dangerous. They state that improperly prepared metals cause disease and death, list the signs of poisoning by insufficiently “killed” copper or lead, and prescribe remedies for them. Their answer was more thorough processing: a correctly prepared bhasma, they hold, is safe. Modern defenders of rasaśāstra make the same argument in modern language, distinguishing “properly prepared” bhasmas (assumed to be safe) from improperly prepared or adulterated products (acknowledged to be dangerous).

    9.5.2 What Toxicology Shows¶

    Toxicology does not support the traditional view as a general rule. The toxicity of lead, mercury and arsenic depends on how much of the element is absorbed, and processing can change that only to a limited degree. Lead oxide, the product of “killing” lead, is absorbed from the gut and is a well-known source of lead poisoning; arsenic sulphides are less soluble than white arsenic but are still toxic; mercuric sulphide is less readily absorbed than mercury salts but accumulates with long use (Liu et al. 2008). The relevant evidence comes from three sources:

    1. Product surveys. Saper and colleagues found lead, mercury or arsenic in about a fifth of Ayurvedic products bought in Boston (2004) and online (2008), with rasaśāstra products the most often affected; if taken as recommended, the metal-containing products could lead to intakes above accepted safety limits (Saper et al. 2004, 2008). Similar results have been reported from other countries (Ernst 2002).

    2. Clinical cases. Lead poisoning, sometimes severe, has been repeatedly reported in people taking Ayurvedic medicines, including pregnant women and children (Centers for Disease Control and Prevention 2004; Kales and Saper 2009).

    3. Experimental studies. Animal studies of individual bhasmas, most of them by Indian Āyurvedic research groups, have produced mixed results. A typical study gave rats copper bhasma for twenty-eight days and found no signs of toxicity at doses corresponding to the therapeutic dose, but mild changes in the liver, kidney, heart and thymus at ten times that dose, and less toxicity in the sample given the traditional final “ambrosial” treatment (amṛtīkaraṇa) (Chaudhari et al. 2016). Reviews of the toxicology of mercury–sulphur preparations, including one by an Indian group, weigh the low solubility of mercuric sulphide against the evidence that it is absorbed and accumulates with long use (Kamath et al. 2012; Liu et al. 2008). Short animal studies of this kind are evidence that a product is not acutely poisonous at the doses tested; they cannot by themselves establish safety for long-term human use.

    Defenders of rasaśāstra point to the animal studies and to the texts’ long experience; critics point to the product surveys and the clinical cases. The two bodies of evidence answer different questions—whether a correctly made product is acutely toxic, and whether the products actually sold and taken are safe—and both are needed for a fair judgement.

    Table 9.2 sets the main traditional claims beside this evidence.

    Table 9.2. Traditional Claims and Toxicological Assessment
    Claim Source Assessment
    Śodhana and māraṇa remove the toxicity of metals Rasa texts; modern practitioners Not established; the toxic element remains in the product
    Passing the traditional tests shows a bhasma is safe Rasa texts The tests measure fineness and non-metallic state, not toxicity
    Problems arise only from improper preparation or adulteration Modern defenders Contamination is common, but intentional metal content is itself a hazard
    Fine particle size makes bhasmas safe Modern “nanomedicine” literature Unsupported; fine particles may be absorbed more readily

    9.5.3 Regulation¶

    In India, Ayurvedic, Siddha and Unani drugs are regulated under the Drugs and Cosmetics Act, 1940, and classical formulations may be manufactured according to the official Ayurvedic Formulary and Pharmacopoeia. From the mid-2000s India required testing of exported Ayurvedic products for heavy metals, and many countries restrict or prohibit the import of products containing heavy metals. Regulations do not, however, guarantee the safety of products bought informally, over the internet, or from unlicensed practitioners.

    9.6 Arsenic and Lead in the Rasa Texts¶

    Two groups of substances deserve separate mention because of their toxicity.

    9.6.1 Arsenic¶

    The rasa texts use three arsenic minerals: orpiment (haritāla, arsenic trisulphide), realgar (manaḥśilā, arsenic sulphide) and a white substance (gaurīpāṣāṇa, śaṅkhiyā) usually identified as white arsenic (arsenic trioxide). The texts classify them among the uparasas and sādhāraṇa rasas, recognise that they are poisons, and prescribe “purifications” before use, followed by calcination or sublimation. Arsenic compounds were also widely used in pre-modern medicine elsewhere—in Europe, for instance, as Fowler’s solution until the 20th century—and arsenic trioxide is today used, under strict control, in the treatment of a particular leukaemia. That modern use, however, depends on precise dosing of a pure compound with careful monitoring; it lends no support to the use of traditional arsenical preparations. Chronic arsenic exposure causes skin lesions, nerve damage and cancers, as the mass poisoning from contaminated groundwater in Bengal and Bangladesh has tragically shown.

    9.6.2 Lead¶

    Lead (nāga, sīsa) is “killed” in the rasa texts to make nāga bhasma, prescribed especially for urinary disorders and prameha, and litharge and red lead appear in compound medicines and ointments. Lead is the metal most often implicated in poisoning from Ayurvedic products (Saper et al. 2004; Centers for Disease Control and Prevention 2004; Kales and Saper 2009). Lead poisoning can cause anaemia, abdominal pain, kidney damage, nerve damage and, in children and the unborn, lasting harm to the developing brain. There is no level of lead exposure known to be safe.

    9.7 Rasaśāstra in Modern Āyurveda¶

    Rasaśāstra became part of the curriculum of the Āyurvedic colleges established from the early 20th century (see also Volume 13, Āyurveda), and today “Rasaśāstra and Bhaiṣajya Kalpanā” is a compulsory subject in the Indian degree in Āyurvedic medicine. Its textbooks, largely based on the Rasaratnasamuccaya, the Āyurvedaprakāśa and the Rasataraṅgiṇī, present the classical procedures as standard methods, and pharmaceutical companies manufacture bhasmas and herbo-mineral compounds on an industrial scale.

    Within Āyurveda itself, opinions differ. Some practitioners and researchers regard metallic preparations as the most powerful part of their pharmacy and seek to demonstrate their safety and efficacy by modern methods. Others prefer to avoid them, or reserve them for particular cases, in view of the safety evidence and of the regulations of importing countries (Section 9.5.3). Independent studies continue to find products whose metal content is unacceptable (Saper et al. 2008; Ernst 2002). The debate is not between “tradition” and “modernity” as such, but over what standard of evidence should govern the use of toxic substances as medicines.

    9.8 Why Metal Medicines Appealed¶

    If metal medicines were dangerous, why did they become so popular, in India as in early modern Europe? The reasons below are the present author’s suggestions, drawn from the general history of medicine; they have not been tested systematically against the Indian sources.

    • The prestige of metals. Gold, silver and mercury carried enormous symbolic value, and the idea that their imperishability could be transferred to the body was intuitively attractive. The rasa texts made this explicit in their doctrine that what perfects metal will perfect the body.

    • Potency in small quantities. Metal preparations were prescribed in small amounts, and many produced noticeable effects—some of them, in modern terms, toxic effects. In medical cultures that valued strong action, a medicine that “did something” could appear powerful.

    • Stability and portability. Calcined powders keep indefinitely, unlike many herbal preparations, and they are easy to store, transport and sell.

    • Elaborate preparation. The long, costly and secret preparation of a bhasma itself conferred value, and distinguished the learned physician-alchemist from ordinary practitioners.

    • The difficulty of detecting harm. Chronic metal poisoning develops slowly and its symptoms—weakness, anaemia, abdominal pain, nerve damage—resemble those of many diseases. Without modern toxicology, harm caused by a medicine was easily attributed to the disease, to incorrect preparation, or to the patient.

    These reasons are historical explanations, not justifications. They help to explain why the European medical tradition also used mercury, antimony and arsenic for centuries, and why it took modern toxicology and controlled trials to change medical practice.

    9.9 Siddha Preparations¶

    Tamil Siddha medicine (on its texts and history, see Section 2.14) has its own range of metal and mineral preparations, including calcined powders (parpam) and red calcined or sublimed preparations (centūram), as well as preparations of mercury, arsenic and other “poisons” (pāṣāṇam). Its methods and claims parallel those of Sanskrit rasaśāstra, and the same safety concerns apply (Weiss 2009).

    9.10 Bhasmas in the History of Chemistry¶

    Seen historically, the bhasma tradition represents a long and sophisticated practice of handling metals and minerals: controlled oxidation and sulphidation, prolonged grinding, sealed-vessel heating graded by fuel, and tests for completeness of reaction. It is one of the richest pre-modern traditions of what would now be called inorganic preparative chemistry, and it is a legitimate subject for historians of science. It is also a reminder that pre-modern medicine, in India as in Europe and China, often gave patients toxic metals. The history of European mercurial and antimonial medicine, and of the Chinese elixirs, belongs to the same story (Chapter 3). Honouring the ingenuity of past practitioners does not require endorsing their therapeutic claims.

    What Is Secure and What Is Debated

    Secure: what the texts prescribe; what the traditional tests can and cannot measure; what modern analysis finds in the products—mostly fine oxides and sulphides, often with other elements, of variable composition; and that product surveys and clinical cases have repeatedly found lead, mercury and arsenic in Ayurvedic medicines. Debated: whether correctly prepared bhasmas are safe in long-term use, on which Indian animal studies and international toxicology still disagree; whether particle size matters to their action; and whether any of them is effective, which only properly designed clinical trials could show.
