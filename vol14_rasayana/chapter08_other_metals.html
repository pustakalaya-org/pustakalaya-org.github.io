    Chapter 8: Gold, Silver, Copper, Lead, Tin and Their Alloys¶

    8.1 Gold (Suvarṇa, Hiraṇya)¶

    Gold—hiraṇya in the Ṛgveda, later usually suvarṇa (“of good colour”), with many poetic synonyms—was the most prestigious of metals in South Asia as elsewhere, associated with the sun, with fire and with ritual purity, and used for ornament, coinage, gifts to temples and, in medicine, as a rejuvenative.

    8.1.1 Sources¶

    Gold was obtained both from reefs and from river sands. The ancient workings in the Kolar and Hutti fields of Karnataka followed gold-bearing quartz veins to considerable depths (Section 4.3); the age of the deepest ancient workings is uncertain, but mining there goes back at least to the first millennium CE (Biswas and Biswas 1996; Craddock 1995). Alluvial gold was panned in many rivers—the Subarnarekha (“streak of gold”) of eastern India takes its name from it—and in the north-western mountains. Herodotus’s story of gold-digging “ants” in India, larger than foxes, is probably a garbled account of gold dust collected in the north-western highlands. A great deal of gold also entered India through trade: Roman gold coins in the early centuries CE, and gold and silver from many sources in later periods. Hoards of Roman gold and silver coins, mostly of the 1st century CE, have been found in large numbers in south India, especially in the Coimbatore region and elsewhere in Tamil Nadu and Kerala, along the routes that brought pepper, beryl, textiles and other goods to the coast. Roman writers complained of the drain of precious metal to India; Indian rulers and merchants, for their part, often valued the coins as bullion, and some were cut or defaced to prevent their circulation as currency.

    8.1.2 Assaying¶

    The Arthaśāstra describes the testing of gold by touchstone (nikaṣa): the streak left by an object on a black stone is compared with the streaks of reference pieces of known fineness. It also describes testing by colour and by fire, and lists the many ways in which goldsmiths could adulterate gold (Olivelle 2013). The touchstone was used throughout the ancient world and is still used by Indian goldsmiths.

    8.1.3 Refining¶

    Two refining processes were widespread in the pre-modern world and were known in India:

    • Cupellation: base metals are removed from gold or silver by melting it with lead in a porous vessel under an air blast; the lead and base metals oxidise and are absorbed by the vessel, leaving the precious metal.

    • Cementation with salt: thin sheets of gold–silver alloy are heated with salt and brick dust; the silver is converted to silver chloride and absorbed, leaving purer gold.

    These are well-understood processes, and both are described in detail in the Āʾīn-i Akbarī (Section 8.13). Statements that Sanskrit texts knew aqua regia or other mineral acids are not supported by the evidence.

    8.2 Silver (Rajata, Rūpya)¶

    Silver was obtained mainly from argentiferous lead ores (galena). The ore was smelted to lead, and the silver separated by cupellation. The Rajpura–Dariba and Zawar mines in Rajasthan were exploited for silver and lead from the first millennium BCE, and Craddock’s work there has documented both the mining and the cupellation (Craddock 2017). Silver was the main metal of the early punch-marked coinage and, from the 16th century, of the rupee. Large quantities came from abroad: Roman silver in the early centuries CE, and in the early modern period American silver brought by European trade to pay for Indian textiles and spices.

    8.3 Copper (Tāmra)¶

    Copper was the first metal to be smelted in South Asia. Copper objects appear at Mehrgarh in the Neolithic, and copper metallurgy was well established in the Chalcolithic cultures of the 4th and 3rd millennia BCE and in the Harappan civilisation (Kenoyer and Miller 1999; Agrawal 2000). The Ganeshwar–Jodhpura culture of north-eastern Rajasthan, near the Khetri copper belt, produced large numbers of copper objects and probably supplied the Harappan cities; the “Copper Hoards” of the Ganga–Yamuna region (2nd millennium BCE) include harpoons, flat axes and anthropomorphic figures of copper. Table 8.1 lists some of the ancient copper-producing regions.

    Table 8.1. Some Ancient Copper-Producing Regions of India

    Copper was smelted from oxide and carbonate ores (malachite, azurite, cuprite) and from sulphide ores (chalcopyrite), which require roasting before smelting. In simplified form, the smelting of a carbonate ore can be written

    \[\text{CuCO}_3 \cdot \text{Cu(OH)}_2 \xrightarrow{\ \text{heat}\ } 2\,\text{CuO} + \text{CO}_2 + \text{H}_2\text{O}, \qquad \text{CuO} + \text{CO} \longrightarrow \text{Cu} + \text{CO}_2 .\]

    8.4 Tin (Vaṅga, Trapu) and Lead (Nāga, Sīsa)¶

    South Asia has little tin. Small deposits are known (for example in Hazaribagh and in the Bastar region), but much of the tin used for bronze must have been imported—probably from Central Asia or Afghanistan in early periods, and later from Southeast Asia, where the Malay peninsula was a major source (Kenoyer and Miller 1999; Biswas and Biswas 1996).

    Lead, by contrast, was abundant, especially in the lead–zinc ores of Rajasthan. It was used for weights, for alloys (leaded bronze, pewter), for cupellation, and in the form of its oxides (litharge, red lead) and carbonate (white lead) as pigments and cosmetics. Lead was also used in Āyurvedic and rasa preparations (nāga bhasma). Lead is a cumulative poison that damages the brain, nerves, blood and kidneys, and is particularly harmful to children and in pregnancy; there is no safe level of exposure.

    The principal alloys of Indian metalwork are listed in Table 8.2.

    8.5.1 Bronze and High-Tin Bronze¶

    Copper–tin bronze was used in the Harappan period, though less than arsenical copper, and became important in the first millennium BCE. A special South Asian tradition is that of high-tin bronze, with roughly 20–25 per cent tin, which is brittle as cast but can be hot-worked and quenched to make vessels; Sharada Srinivasan and Ian Glover have documented the survival of this craft in Kerala, alongside cast high-tin bronze for bells and mirrors (Srinivasan and Glover 1995). High-tin bronze vessels are also known from early contexts in south India and Southeast Asia.

    8.5.2 The Aranmula Mirror¶

    The metal mirror (kaṇṇāṭi) made at Aranmula in Kerala is a cast and polished speculum alloy of copper and tin, with about a third tin, which takes a brilliant polish. Its makers keep their recipe within families; Srinivasan and Glover’s analyses confirmed its high-tin composition (Srinivasan and Glover 1995). Aranmula mirrors have a Geographical Indication registration.

    8.5.3 Pañcaloha and Aṣṭadhātu¶

    Texts on image-making and modern craft practice speak of images made of “five metals” (pañcaloha) or “eight metals” (aṣṭadhātu), lists that may include gold, silver, copper, tin, lead, iron and zinc, and sometimes mercury. These are ritual and symbolic names. Analyses of South Indian bronze images, including Chola images, generally show copper alloyed with variable amounts of tin and lead (and sometimes zinc), with at most traces of precious metals; there is no evidence that a fixed multi-metal recipe was followed (Dehejia 2002; Biswas and Biswas 1996). Associations of particular metals with particular planets belong to later astrological lore.

    8.5.4 Bidri Ware¶

    Bidri ware, named after Bidar in the Deccan and made from the Bahmanī or Barīd Shāhī period (15th–16th centuries) onwards, uses an alloy that is mainly zinc. Objects are cast, engraved and inlaid with silver (or brass), and the body is then blackened with a paste traditionally made of soil and sal ammoniac, which darkens the zinc alloy and leaves the inlay bright. Bidri is a good example of a craft that depended on the availability of metallic zinc (Chapter 7).

    8.6 Lost-Wax Casting and the Chola Bronzes¶

    The lost-wax (cire perdue) process was used in South Asia from the Harappan period onwards. A model is made in wax, often over a clay core; it is covered with layers of clay; the mould is heated so that the wax runs out; molten metal is poured into the cavity; and the mould is broken to release the casting, which is then chased and finished. The Sanskrit term madhūcchiṣṭa-vidhāna (“method of beeswax”) is the title of the account of the process in the Mānasollāsa, the encyclopaedia compiled about 1130 under the Cālukya king Someśvara III, which is the passage usually cited for it; other texts on image-making describe the method more briefly.

    The bronze images made under the Chola dynasty in Tamil Nadu (9th–13th centuries)—Śiva as Naṭarāja, Pārvatī, Viṣṇu, the saints—are among the masterpieces of world metal sculpture. They were cast solid, by the lost-wax method, in copper alloys, and were made to be carried in procession as well as installed in temples (Dehejia 2002). The craft continues among the hereditary image-makers (sthapati) of Swamimalai, who use a modelling wax of beeswax and resin and moulds of fine alluvial clay. The tribal Dhokra metal-casters of eastern and central India use a related lost-wax technique with wax threads. The images as works of art are treated in Volume 16, Vāstu–Śilpa, and the living metal crafts, including the Swamimalai workshops, in Volume 17, Śilpa–Kāru.

    8.6.1 The Metal of South Indian Images¶

    What the Chola images are made of has been established by analysis rather than from texts. The most extensive study is Sharada Srinivasan’s analysis of more than a hundred South Indian metal images of the Pallava, Chola, Vijayanagara and later periods, which combined the measurement of major and trace elements with lead isotope ratios (Section 4.5) (Srinivasan 1999). Its broad results are consistent with those of earlier, smaller studies. The images are copper alloys, most of them bronzes—copper with some tin and, in many images, a substantial proportion of lead, though some fine images are of bronze low in both tin and lead—and zinc-bearing alloys appear to become more common in the later periods, as metallic zinc and brass became widely available (Chapter 7). Gold and silver, when present at all, are at trace levels. The composition varies from image to image and from workshop to workshop, as one would expect of founders who melted down whatever scrap and ingot metal was at hand. Srinivasan also argued that the combination of composition, trace elements and isotope ratios can help to separate groups of images by period and region, and so to judge whether an image is consistent with the date and origin attributed to it on grounds of style—a matter of practical importance, since South Indian bronzes have long been stolen, copied and traded illicitly.

    The choice of a leaded alloy makes sense for casting. Lead does not dissolve in solid copper but remains as fine globules; it lowers the temperature at which the alloy is fully molten and keeps it fluid for longer as it fills the mould, so that thin limbs, fingers and the fine detail of ornament and flame-aureole are reproduced. It also makes the metal softer and easier to chase and file. Its disadvantage is that it weakens the alloy, but a solid image, unlike a sword or a bell, has no need of strength or resonance. The South Indian images were cast solid, which demands much more metal than hollow casting around a clay core, the method preferred in the Himalayan traditions; solid casting avoids the problem of supporting and removing a core but increases the risk of shrinkage cavities as a thick casting cools, and X-radiography of the images reveals such internal flaws as well as old repairs.

    The condition of the images also has a chemistry. Many were buried for safety in times of war or upheaval and recovered centuries later, and the surface of buried copper alloys is converted to green and blue corrosion products, mainly copper carbonates. Where chlorides from saline soil or sea air are present, a more damaging form of corrosion (“bronze disease”) can develop, in which unstable copper chlorides continue to react with moisture and oxygen after excavation and eat into the metal; conservators control it by removing chlorides and keeping the objects dry. Images in worship are treated differently: they are bathed, anointed and dressed, and the smooth, dark surfaces of long-worshipped images are the product of centuries of ritual handling.

    8.7 The Goldsmith’s Techniques¶

    Indian goldsmiths and silversmiths commanded the full range of pre-modern techniques for working precious metals—repoussé and chasing, wire-drawing and filigree, granulation (attested in early historic jewellery, for example at Taxila), and the kundan setting of the Mughal and Rajput courts. The crafts themselves are described in Volume 17, Śilpa–Kāru; here their chemistry is the point. Granulation depends on fusing tiny gold spheres to a gold surface with a copper-based bonding agent at a carefully controlled temperature. Solders were made by alloying gold with silver and copper to lower its melting point, and the colour of gold objects was improved by surface treatments that removed some of the base metal. Copper and silver were gilded with gold leaf or by fire-gilding with gold amalgam (Chapter 3). Kundan setting, in which stones are held by gold foil pressed around them without claws, needs gold of very high purity to be soft enough—one reason why refining (Section 8.13) mattered to the jeweller as well as to the mint.

    8.7.1 Enamelling¶

    Enamelling (mīnākārī) fuses powdered coloured glass onto a metal surface. It was practised at the Mughal court and became a speciality of Jaipur, Lucknow, Delhi, Varanasi and other centres; the finest work is champlevé enamel on gold, in which the design is cut into the metal and the hollows filled with enamel, often on the reverse of jewellery set with stones on the front. Enamelling requires glasses of different colours with suitable melting points, applied in order from the most to the least refractory; it is, in effect, a branch of glass chemistry (Chapter 12). Many traditional red and yellow enamels contained lead and other heavy-metal colorants.

    8.8 Other Traditions of Metal Images¶

    The Chola bronzes are the most celebrated, but by no means the only, Indian metal images.

    Early historic and Gupta images
    Copper-alloy images of the Buddha and of Hindu and Jain deities were cast from the early centuries CE. The largest surviving early example is the Sultanganj Buddha, a standing figure about 2.3 m high and weighing some 500 kg, cast in copper, found in Bihar in the 1860s and now in Birmingham Museum and Art Gallery; it is usually dated to the 6th–7th centuries.
    Eastern India
    Pāla-period workshops in Bihar and Bengal (8th–12th centuries) produced Buddhist and Hindu images in large numbers, which were carried to Tibet, Nepal and Southeast Asia and influenced metal sculpture there.
    The Himalaya and Kashmir
    Kashmir, the western Himalaya and Nepal developed their own traditions of cast images, often in brass with inlays of silver and copper; Newar metal-workers of the Kathmandu valley supplied Tibet for centuries.
    Jain images
    Jain temples of western India contain many images in brass and other alloys, often inscribed with the date and donor, which make them an important dated series for the history of alloys.

    The varied alloys of these images—copper, bronze, leaded bronze, brass—reflect local supplies of tin and zinc as well as workshop traditions; analyses of dated images are one of the best sources for the history of brass in India (Craddock 1998).

    8.9 Copper Plates¶

    Copper was also a writing material. From about the 4th century CE rulers across India recorded grants of land to Brahmins, temples and monasteries on copper plates (tāmrapaṭṭa, tāmraśāsana), engraved with the text of the grant and often joined by a ring bearing the royal seal. Thousands survive, and they are among the most important sources for the political, economic and social history of early medieval India. Their production required the casting or hammering of sheets of copper, careful engraving, and the casting of seals—a substantial demand for the services of metal-workers. The largest sets, such as the Chola grants from Tamil Nadu, consist of dozens of heavy plates.

    8.10 Metal-Working Communities¶

    The workers in non-ferrous metals formed distinct communities: goldsmiths (svarṇakāra, Hindi sunār), coppersmiths and brass-workers (tāmrakāra, kaseṛā, ṭhaṭherā), bell-metal workers (kansāri), and in the south the five-craft Viśvakarma groups who worked gold, brass and copper, iron, wood and stone (Mukherjee 1978). Their knowledge was hereditary and their social status ambiguous: indispensable to temple, court and household, they were nonetheless ranked below the priestly and landowning castes in the Brahmanical order, and in the south the Viśvakarma groups engaged in long disputes over their ritual rank. The Arthaśāstra’s detailed lists of goldsmiths’ frauds show the suspicion with which their customers regarded them (Olivelle 2013).

    8.11 Coinage¶

    South Asia has one of the world’s longest coinage traditions; Table 8.3 gives a brief outline.

    Table 8.3. Outline of Indian Coinage
    Period Coinage Metals and features
    c. 5th–2nd c. BCE Punch-marked coins Silver, of fixed weight, stamped with symbols
    c. 2nd c. BCE–3rd c. CE Indo-Greek, Śaka, Kuṣāṇa Silver, copper and (Kuṣāṇa) gold; bilingual legends
    c. 320–550 CE Gupta Gold dīnāra (the name from the Roman denarius); fine portrait types
    c. 1200–1550 Delhi Sultanate Silver tanka; billon and copper jital
    1540s onwards Sūr and Mughal Silver rupee (introduced by Sher Shāh Sūr), gold mohur, copper dām

    The Arthaśāstra’s superintendent of the mint was to issue coins of fixed alloy and weight, and its examiner of coins collected fees on coin brought into circulation (Olivelle 2013). The fineness of actual coins varied considerably over time, and periods of debasement—for instance of the later Gupta gold coinage—are visible in modern analyses.

    The techniques of coin production changed over time. The early punch-marked coins were made by cutting pieces of silver of the required weight from sheet or bar and stamping them with a number of separate symbol punches. Cast copper coins were also made, in moulds, some of which survive. From the Indo-Greek period onwards most coins were struck between engraved dies, which required dies of hard steel or bronze and skilled engravers; the fine portraiture of Kuṣāṇa and Gupta gold coins reflects the quality of the die-cutters. The Mughal mints, described in the Āʾīn-i Akbarī, refined the bullion brought to them by merchants, tested it, and struck coins of a high and remarkably consistent fineness (Abuʾl-Faẓl ʿAllāmī 1873). The silver rupee of the Mughals and their successors became one of the most widely used currencies of the Indian Ocean world.

    8.12 Ṭhakkura Pherū and the Delhi Mint¶

    Nearly three centuries before Abuʾl Faẓl, an official of the Delhi Sultanate wrote down what he knew of coins and metals. Ṭhakkura Pherū was a Jain from Kannāṇa, in present-day Haryana, who served the Khaljī sultans in the treasury and the mint in the early 14th century. He wrote a series of short technical works in Apabhraṃśa, the late literary Prakrit of northern India, on gems, coins, metals, architecture, astrology and arithmetic; his dated works fall between the 1290s and about 1318. His arithmetic, with its units of weight, measure and currency mixing Indian and Persian terms, is discussed in Volume 11, Gaṇita, and his treatise on gems in Chapter 10 (SaKHYa 2009).

    Two of his works concern the subject of this chapter. The Dravyaparīkṣā (“Examination of Money”), dated to about 1318, deals first with the testing and refining of gold and silver and with the making of the alloys used for coin, and then catalogues the coins that passed through the Delhi treasury—issues of earlier and regional dynasties as well as of the Delhi sultans themselves—giving for each its weight and its content of fine gold or silver in the units of the mint. The Dhātūtpatti (“The Origin of Metals”) is a short work on the metals and their alloys. Both are practical works, concerned with materials as the mint and the market handled them.

    The Dravyaparīkṣā is unusual in three respects. Its author was a working official who wrote from his own experience, not a scholar compiling earlier texts, and he wrote in a vernacular literary language rather than in Sanskrit. It is quantitative: its figures for the fineness of named coins make it, in effect, the earliest Indian assay register, and they allow numismatists to compare the written record with the analysis of surviving coins. And it reflects the mixed world of the Sultanate mint, in which Jain and Hindu bankers, assayers and money-changers worked for Turkish rulers, using Persian names for coins and units alongside Indian ones. Pherū’s works are a reminder that the technical knowledge of medieval India was not held only in Sanskrit, or only by Brahmins, and that the practice of the Delhi mint, like that of Akbar’s described below, was the product of continuous craft traditions that outlasted changes of dynasty.

    8.13 The Mughal Mint in the Āʾīn-i Akbarī¶

    The fullest pre-modern description of metallurgical practice in India is not a Sanskrit text but the opening chapters of the first book of Abuʾl Faẓl’s Āʾīn-i Akbarī (completed c. 1598), which describe the imperial mint (Abuʾl-Faẓl ʿAllāmī 1873). Abuʾl Faẓl writes as a courtier and credits many improvements to the emperor himself, but his account of the workmen, their tasks and their fees is detailed and specific, and it records the practice of Akbar’s mints in the late 16th century. The references below are to the āʾīns of Book 1 in Blochmann’s translation.

    8.13.1 The Workmen of the Mint¶

    The mint was run by a superintendent (dārogha) with an assayer (ṣayrafī), an officer (amīn) who settled disputes, an accountant (mushrif), a merchant who bought bullion, a treasurer and a weighman, and a long series of craftsmen: melters and refiners; the cutter (ẓarrāb) who cut blanks from the ingots; the engraver of the dies; the striker (sikkachī) who stamped the blanks between the dies; and others who recovered precious metal from the mint’s waste (Āʾīn 1.5–9). Abuʾl Faẓl remarks that Indian cutters prepared the blanks so exactly, without an anvil, that they did not differ by a hair, and he names the die-engraver Mawlānā ʿAlī Aḥmad of Delhi, whose lettering cut in steel rivalled the work of calligraphers. The list shows how far coinage depended on a division of skilled labour.

    8.13.2 Assaying¶

    The assayer judged gold first by its colour and lustre and then by the touchstone (Āʾīn 1.6). To make the test objective, the mint kept a set of touch-needles tipped with gold alloys of known fineness, prepared by melting pure gold with measured amounts of a silver–copper alloy; the streak of the gold under test was compared with the streaks of the needles. Fineness was expressed in degrees (bān). Abuʾl Faẓl notes that the Persians reckoned ten degrees for the purest gold, while Indian assayers used a scale of twelve (bārahbānī). The method is the touchstone assay of the Arthaśāstra, now organised with graded standards. Silver was judged by its colour, by filing and boring, and by heating a sample and plunging it into water: a black colour showed lead, red showed copper, and greyish white showed tin (Āʾīn 1.7).

    8.13.3 Refining¶

    The Āʾīn describes the standard pre-modern refining processes (Āʾīn 1.7–9):

    • Refining gold. Gold was refined by cementation. Thin plates of gold were stacked in layers with a mixture of brick dust and a salt, covered with dried cow-dung cakes and heated repeatedly. Silver and base metals passed out of the gold into the cement. The result was checked by the sound of a broken plate, by the touchstone, and by heating a sample alongside pure gold and comparing the weights. The spent cement (sālonī) was kept so that its silver could be recovered.

    • Refining silver. Silver was refined by cupellation, in a shallow hearth of wood ash in which the impure silver was melted with lead under the blast of bellows. The lead and base metals oxidised and soaked into the ash, leaving a disc of refined silver. The lead-rich ash (kharal, a form of litharge) was itself reprocessed.

    • Parting silver from gold. Silver was separated from gold by melting the alloy repeatedly with copper and with sulphur, which carried the silver off as a crust on the surface and left the gold.

    • Recovery from waste. Mint sweepings were washed to concentrate the heavy gold. The residue was rubbed with mercury, which took up the gold as an amalgam, and the mercury was then driven off by heat in a retort. Ashes and slags were smelted with lead and with fluxes such as borax, and the lead was cupelled again. Even the sweepers who carried off the mint’s dust washed it for gold and paid a duty on their profits.

    One of the materials named in these chapters, rāsī, is rendered “aqua fortis” in Blochmann’s translation. The text, however, says that it was made from saltpetre and an alkaline salt (ashkhar, which Blochmann renders as natron), and what it actually was is uncertain. The passage is therefore not good evidence for the preparation of mineral acids (Section 13.11).

    8.13.4 The Theory of Metals¶

    After describing practice, Abuʾl Faẓl adds two chapters of natural philosophy (Āʾīn 1.13–14). The first follows the theory of the Islamic philosophers and alchemists. The seven metals are formed in the earth from mercury and sulphur, in different proportions and degrees of purity, and mercury is called the “mother” and sulphur the “father” of the metals. The chapter then lists alloys in use in India, with their Indian names: a copper–tin bell metal (kānsī), a copper–lead alloy, brass (pītal) made with zinc (rūḥ-i tūtiyā), and the “eight-metal” alloy (ashtdhāt, Sanskrit aṣṭadhātu). It also mentions a lead-like metal that some identified with zinc and that was mined, it says, in the territory of Jalor in the province of Ajmer. The second chapter discusses the specific gravities of metals and minerals and their measurement by weighing in water and by displacement, and reproduces a table that Abuʾl Faẓl attributes to Abū Rayḥān al-Bīrūnī.

    The two kinds of chapter sit side by side. The practical chapters describe processes that a modern metallurgist recognises at once: touchstone assay, cementation, cupellation, parting with sulphur and amalgamation. The theoretical chapters rest on the mercury–sulphur theory that Arabic and Latin alchemy shared, and that Indian rasaśāstra held in its own terms (Chapter 3). As in early modern Europe, practical metallurgy and alchemical theory coexisted in the same culture, and the practice did not derive from the theory.

    8.14 Bells¶

    Temple bells, gongs and cymbals are traditionally cast in high-tin bronze (bell metal), whose hardness and elasticity give a long, clear ring. Bell-founders in many regions of India continue to cast bells by traditional methods. The acoustic quality depends on composition, shape, wall thickness and casting quality, which founders control by experience.

    The composition of bell metal reflects a balance that founders found empirically and that metallurgy explains. Copper alone is too soft and absorbs vibration; as tin is added, a hard, brittle tin-rich constituent (the delta phase) forms between the grains of copper-rich metal, and the alloy becomes stiffer and rings for longer. With about a fifth to a quarter of tin, as in most Indian bell metal (Table 8.2), the alloy has an excellent ring but is too brittle to be hammered when cold, which is why bells, gongs and the Aranmula mirror are cast to shape and finished by grinding and polishing. Above about a quarter of tin it becomes so brittle that a bell may crack when struck. Lead, which helps the casting of images, is unwelcome in a bell, because the soft lead globules damp the vibration and deaden the tone.

    The Kerala vessel-makers documented by Srinivasan and Glover exploit a different property of the same alloy. When high-tin bronze of about the bell-metal composition is heated to a red heat, the brittle constituent dissolves into a phase that can be hot-forged; if the forged vessel is then quenched in water, this phase is retained in a form that is much tougher than the cast metal. The craftsmen therefore hammer their vessels hot and quench them, producing thin, resonant bowls that would shatter if made from the cast alloy (Srinivasan and Glover 1995). Metallography of such vessels shows the needle-like structure of the quenched phase, quite different from the structure of a cast bell (Section 4.5). The same alloy, treated in two ways, thus gives two families of objects: cast bells and mirrors, and wrought and quenched vessels.

    8.15 Metals in Medicine¶

    The rasa texts prescribe calcined preparations of gold, silver, copper, tin, lead and other metals (Chapter 9). Calcined copper (tāmra bhasma) and copper sulphate (tuttha) are particularly notable: the texts themselves regard copper as dangerous unless thoroughly processed and list the symptoms of copper poisoning.

    The custom of storing drinking water in copper vessels is widespread in India. Copper ions are known to have antimicrobial effects, and some studies have reported reductions in bacterial contamination of water kept in copper pots. That is a different matter from taking copper compounds as medicine. Copper salts are toxic, and copper sulphate in particular has caused serious and fatal poisoning in India.

    Health and Safety Note

    Copper sulphate, lead compounds and other metal salts are poisonous. No doses or methods are given; see the full note in Section 1.1.

    8.16 Metals in Trade¶

    Indian metals and metalwork travelled widely. The Periplus of the Erythraean Sea records the export of Indian iron and steel and the import of copper, tin and lead into Indian ports in the 1st century CE (Casson 1989). Crucible steel went to Iran and the Arab world (Chapter 6); bronze images went with Buddhism and Hinduism to Sri Lanka, Southeast Asia and the Himalayas; brass and Bidri ware were exported to the Islamic world and Europe. At the same time India was a major importer of precious metals, especially silver and gold, in exchange for textiles, spices and other goods—a pattern noted with dismay by Roman writers and, much later, by European mercantilist economists.

    What Is Secure and What Is Debated

    Secure: the refining and assay methods of the Delhi and Mughal mints, recorded by Ṭhakkura Pherū and the Āʾīn-i Akbarī; the high-tin bronze traditions of bells, mirrors and quenched vessels; and the composition of South Indian images, which are copper alloys of variable make-up, mostly leaded bronzes rather than a fixed “five-metal” recipe. Debated or unknown: where the Harappans obtained their copper and tin; how early the Kolar and Hutti reefs were mined; and what the Āʾīn’s rāsī actually was.
