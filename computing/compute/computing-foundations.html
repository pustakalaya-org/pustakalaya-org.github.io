    Chapter 1: Computing Foundations¶

    Before you can understand CPUs, GPUs or supercomputers, you need the basics: what a computer is, how it stores numbers, text and pictures as bits, and how tiny switches called logic gates are combined into a working processor. This chapter starts from zero with everyday analogies, then shows how programs run, how we measure speed, and how chips have improved over 80 years of history. Take it slowly — everything later in the guide builds on these ideas.

    1.1 What Is a Computer?¶

    A computer is a machine that takes in information, follows a list of instructions to work on it, and gives back a result — and it can keep both the information and the instructions for later. The physical parts you can touch are called hardware; the instructions are called software. Computers come in every size, from a chip inside a washing machine to a supercomputer filling a warehouse.

    Think of it like…

    A computer is like a kitchen: ingredients come in (input), the cook follows a recipe (processing), dishes go out (output), and the fridge and recipe book keep things for later (storage).

    Real-world example

    Amara, a first-year student, uses a self-checkout at her local supermarket. The scanner reads a barcode (input), a small computer looks up the price and adds it to the total (processing), the screen shows £3.49 and a receipt prints (output), and the shop's server records the sale so stock can be reordered (storage). The same four-step pattern runs inside her phone, her car's engine controller and the cloud servers behind her music app.

    How it works¶

    1. Input devices (keyboard, touchscreen, sensors, network) turn real-world information into data.
    2. The processor (CPU) follows software instructions to calculate, compare and decide.
    3. Memory (RAM) holds the program and data being worked on right now; it forgets everything when the power goes off.
    4. Storage (SSD, hard disk, cloud) keeps files and programs long-term.
    5. Output devices (screen, speaker, printer, motors, network) deliver the result.
    6. Layers of software — firmware, the operating system and applications — tell the hardware what to do.

    Who uses it

    Every IT role starts here: technicians, developers, system administrators, embedded engineers and cloud engineers all describe systems in terms of input, processing, output and storage.

    Key takeaway

    Learn the IPOS model (input-process-output-storage) and the difference between hardware, software and firmware — they are classic first exam questions.

    Going deeper

    • Computers are usually grouped by role: embedded (one job, firmware only), personal (one user, general purpose), server (many users over a network) and HPC or supercomputer (one huge job split across thousands of nodes).
    • Volatility is the key memory-versus-storage difference: DRAM loses its contents almost immediately when power is cut, while flash and magnetic disks keep data for years — so 'saving' means copying data from RAM to storage.
    • A working system is layered: hardware runs firmware (UEFI on a PC, a boot ROM on a microcontroller), which loads an operating-system kernel, which manages applications — each layer hides detail from the one above.
    • The same basic design scales enormously: a small microcontroller may have a few kilobytes of RAM and run at tens of megahertz, while a 2026 cloud server has terabytes of RAM and hundreds of cores.
    • In data centres the unit of 'a computer' keeps growing: hyperscalers buy whole racks at a time, and AI training jobs treat thousands of GPUs linked by fast networks as one giant machine.
    • Mainframes such as IBM's z17 trade raw single-thread speed for reliability and input/output throughput, with redundant parts designed to keep transaction systems running for years without unplanned downtime.

    Common mistakes & misconceptions

    • Confusing RAM with storage: '16 GB of memory' is working space that empties at power-off, while '1 TB' is long-term storage; more storage does not speed up multitasking.
    • Thinking firmware is hardware: firmware is software stored on a chip, so it has bugs and security fixes and should be updated like other software.
    • Assuming 'computer' means a PC: most computers are embedded microcontrollers hidden in cars, appliances and sensors, shipped by the billions every year.

    Supercomputer
    Ranked twice a year by TOP500 (top500.org) · System · Current · Beginner    		 One of the world's fastest computers, built from thousands of linked nodes, used for science, weather forecasting and AI.     1.2 Types of Compute: CPU, GPU, TPU, NPU, MCU and the xPU Family¶

    Not every processor is a CPU. Engineers build different kinds of compute chips for different jobs: all-rounders (CPUs), massively parallel number crunchers (GPUs), AI specialists (TPUs and NPUs), tiny low-power controllers (MCUs), rewireable chips (FPGAs) and single-purpose custom chips (ASICs). Most modern devices combine several of them, and this topic is your map of the whole family before later chapters zoom in.

    Think of it like…

    Think of a hospital: the general doctor (CPU) can handle anything, a team of nurses (GPU) does thousands of routine checks at once, the radiologist (TPU or NPU) only reads scans but does it brilliantly, and the door sensor (MCU) does one tiny job all day on almost no energy.

    Real-world example

    Priya's photo app shows the whole family at work. The phone's CPU runs the app, the GPU draws the screen, the NPU recognises faces, and an MCU in the camera module controls autofocus. When she uploads the album, a data-centre CPU receives it, a DPU handles the network traffic, and GPUs or TPUs tag the pictures with AI.

    How it works¶

    1. Start from the job: is it general logic, huge parallel maths, AI, signal processing, networking or one fixed task?
    2. General, branch-heavy work goes to a CPU; the same sum on millions of numbers goes to a GPU or AI accelerator.
    3. Tiny, battery-powered or real-time control goes to a microcontroller (MCU).
    4. Fixed jobs made in huge volume justify an ASIC; jobs that change or ship in small volume suit an FPGA.
    5. A host CPU almost always stays in charge, handing work to accelerators over PCIe, NVLink or an on-chip bus.
    6. Compare chips using the right metric: IPC and GHz for CPUs, FLOPS and memory bandwidth for GPUs, TOPS per watt for NPUs, microamps per MHz for MCUs.

    Who uses it

    Everyone in computing meets this map: hardware buyers, cloud and AI engineers choosing instances, embedded developers choosing chips, and students facing the classic interview question "CPU vs GPU vs TPU?".

    Key takeaway

    Remember the flexibility-efficiency trade-off: CPU → GPU → FPGA → NPU/TPU → ASIC, each step more efficient at its job but less flexible.

    Going deeper

    • The "xPU" names are mostly marketing shorthand, not formal standards: the same letters can mean different things (IPU is Graphcore's Intelligence Processing Unit and also Intel's Infrastructure Processing Unit), so always check who makes the chip.
    • Typical power budgets span nine orders of magnitude: a sleeping MCU draws microwatts, a phone SoC a few watts, a desktop CPU 65-250 W, and a 2026 data-centre AI GPU around 1,000 W or more.
    • Most chips today are heterogeneous: a laptop or phone SoC puts CPU cores, a GPU, an NPU, an ISP, a DSP, video engines and a security processor on one die, and the operating system sends each task to the best engine.
    • A typical AI server contains several types at once: two host CPUs, four to eight GPUs or accelerators, one or more DPUs/SmartNICs for networking, and a small baseboard management controller (BMC) chip for remote management.
    • Each type is programmed differently: CPUs with ordinary languages, GPUs with CUDA, ROCm or SYCL, NPUs and TPUs through AI frameworks and compilers (ONNX Runtime, XLA), MCUs in C or MicroPython, and FPGAs and ASICs with hardware description languages such as Verilog.
    • Economics decides between FPGA and ASIC: an ASIC can cost tens to hundreds of millions of dollars to design at leading-edge nodes but is cheap per unit, so it only pays off at high volume or for huge efficiency gains.
    • "Compute" in cloud price lists also means virtual machine families — general-purpose, compute-optimised, memory-optimised, GPU/accelerator instances — which map back to these chip types (see the cloud chapter).

    Common mistakes & misconceptions

    • Mixing up the two TPUs: Tensor Processing Unit is Google's AI chip, while Trusted Platform Module is a security chip that stores keys — completely different things.
    • Thinking a GPU or NPU replaces the CPU: accelerators cannot boot or run the operating system on their own; a host CPU sends them work.
    • Comparing TOPS or FLOPS across vendors at face value: check the number format (FP16, FP8, INT8, FP4) and whether sparsity is included, or the comparison is meaningless.
    • Assuming a microcontroller is just a slow CPU: it is a whole computer on a chip, with its own flash, RAM and peripherals, designed for low power and real-time response.

    MCU — microcontroller unit

    SoC — system on a chip (as a compute package)
    Apple, Qualcomm, MediaTek, Samsung, Google, Intel, AMD · Concept · Current · Beginner    		 One chip combining several compute types — CPU, GPU, NPU, ISP, DSP, modem — plus memory controllers, so a device needs few other parts. A smartphone's whole computer is one SoC the size of a fingernail, drawing only a few watts.
    APU — accelerated processing unit
    AMD (term), also used in consoles · Processor · Current · Intermediate    		 AMD's name for a chip with CPU cores and a capable GPU sharing the same memory, avoiding a separate graphics card. PlayStation 5 and Xbox Series X use custom AMD APUs; Ryzen AI Max chips put a large GPU beside 16 CPU cores.
    DSP — digital signal processor
    Texas Instruments, Analog Devices, Cadence Tensilica, CEVA, Qualcomm Hexagon · Processor · Current · Intermediate    		 A processor optimised for fast multiply-accumulate maths on streams of samples, used for audio, radio, sensors and motor control. Noise-cancelling earbuds use a DSP to calculate and play anti-noise thousands of times per second.
    FPGA — field-programmable gate array
    AMD (Xilinx), Altera, Lattice, Microchip · Accelerator · Current · Intermediate    		 A chip of blank logic blocks and wiring that you configure into almost any digital circuit, and can reconfigure later. A stock-trading firm loads a custom FPGA design to decode market data in under a microsecond.
    ASIC — application-specific integrated circuit
    Designed by anyone, made by foundries such as TSMC, Samsung, Intel Foundry · Accelerator · Current · Intermediate    		 A chip designed for one fixed job: the fastest, most efficient option, but costly to design and impossible to change after manufacture. Bitcoin mining machines use SHA-256 ASICs that are thousands of times more efficient than GPUs at that single task.
    DPU / IPU — data or infrastructure processing unit
    NVIDIA (BlueField), AMD (Pensando), Intel (IPU), AWS (Nitro, similar idea) · Component · Current · Intermediate    		 A network-card processor with its own cores that runs networking, storage and security tasks so host CPUs are free for customer work. A cloud provider's DPU encrypts traffic and runs the virtual switch, so customers get every CPU core they pay for.
    Other AI accelerators (LPU, IPU, wafer-scale)
    Groq, Graphcore (SoftBank), Cerebras, AWS Trainium/Inferentia, Microsoft Maia, Meta MTIA · Accelerator · Current · Intermediate    		 Custom AI chips with their own names and designs, competing with GPUs on speed, cost or energy for training or inference. A chatbot company serves answers on Cerebras or Groq hardware because tokens stream out faster than on its GPUs.
    Coprocessor and host model
    General computer-architecture idea · Concept · Current · Beginner    		 A coprocessor or accelerator does specialised work that a host CPU sends it, then hands results back; the CPU stays in charge. The CPU loads an AI model, copies data to the GPU over PCIe, launches the job, then collects the answer.
    BMC — baseboard management controller
    ASPEED (AST2600 and newer), OpenBMC project · Component · Current · Intermediate    		 A small separate processor on a server motherboard that lets administrators power-cycle, monitor and repair the server remotely, even when it is off. An engineer in London reboots a frozen server in Frankfurt through its BMC web page without visiting the data centre.
    QPU — quantum processing unit
    IBM, Google, Quantinuum, IonQ, Rigetti · Processor · Emerging · Intermediate    		 A processor built from qubits that solves certain problems in a completely different way; it is controlled by ordinary CPUs and is still experimental. A chemistry researcher sends a small molecule-simulation circuit from a laptop to a cloud QPU and gets back measurement statistics.

    1.3 Specialised Compute Engines: ISPs, Media Engines, Modems, Security Processors and More¶

    Besides the famous CPU and GPU, chips hide many smaller engines that each do one job very efficiently: turning camera data into photos, compressing video, running the mobile radio, guarding passwords and keys, or controlling machines in real time. Knowing they exist explains why a phone can record 4K video all day while a general-purpose processor alone would overheat.

    Think of it like…

    A big restaurant kitchen has a head chef, but also a pastry station, a grill station and a dishwasher machine; each station is narrow but much faster at its own task than the head chef would be.

    Real-world example

    When Tomás joins a video call on his phone, the ISP cleans up the camera image, the NPU blurs his background, the video engine compresses the stream to AV1 or H.264, the modem sends it over 5G, and the security processor confirmed his face unlock beforehand. The CPU mostly coordinates, so the battery lasts the whole meeting.

    How it works¶

    1. Find a task that is common, repetitive and expensive on a CPU, such as video encoding or image processing.
    2. Designers build a fixed-function or narrowly programmable engine for it inside the SoC.
    3. The operating system or driver exposes it through an API, for example a video-encode API or a camera framework.
    4. Apps call the API; the engine does the work at a fraction of the CPU's energy.
    5. If the engine cannot handle a format or feature, software falls back to the CPU or GPU, more slowly.

    Who uses it

    Embedded, mobile and media engineers, industrial automation engineers and security engineers work with these engines daily; everyone else benefits from them unknowingly.

    Key takeaway

    When a device does something demanding for hours on a small battery — video, camera, radio, face unlock — a dedicated engine is almost certainly doing it, not the CPU.

    Going deeper

    • Fixed-function engines are often 10-100 times more energy-efficient than running the same task in software on a CPU, because they skip instruction fetch and decode and keep data in local buffers.
    • Video engines support specific codecs and profiles: most 2026 chips decode H.264, HEVC, VP9 and AV1, and many also encode AV1; an older chip without AV1 encoding falls back to slow, power-hungry software encoding.
    • Security processors run their own firmware in isolation from the main OS: Apple's Secure Enclave, Google's Titan M2, Microsoft Pluton and discrete TPM chips store keys so even a compromised operating system cannot read them.
    • Modems (baseband processors) run a separate real-time operating system and certified radio firmware; Qualcomm dominates, while Apple introduced its own C1 modem in 2025.
    • Real-time processors such as Arm Cortex-R cores sit inside car ECUs, SSD controllers and hard drives, where a guaranteed response time matters more than raw speed.
    • Industrial PLCs are rugged computers that run control logic in a fixed scan cycle, programmed in IEC 61131-3 languages like ladder logic, and can run for decades.
    • Soft-core processors are CPU designs loaded into an FPGA's fabric (for example AMD MicroBlaze V and Altera Nios V, both RISC-V based), letting one FPGA hold both custom logic and a small CPU.

    Common mistakes & misconceptions

    • Assuming every format is hardware accelerated: if your chip's video engine lacks a codec, encoding silently falls back to the CPU and battery life collapses.
    • Treating 'neuromorphic' or 'quantum' processors as drop-in faster CPUs: they solve narrow classes of problems with very different programming models.
    • Forgetting that hidden engines run firmware too: modems, BMCs and security processors need updates, and vulnerabilities in them are real security risks.

    Try it: Check which hardware video engines your computer offers¶

    # Linux with Intel/AMD graphics (package: vainfo)
vainfo | grep -iE 'profile|entrypoint' | head -n 20

# NVIDIA GPU: list encoders and decoders FFmpeg can use

# macOS: hardware encoders are exposed through VideoToolbox

    1.4 Binary, Hexadecimal and Units of Data¶

    Computers store everything using only two symbols, 0 and 1, because an electronic switch is easy to make either off or on. One 0-or-1 is called a bit, and eight bits make a byte — enough for one letter of text. Long strings of bits are hard for humans to read, so programmers write them in hexadecimal (base 16), where one hex digit stands for exactly four bits.

    One value, three notations

    Binary Hexadecimal
    0 (decimal) 0000 0
    9 1001 9
    10 1010 A
    15 1111 F
    16 0001 0000 10
    200 1100 1000 C8
    255 1111 1111 FF
    65,535 1111 1111 1111 1111 FFFF

    Each hex digit maps to exactly four bits, so converting is a lookup, not arithmetic.

    Think of it like…

    Binary is like a row of light switches where each switch is worth double the one to its right (1, 2, 4, 8 ...): the number is simply the total of the switches that are on.

    Real-world example

    Leo is styling a web page and sees the colour #FF8800. He splits it into three bytes: FF (255 red), 88 (136 green) and 00 (0 blue) — a bright orange. Later he buys a '1 TB' external drive and Windows shows only about 931 GB. Nothing is missing: the box counts 1,000,000,000,000 bytes, while Windows divides by 1024 × 1024 × 1024 (and labels the result 'GB' even though it really means GiB).

    How it works¶

    1. Each position in a binary number is worth a power of 2: 1, 2, 4, 8, 16, 32, 64, 128 and so on.
    2. To turn binary into decimal, add up the place values of the 1s: 1011 = 8 + 0 + 2 + 1 = 11.
    3. To turn decimal into binary, keep dividing by 2 and read the remainders from bottom to top.
    4. Group bits in fours from the right; each group becomes one hex digit (0-9, then A-F for 10-15).
    5. Measure sizes in bytes: decimal (SI) units step by 1000 (kB, MB, GB) and binary (IEC) units by 1024 (KiB, MiB, GiB).

    Who uses it

    Programmers, network engineers (IP and MAC addresses), security analysts (hex dumps) and web designers (colour codes) use binary and hex every day.

    Key takeaway

    Memorise the powers of two up to 2^16 = 65,536, plus 2^10 = 1024 and 2^32 ≈ 4.3 billion — they come up constantly.

    Going deeper

    • n bits give 2^n distinct values: 8 bits give 256, 16 bits 65,536, 32 bits about 4.29 billion and 64 bits about 1.8 × 10^19 — limits that decide the size of counters, addresses and IDs.
    • Hex is written differently by context: 0x in C, Python and most languages, # in CSS colours, an h suffix in some assemblers, and %XX percent-encoding inside URLs.
    • Drive makers use decimal units (1 TB = 10^12 bytes) while Windows divides by powers of 1024 but keeps decimal labels; macOS has shown decimal units since 2009, so the same drive looks different on each.
    • Byte order shows up when reading binary files or packet captures: the 32-bit value 0x12345678 is stored as 78 56 34 12 on little-endian x86 but sent as 12 34 56 78 on the network.
    • Bit masks pack many yes/no flags into one integer: Unix permissions, CPU feature flags reported by CPUID and network header fields are all read with AND masks and shifts.
    • To convert quickly, memorise the 16 nibble patterns (0000-1111 ↔ 0-F); any binary number then converts to hex by grouping bits in fours from the right, and back again.

    Common mistakes & misconceptions

    • Mixing up b and B: 100 Mbit/s is 12.5 MB/s, not 100 MB/s — a small b means bits and a capital B means bytes.
    • Assuming a '1 TB' drive is faulty when Windows shows 931 GB: it is a units difference (10^12 bytes ÷ 2^30), not missing space.
    • Grouping bits from the left when converting to hex: always group in fours from the least significant (right) end, padding the leftmost group with zeros.

    Try it: Convert between number bases in Python¶

    >>> bin(11), hex(255), oct(8)
('0b1011', '0xff', '0o10')
>>> int('1011', 2), int('ff', 16)
(11, 255)
>>> f'{200:08b}'          # 200 as 8 bits
'11001000'
>>> 10**12 / 2**30        # a '1 TB' drive measured in GiB
931.3225746154785

    1.5 Whole Numbers: Integers, Two's Complement and Overflow¶

    Computers store whole numbers (integers) in a fixed number of bits, such as 8, 32 or 64. Unsigned integers count upwards from zero; to include negative numbers almost every computer uses a trick called two's complement, where the top bit means 'minus a big amount'. Because the box has a fixed size, a number that grows too big wraps around — this is called overflow.

    The same 8 bits read two ways

    As unsigned As two's complement
    00000000 0 0
    00000101 5 5
    01111111 127 127 (int8 max)
    10000000 128 −128 (int8 min)
    11111011 251 −5
    11111111 255 (uint8 max) −1

    The bit patterns are identical; the meaning changes only once the top bit is 1.

    Think of it like…

    A fixed-size integer is like a car's six-digit mileage counter: after 999,999 it rolls back to 000,000.

    Real-world example

    Sam writes a small game that stores a player's coins in an 8-bit unsigned variable (0-255). A player with 250 coins picks up 10 more and suddenly has 4 — the value wrapped around (260 − 256). Switching to a 32-bit integer raises the limit to about 4.29 billion. In the real world, the first Ariane 5 rocket was lost in 1996 partly because a 64-bit floating-point value was converted into a 16-bit signed integer that was too small to hold it.

    How it works¶

    1. Choose a width: n bits give 2^n different patterns (8 bits give 256).
    2. Unsigned numbers run from 0 to 2^n − 1 (8-bit: 0 to 255).
    3. In two's complement the top bit is worth −2^(n−1), so 8-bit signed numbers run from −128 to +127.
    4. To make a number negative, flip every bit and add 1: 5 = 00000101 becomes 11111011 = −5.
    5. The same adder circuit works for signed and unsigned numbers; a carry out of the top bit is simply dropped.
    6. If the true answer does not fit, overflow happens: depending on the language the value wraps, saturates or raises an error.

    Who uses it

    C/C++ and embedded programmers, game developers, database designers and security researchers (who hunt integer-overflow bugs) must all understand integer sizes.

    Key takeaway

    Know the ranges by heart: int8 −128 to 127, uint8 0 to 255, int32 about ±2.1 billion, uint32 0 to about 4.29 billion.

    Going deeper

    • One ADD instruction serves signed and unsigned numbers; only the flags differ — the carry flag signals unsigned overflow and the overflow flag signals signed overflow, and the program chooses which to test.
    • In C and C++ signed overflow is undefined behaviour, so optimisers may assume it never happens, while unsigned arithmetic wraps modulo 2^n; building with -fsanitize=undefined catches signed overflow during testing.
    • Languages handle overflow differently: Java and Go wrap silently, Rust panics in debug builds and wraps in release builds unless you use checked_ or saturating_ methods, C# offers a checked keyword, and Python integers never overflow.
    • Sign extension copies the top bit when widening (−5 as int8, 0xFB, becomes 0xFFFFFFFB as int32), while zero extension pads unsigned values with zeros; x86 MOVSX and MOVZX do exactly this.
    • The two's complement range is asymmetric: int8 holds −128 but not +128, so negating or taking abs() of the minimum value overflows — a classic edge case for unit tests.
    • Integer division truncates toward zero in C, Java and Rust (−7 / 2 = −3), but Python's // rounds down (−7 // 2 = −4), and the sign of the remainder differs to match.

    Common mistakes & misconceptions

    • Checking for overflow after it happens in C (if (a + b < a)) on signed integers: the compiler may delete the check; test before adding or use __builtin_add_overflow.
    • Mixing signed and unsigned values in C comparisons: -1 < 1u is false, because -1 is converted to a huge unsigned number.
    • Assuming int is always 32 bits: C only guarantees at least 16, so use int32_t or int64_t when the exact width matters.
    • Storing money as floating point instead of whole pence or cents (or a decimal type), which introduces rounding errors into totals.

    Try it: Watch 8-bit integers wrap around and see two's complement¶

    import ctypes

print(ctypes.c_int8(127 + 1).value)     # -128 (signed overflow)

    1.6 Real Numbers: Floating Point and IEEE 754¶

    Floating-point numbers are how computers store numbers with fractions, as well as very large and very tiny numbers. They work like scientific notation (6.02 × 10²³), keeping a sign, a fixed number of significant digits and an exponent — but in binary. Because only so many digits fit, most decimal fractions such as 0.1 cannot be stored exactly, so tiny rounding errors appear.

    Think of it like…

    Floating point is like writing every number on a small card with room for only seven significant digits plus '× 10 to the power of something': you can write the size of an atom or of a galaxy, but never with unlimited detail.

    Real-world example

    Priya, a second-year student, types 0.1 + 0.2 == 0.3 into Python and gets False. The reason: 0.1 and 0.2 are endless repeating fractions in binary (just as 1/3 is 0.333... in decimal), so each is stored very slightly off, and their sum prints as 0.30000000000000004. Her lecturer explains that scientific code compares floats with a tolerance using math.isclose(), while banking code uses decimal types or counts whole pennies.

    How it works¶

    1. Write the number in binary scientific notation, for example 6.5 = 1.101 (binary) × 2².
    2. Store the sign bit: 0 for positive, 1 for negative.
    3. Store the exponent with a bias added (127 for FP32, 1023 for FP64) so it is always a positive pattern.
    4. Store the fraction bits after the leading 1; that 1 is implied, which saves a bit.
    5. Round to the nearest value that fits — this rounding is where small errors creep in.
    6. Reserved patterns represent zero, ±infinity, NaN (Not a Number) and tiny 'subnormal' numbers.

    Who uses it

    Scientific programmers, game and graphics developers, data scientists, AI engineers (who use FP16, BF16 and FP8) and anyone handling money must understand floating-point limits.

    Key takeaway

    Never compare floats with ==; use a tolerance such as math.isclose(), and remember FP64 gives about 15-16 significant decimal digits and FP32 about 7.

    Going deeper

    • Precision is relative, not absolute: FP64 values near 1 are about 2.2 × 10^-16 apart, but near 10^16 neighbouring doubles are 2 apart, so adding 1 to 10^16 changes nothing.
    • Whole numbers are exact in FP64 only up to 2^53 (about 9 × 10^15) — which is why JavaScript defines Number.MAX_SAFE_INTEGER and BigInt, and why 64-bit IDs in JSON are often sent as strings.
    • Subnormal numbers fill the gap next to zero so underflow is gradual, but many processors handle them far more slowly; performance code sometimes enables flush-to-zero modes (FTZ/DAZ) to avoid that cost.
    • The default IEEE 754 rounding is round-to-nearest with ties to even, which avoids systematic bias; directed modes (towards +∞, −∞ or zero) support interval arithmetic and error analysis.
    • Floating-point addition is not associative — (a + b) + c can differ from a + (b + c) — so parallel reductions, -ffast-math and different GPU thread orders can change results slightly between runs.
    • AI moved to narrow formats because moving data dominates cost: going from FP32 to BF16 halves memory traffic and roughly doubles tensor-core throughput, and FP8 or FP4 with scaling factors push further.
    • Besides IEEE 754-2019, a separate IEEE working group (P3109) and the industry OCP FP8 and microscaling (MX) specifications define the 8-bit and smaller formats used in machine learning.

    Common mistakes & misconceptions

    • Comparing floats with ==: use a relative or absolute tolerance (math.isclose, numpy.allclose), because tiny rounding differences are normal.
    • Using binary floats for currency: 0.10 cannot be stored exactly, so use whole cents or a decimal type for money.
    • Believing more printed decimal places means more accuracy: FP32 carries only about 7 significant digits whatever print() displays.
    • Expecting bit-identical results across machines with -ffast-math or different summation orders; reproducibility needs a fixed order and strict IEEE settings.

    Try it: Look inside a float with Python's struct module¶

print(f'{bits:032b}')
# 0 10000001 10100000000000000000000
# sign | exponent (127+2) | fraction (.101)

    Emoji (U+1F600) F0 9F 98 80 D8 3D DE 00 00 01 F6 00

    UTF-8 keeps ASCII to one byte, while characters outside the Basic Multilingual Plane need four bytes in every encoding.

    Think of it like…

    Character encoding is like a codebook shared by two spies: if both use the same book the message is clear, but with different books it turns into nonsense.

    Real-world example

    Kofi exports a customer list from an old accounting program, and names like 'José' appear as 'JosÃ©'. The file was saved in UTF-8 but opened as Windows-1252, so the two bytes of 'é' showed up as two wrong characters — this garbling is called mojibake. Re-opening the file as UTF-8 fixes it. The same week he notices a 3-minute song is about 32 MB as a CD-quality WAV file but only about 7 MB as a 320 kbit/s MP3, because MP3 throws away sounds people barely hear.

    How it works¶

    1. Each character is given a number called a code point: 'A' = U+0041, '€' = U+20AC, '😀' = U+1F600.
    2. An encoding turns code points into bytes: UTF-8 uses 1-4 bytes per character, UTF-16 uses 2 or 4.
    3. Images are grids of pixels; each pixel usually stores red, green and blue values from 0 to 255 (24 bits).
    4. Sound is sampled: the wave's height is measured many times a second (44,100 for CD audio) and each sample stored as a number.
    5. Compression shrinks files: lossless (PNG, FLAC, ZIP) keeps every bit, lossy (JPEG, MP3, AAC) discards detail people barely notice.

    Who uses it

    Web and app developers, data engineers, localisation teams, multimedia and game developers, and digital forensics analysts all work with encodings and media formats.

    Key takeaway

    Use UTF-8 everywhere (files, databases, web pages), and remember that a raw image needs width × height × bytes per pixel of memory.

    Going deeper

    • UTF-8 is self-synchronising: lead bytes begin 0, 110, 1110 or 11110 and continuation bytes begin 10, so a decoder can find character boundaries even after jumping into the middle of a stream.
    • One visible character can be several code points: 'é' may be U+00E9 or 'e' plus the combining accent U+0301, and family emoji join several code points with zero-width joiners — such units are called grapheme clusters.
    • Uncompressed media sizes are simple products: image = width × height × bytes per pixel; audio = sample rate × bytes per sample × channels × seconds; raw video multiplies one frame by frames per second.
    • Images often add a fourth alpha (transparency) channel, giving 32-bit RGBA pixels, and HDR video uses 10 or 12 bits per colour channel to avoid visible banding in smooth gradients.
    • Lossy codecs exploit human perception: JPEG uses the discrete cosine transform and quantisation, MP3 and AAC use psychoacoustic models, and video codecs such as H.264, HEVC and AV1 mostly encode changes between frames.
    • Dedicated media engines in CPUs, GPUs and phone SoCs encode and decode H.264, HEVC and AV1 in hardware, using a small fraction of the power that software decoding on general cores would need.

    Common mistakes & misconceptions

    • Assuming one character equals one byte: in UTF-8 'naïve' is 6 bytes for 5 characters, so byte-length limits can cut a character in half.
    • Relying on the system default encoding: always pass encoding='utf-8' (or the correct encoding) when reading and writing text files in code.
    • Re-saving lossy files repeatedly: each JPEG or MP3 re-encode loses more detail, so keep a lossless master and export copies from it.
    • Treating Base64 as encryption: it is only an encoding, and anyone can decode it instantly.

    1.8 Logic Gates, Boolean Algebra and Transistors¶

    A logic gate is a tiny circuit that takes one or more bits in and gives one bit out according to a simple rule, such as 'output 1 only if both inputs are 1' (AND). Gates are built from transistors, which are electrically controlled switches. The maths for combining true and false values is called Boolean algebra, and with enough gates you can build every calculation a computer does.

    Truth table for three key gates

    AND OR XOR
    A=0, B=0 0 0 0
    A=0, B=1 0 1 1
    A=1, B=0 0 1 1
    A=1, B=1 1 1 0

    XOR is 1 only when its inputs differ — exactly the sum bit of a one-bit adder.

    Think of it like…

    Gates are like rules for opening a door: an AND door needs a key and a passcode, an OR door opens with either one, and a NOT door does the opposite of whatever you tell it.

    Real-world example

    Jade designs an alarm for a fictional bike shop, Spoke & Sprocket: the siren should sound if the system is armed AND (the door opens OR the window breaks). In Boolean algebra that is Siren = Armed · (Door + Window). She writes its truth table (8 rows for 3 inputs), builds it in the free simulator Logisim-evolution with one OR gate and one AND gate, and checks every row before soldering the real circuit.

    How it works¶

    1. Represent true/false as 1/0, which inside a chip means a high or low voltage.
    2. Basic gates: NOT flips a bit, AND outputs 1 only if all inputs are 1, OR outputs 1 if any input is 1.
    3. Derived gates: NAND and NOR are AND and OR followed by NOT; XOR outputs 1 when its inputs differ.
    4. A truth table lists the output for every input combination — 2^n rows for n inputs.
    5. Boolean algebra rules, including De Morgan's laws, simplify an expression so it needs fewer gates.
    6. On a chip each gate is a handful of CMOS transistors switching billions of times per second.

    Who uses it

    Digital design, FPGA and chip (ASIC) engineers, embedded developers and computer science students use gates and Boolean logic; every programmer uses the same logic in if-statements.

    Key takeaway

    Remember that NAND (or NOR) on its own can build every other gate, and De Morgan's law: NOT(A AND B) = (NOT A) OR (NOT B).

    Going deeper

    • A CMOS gate pairs a pull-up network of p-type transistors with a pull-down network of n-type transistors; for any input only one network conducts, so almost no current flows while the gate is not switching.
    • Dynamic power in CMOS is roughly activity × capacitance × voltage² × frequency, which is why lowering the supply voltage saves so much energy and why chips switch off the clock to idle blocks (clock gating).
    • NAND is the natural CMOS gate: it needs 4 transistors, while AND needs 6 (a NAND plus an inverter), so standard-cell libraries build most logic from NAND, NOR and other inverting gates.
    • Every gate has a propagation delay measured in picoseconds; the slowest chain of gates between two flip-flops, the critical path, sets the maximum clock frequency of the whole design.
    • Any Boolean function can be written as a sum of products (an OR of ANDs) taken straight from the truth table's 1-rows; Karnaugh maps by hand, or tools such as Espresso, then minimise it.
    • Real chips are not drawn gate by gate: logic-synthesis tools from Synopsys, Cadence and the open-source Yosys project turn hardware-description code into optimised gate netlists.

    Common mistakes & misconceptions

    • Confusing logical and bitwise operators in code: && and || work on whole true/false values, while & and | act on every bit separately.
    • Applying De Morgan's law halfway: negate each input and also swap AND for OR — NOT(A AND B) equals NOT A OR NOT B.
    • Thinking gates switch instantly: every gate has a delay, and ignoring delays causes glitches and timing failures in real circuits.

    Try it: Print truth tables with a tiny Python script¶

    1.9 Circuit Building Blocks: Combinational and Sequential Logic¶

    Gates are combined into bigger building blocks. Combinational circuits — adders, multiplexers, decoders and the ALU — produce outputs that depend only on their inputs right now, like a calculator. Sequential circuits — latches, flip-flops, registers and counters — also have memory, so their output depends on what happened before, and a clock signal keeps them all in step.

    Think of it like…

    A combinational circuit is like a vending machine's price display, which just reacts to the button you press now; a sequential circuit is like its coin counter, which remembers how much you have already put in.

    Real-world example

    For a lab, Tomás builds a 4-bit adder by chaining four full adders, each passing its carry to the next, so 0110 (6) + 0111 (7) gives 1101 (13). He then adds a 4-bit register made of D flip-flops to hold a running total, and a push-button clock: each press adds the input to the stored value. Without realising it, he has built the heart of a CPU — an ALU, a register and a clock.

    How it works¶

    1. A half adder uses XOR for the sum bit and AND for the carry; a full adder also accepts a carry in.
    2. Chaining full adders makes a multi-bit adder; faster designs work out the carries in advance (carry-lookahead).
    3. Multiplexers pick one of several inputs; decoders turn a binary number into a single active output line.
    4. An ALU combines adders and logic gates with a multiplexer that chooses which operation's result to output.
    5. Feedback loops create memory: a latch holds a bit, and a flip-flop captures its input only at a clock edge.
    6. Registers, counters and finite state machines are flip-flops plus logic, all synchronised by the clock.

    Who uses it

    Digital designers, FPGA and ASIC engineers, verification engineers and computer architecture students work with these blocks daily, usually written in a hardware description language.

    Key takeaway

    Combinational = no memory, output depends only on present inputs; sequential = memory plus a clock. Also learn the full adder: Sum = A ⊕ B ⊕ Cin, Cout = AB + Cin(A ⊕ B).

    Going deeper

    • Synchronous designs work because every flip-flop updates on the same clock edge: the combinational logic between two registers must settle within one clock period, minus setup time and clock skew.
    • Setup and hold times are a flip-flop's timing contract: data must be stable just before (setup) and just after (hold) the clock edge, or the flip-flop can become metastable and output garbage.
    • Carry-lookahead adders compute generate (G = A·B) and propagate (P = A ⊕ B) signals so all carries are ready after about log2(n) gate levels instead of n, letting 64-bit addition fit in a fraction of a nanosecond.
    • Finite state machines come in two styles: Moore machines' outputs depend only on the current state, while Mealy machines' outputs also depend on the current inputs and often need fewer states.
    • FPGAs build these blocks from lookup tables (LUTs), which can mimic any small combinational function, plus flip-flops — so the same Verilog can run on an FPGA prototype or become a custom chip.
    • Signals crossing between unrelated clock domains need synchronisers, typically two flip-flops in series, to make metastability vanishingly rare — a classic source of intermittent, hard-to-reproduce hardware bugs.

    Common mistakes & misconceptions

    • Confusing latches and flip-flops: a latch is transparent the whole time it is enabled, while a flip-flop samples only at a clock edge.
    • Writing HDL like software: Verilog describes parallel hardware, so every always block runs at the same time rather than line by line.
    • Assuming ripple-carry adders are good enough for wide numbers: the carry delay grows with width, so CPUs use lookahead or prefix adders.

    Try it: A full adder and a 4-bit ripple-carry adder in Python¶

    def full_adder(a, b, cin):
    s = a ^ b ^ cin
    cout = (a & b) | (cin & (a ^ b))
    return s, cout

def add4(x, y):
    carry, result = 0, 0
    for i in range(4):
        s, carry = full_adder((x >> i) & 1, (y >> i) & 1, carry)
        result |= s << i
    return result, carry

print(add4(6, 7))   # (13, 0)
print(add4(9, 9))   # (2, 1)  -> overflow: 18 = 16 + 2

    1.10 Computer Architecture Models: von Neumann vs Harvard¶

    Almost every computer follows a plan written down in 1945: a processor, one memory that holds both the program and its data, and input/output, all joined by shared wires called buses. This is the von Neumann, or stored-program, architecture. The Harvard architecture instead keeps instructions and data in separate memories so both can be fetched at once, and most modern chips mix the two ideas.

    Three ways to connect processor and memory

    von Neumann Harvard Modified Harvard
    Memories One, for code and data Separate code and data One shared main memory
    Paths to CPU One shared bus Two independent buses Split L1i and L1d caches
    Code + data fetched together? No, they take turns Yes Yes, from the L1 caches
    Typical use Classic textbook model DSPs, small MCUs (AVR) PCs, phones, servers

    Most modern chips are von Neumann outside the core and Harvard inside it.

    Think of it like…

    Von Neumann is a chef with one bookshelf holding both recipes and ingredients, walking up and down one corridor; Harvard gives the chef two shelves and two corridors, so a recipe and an ingredient can arrive at the same time.

    Real-world example

    Noor compares her laptop with the microcontroller in her robotics club's line-following robot. The laptop's CPU loads programs and data from the same RAM (von Neumann), but inside the chip it has separate level-1 caches for instructions and data — a 'modified Harvard' design. The robot's microcontroller keeps its program in flash and its variables in separate SRAM, much closer to pure Harvard. Both still slow down when the processor has to wait for memory: the von Neumann bottleneck.

    How it works¶

    1. The control unit fetches the next instruction from memory, using the program counter to know where it is.
    2. Addresses travel on the address bus, values on the data bus, and read/write commands on the control bus.
    3. The ALU and registers carry out the instruction, and results go back to registers or memory.
    4. In a von Neumann design one path carries both instructions and data, so they must take turns.
    5. In a Harvard design separate paths let an instruction and a piece of data be fetched in the same cycle.
    6. Modern CPUs add caches, including split L1 instruction and data caches, to soften the memory bottleneck.

    Who uses it

    Computer architects, embedded and firmware engineers, performance engineers and students sitting computer architecture exams all rely on this model.

    Key takeaway

    Know the von Neumann bottleneck by name: processors became far faster than memory, which is why caches, prefetching and high-bandwidth memory exist.

    Going deeper

    • Von Neumann's 1945 EDVAC report described five parts — arithmetic, control, memory, input and output — plus binary stored programs, a blueprint that almost every later general-purpose computer followed.
    • Because code is just data, programs can create other programs (compilers, JITs, loaders), but attackers can also inject code — which is why CPUs now mark data pages as non-executable (the NX bit).
    • Processor speed rose far faster than DRAM latency for decades (the 'memory wall'), so a modern core waits hundreds of cycles on a DRAM miss and depends on caches and prefetching to stay busy.
    • Separate instruction and data paths pay off in small chips: an AVR microcontroller fetches its next instruction from flash while the current one accesses SRAM, so most instructions complete in a single cycle.
    • Because L1i and L1d are separate, freshly written code (JIT output, self-modifying code) needs cache-maintenance or synchronisation steps before it runs — explicit instructions on Arm, mostly automatic on x86.
    • Alternatives to the von Neumann model — dataflow machines, systolic arrays as in Google's TPUs, neuromorphic chips and processing-in-memory — all aim to cut the cost of moving data to a central processor.

    Common mistakes & misconceptions

    • Calling modern PCs 'Harvard' because they have split L1 caches: main memory is still shared, so they are modified Harvard machines.
    • Believing a faster clock fixes the von Neumann bottleneck: if data cannot arrive faster, a faster core simply waits longer.
    • Confusing address-bus width with data-bus width: the first limits how much memory can be addressed, the second how much moves per transfer.

    1.11 How a Program Runs: From Source Code to Machine Code¶

    Programs are written in human-readable languages like C or Python, but the CPU only understands machine code — numbers that encode simple instructions such as 'add these two registers'. Tools translate one into the other: a compiler converts the whole program ahead of time, while an interpreter translates and runs it piece by piece. The operating system then loads the program into memory and the CPU runs it with an endless fetch-decode-execute loop.

    Think of it like…

    Compiling is like translating a whole novel before it is published; interpreting is like a live interpreter translating a speech sentence by sentence as it is spoken.

    Real-world example

    Ravi writes hello.c and runs gcc hello.c -o hello. The preprocessor pulls in stdio.h, the compiler turns C into x86-64 assembly, the assembler turns that into machine code in an object file, and the linker joins it with the C library to make an executable. When he types ./hello, the operating system's loader maps the file into memory, sets up a stack and jumps to the start, and the CPU starts fetching instructions. His friend's Python script takes a different route: CPython compiles it to bytecode and an interpreter loop runs that bytecode.

    How it works¶

    1. Write source code in a high-level language such as C, Rust, Java or Python.
    2. Compile it: translate to assembly or straight to machine code (or to bytecode for a virtual machine).
    3. Assemble and link: turn assembly into object files and join them with libraries into one executable.
    4. Load: the operating system maps the executable into memory, creates a process and jumps to its entry point.
    5. Execute: the CPU repeats fetch (get the instruction at the program counter), decode (work out what it means), execute (do it) and write back the result.

    Who uses it

    Software developers, compiler and toolchain engineers, reverse engineers, malware analysts and performance engineers all need to know what happens between source code and silicon.

    Key takeaway

    Use Compiler Explorer (godbolt.org) or gcc -S to see the assembly your code becomes — it is the quickest way to understand what the CPU really does.

    Going deeper

    • Modern compilers have three layers: a front end parses the language into an intermediate representation (LLVM IR, GCC GIMPLE), a middle end optimises it and a back end emits code for each ISA — one optimiser serves many languages and chips.
    • Optimisation levels trade build time and debuggability for speed: -O0 keeps code close to the source for debugging, -O2 is the usual release setting, and -O3 adds more aggressive inlining and vectorisation.
    • Dynamic linking (.so, .dll, .dylib files) lets programs share one library copy and receive security fixes without relinking; static linking makes self-contained binaries, popular for containers and Go programs.
    • Before main() runs, start-up code (_start, from crt files) sets up the stack, arguments and environment, initialises the C library and runs constructors; returning from main calls exit(), which flushes output buffers.
    • Address space layout randomisation (ASLR) makes the loader place code, stack, heap and libraries at random addresses on each run, so attackers cannot rely on fixed locations; position-independent executables (PIE) make it possible.
    • Most runtimes are hybrids: Java and .NET start by interpreting or quickly compiling and JIT-compile hot methods, Android's ART mixes ahead-of-time and JIT compilation, and CPython 3.13 added an experimental JIT.

    Common mistakes & misconceptions

    • Saying Python is 'not compiled': CPython compiles source to bytecode first, and it is the bytecode that is interpreted.
    • Shipping binaries built with -march=native: they can crash with 'illegal instruction' on older CPUs that lack the same extensions.
    • Confusing compiler errors with linker errors: 'undefined reference' comes from the linker and usually means a missing library or object file, not bad syntax.
    • Timing debug (-O0) builds and drawing performance conclusions; always measure optimised release builds.

    1.12 Measuring Performance: Clock Speed, IPC, FLOPS and Amdahl's Law¶

    'Fast' can mean two things: how long one task takes (latency) or how many tasks are finished per second (throughput). A processor's speed on a program depends on how many instructions it needs, how many clock cycles each takes on average, and how fast the clock ticks. Laws such as Amdahl's law show that adding more processors only helps as much as the work can be split up.

    Think of it like…

    Latency is how long one pizza takes to bake; throughput is how many pizzas the shop sells per hour — a bigger oven raises throughput without baking any single pizza faster.

    Real-world example

    Mei's video-render script takes 100 seconds: 90 seconds of work can run in parallel, but 10 seconds must run on one core. On 10 cores, Amdahl's law predicts 10 + 90 ÷ 10 = 19 seconds, about a 5.3× speed-up — and even with unlimited cores it can never beat 10 seconds (10×). Her teammate points out Gustafson's view: with 10 cores they could render a scene about ten times bigger in roughly the same time. Mei also realises that halving the instructions in the serial part would help as much as a faster clock.

    How it works¶

    1. Clock rate is cycles per second (GHz); one cycle at 4 GHz lasts 0.25 ns.
    2. CPI (cycles per instruction), or its inverse IPC, shows how much work each cycle does.
    3. CPU time = instruction count × CPI ÷ clock rate — improving any of the three makes a program faster.
    4. Latency measures time per task, throughput measures tasks per second, and bandwidth measures data moved per second.
    5. Amdahl's law: speed-up = 1 ÷ ((1 − p) + p ÷ n), where p is the parallel fraction and n the number of processors.
    6. Benchmarks such as SPEC CPU, Geekbench and HPL give comparable scores on standard workloads.

    Who uses it

    Performance engineers, HPC specialists, game developers, cloud architects sizing servers and anyone buying hardware rely on these measures.

    Key takeaway

    Clock speed alone does not decide performance — think instruction count × CPI ÷ clock rate, and apply Amdahl's law before parallelising anything.

    Going deeper

    • The 'iron law' splits CPU time into instruction count (set by algorithm, compiler and ISA), CPI (set by microarchitecture and memory behaviour) and cycle time (set by circuit design and process) — every optimisation targets one factor.
    • Wall-clock (elapsed) time includes waiting for disks, networks and other programs, while CPU time counts only cycles spent running your code; users feel wall-clock time, so report both.
    • Peak FLOPS = cores × clock × FLOPs per cycle per core: a core with two 512-bit FMA units performs 2 units × 16 FP32 lanes × 2 operations = 64 FP32 FLOPs per cycle, which real code rarely sustains.
    • Supercomputer lists report Rpeak (theoretical) and Rmax (achieved on HPL); large GPU systems typically reach roughly 60-80% of peak on HPL, and real applications usually achieve much less.
    • Benchmark suites summarise many tests with a geometric mean of speed ratios (as SPEC does) so no single test dominates; for interactive services, tail-latency percentiles such as p99 matter more than averages.
    • SPEC CPU 2026, released in May 2026 with 52 benchmarks in four suites, succeeds SPEC CPU 2017 and adds an optional energy metric, so expect vendors to quote both suites for a while.
    • Gustafson's scaled speed-up, (1 − p) + p × n, explains why machines with millions of cores are useful: scientists grow the problem size with the machine instead of shrinking the run time.

    Common mistakes & misconceptions

    • Comparing CPUs by GHz alone: a 3.5 GHz chip with higher IPC can beat a 5 GHz chip, so compare benchmarks on your own workload.
    • Reporting only average latency: a service averaging 20 ms may still make 1 in 100 users wait seconds, so track percentiles too.
    • Ignoring Amdahl's law: buying four times the cores rarely gives four times the speed when part of the job is serial or I/O-bound.
    • Mixing up throughput and latency: adding servers raises requests per second but does not make any single request faster.

    1.13 Moore's Law, Dennard Scaling and Process Nodes¶

    In 1965 Gordon Moore noticed that the number of transistors on a chip was doubling at a steady pace — later settled at about every two years — and this 'Moore's law' held for decades. For a long time, smaller transistors were also faster and more power-efficient (Dennard scaling), but that bonus ended in the mid-2000s, so clock speeds stopped climbing and chips gained more cores instead. Chips are made in factories called fabs using generations of technology called process nodes, such as '3 nm' or '2 nm' — names that are now marketing labels rather than real measurements.

    Think of it like…

    Moore's law is like fitting ever more houses into the same town by making each one smaller; Dennard scaling was the bonus that smaller houses also needed less heating — until the walls got so thin that heat leaked everywhere.

    Real-world example

    Students at a fictional college, Westbrook Tech, line up three chips: the Intel 4004 from 1971 with about 2,300 transistors, a 2005 dual-core desktop processor with a few hundred million, and a 2024 AI GPU with over 200 billion. That is roughly a 100-million-fold increase in about 50 years. They also notice clock speeds climbed from 740 kHz to around 3-4 GHz by the mid-2000s and then barely moved — the point where Dennard scaling broke down.

    How it works¶

    1. Fabs print transistors onto silicon wafers using lithography: light projects circuit patterns onto light-sensitive coatings.
    2. Shrinking features fits more transistors per square millimetre, lowering the cost of each transistor.
    3. Under Dennard scaling, smaller transistors used proportionally less power, so clocks could rise without overheating.
    4. Around 2005-2006 leakage current stopped voltages falling, so heat — the 'power wall' — became the main limit.
    5. The industry responded with multicore chips, specialised accelerators, 3D transistor shapes (FinFET, then gate-all-around) and chiplets.
    6. In 2026 the leading foundries (TSMC, Samsung, Intel) are shipping 2 nm-class nodes, and backside power delivery is arriving.

    Who uses it

    Semiconductor process engineers, chip designers, hardware analysts and anyone choosing or buying processors should understand nodes and scaling trends.

    Key takeaway

    A '2 nm' node contains no feature that is literally 2 nm wide — compare nodes by transistor density, performance and power, not by their names.

    Going deeper

    • Moore's 1965 article predicted the number of components per chip would double every year; in 1975 he revised it to about every two years, a pace the industry roughly kept until the 2010s before slowing.
    • Dennard scaling ended because supply voltage stopped falling: near and below about 1 V, threshold-voltage limits and leakage current meant smaller transistors kept wasting power even when idle.
    • Transistor shapes changed to keep leakage under control: planar devices until about 22 nm, FinFETs (Intel 2011-12, TSMC 16 nm in 2015), gate-all-around nanosheets (Samsung 2022, TSMC N2 and Intel 18A from 2025), with stacked CFETs next on roadmaps.
    • Leading-edge fabs cost tens of billions of dollars, so only TSMC, Samsung and Intel compete at the frontier; Japan's Rapidus is aiming for 2 nm-class mass production around 2027.
    • SRAM cells have shrunk much more slowly than logic at recent nodes, one reason designers move large caches onto separate stacked dies, as in AMD's 3D V-Cache.
    • Cost per transistor stopped falling quickly after roughly the 28 nm era, so 'More than Moore' approaches — chiplets, advanced packaging and specialised accelerators — now deliver much of the progress.

    Common mistakes & misconceptions

    • Reading 'Moore's law is dead' as 'no more progress': density still rises, just more slowly and at a higher cost per wafer.
    • Comparing node names across foundries: Intel 18A, TSMC N2 and Samsung SF2 differ in density and power, so compare published data instead.
    • Confusing Moore's law with clock speed: transistor counts kept climbing after 2005 even though clock frequencies stalled.

    Million transistors per mm² (MTr/mm²) · Metric · Current · Intermediate    		 Transistors per square millimetre — a more honest way to compare process nodes than their names. Leading-edge logic processes pack well over 100 million transistors into each square millimetre.
    More than Moore: chiplets and 3D packaging
    e.g. TSMC CoWoS, Intel Foveros, UCIe interconnect standard · Technology · Current · Intermediate    		 Keeping progress going as shrinking slows by joining several smaller dies side by side or stacked in one package; detailed in the processors chapter. NVIDIA's B200 joins two large GPU dies in one package, and AMD EPYC CPUs combine many CPU chiplets with an I/O die.
    Silicon wafer (300 mm)
    Wafer makers such as Shin-Etsu, SUMCO and GlobalWafers · Component · Current · Beginner    		 A thin, polished disc of ultra-pure crystalline silicon on which hundreds of chips are made at once; 300 mm is the leading-edge size. One 300 mm wafer of a mid-sized phone chip can hold several hundred dies, which are tested, cut apart and packaged.
    CFET (complementary FET)
    Research roadmaps from imec, Intel, TSMC and Samsung · Technology · Emerging · Advanced    		 A future transistor that stacks the n-type and p-type devices of a CMOS pair on top of each other, roughly halving logic-cell area. Research groups have demonstrated stacked CFET inverters, with production expected only after today's 2 nm and 1.4 nm-class generations.

    1.14 Orders of Magnitude: Prefixes and Latency Numbers¶

    Computing covers enormous ranges: one CPU step takes a fraction of a nanosecond, while a message to another continent takes hundreds of millions of times longer. Engineers use prefixes such as nano, micro, milli, kilo, mega and giga to talk about these scales, and memorise rough 'latency numbers' so they can quickly judge whether a design will be fast. Getting the order of magnitude (the power of ten) right matters far more than being exact.

    Think of it like…

    If one CPU cycle (about 0.3 ns) were stretched to one second, reading main memory would take about five minutes, an SSD read about four days, and a packet to another continent about fifteen years.

    Real-world example

    Jonas, a junior developer at a fictional delivery app called Parcelpath, must decide where to keep user sessions. Main memory answers in about 100 ns, a local NVMe SSD in roughly 100 µs (1,000× slower), and a database in another region in about 50-150 ms (another 1,000× slower). Each page load needs 20 session reads, so the cross-region design would add up to around two seconds, while an in-memory cache adds only microseconds. A quick back-of-the-envelope estimate settles the debate before anyone writes code.

    How it works¶

    1. Learn the SI prefixes in steps of 1000: nano (10^-9), micro (10^-6), milli (10^-3), kilo (10^3), mega (10^6), giga (10^9), tera, peta, exa, zetta.
    2. Memorise rough latencies: cache ~1-10 ns, RAM ~100 ns, SSD ~10-100 µs, hard disk ~5-10 ms, intercontinental ~100-150 ms or more.
    3. Multiply counts by costs for a back-of-the-envelope estimate (number of operations × time per operation).
    4. Compare options by ratio: a 1000× gap always matters, a 10% gap rarely does at the design stage.
    5. Measure afterwards to confirm, because real hardware varies by generation and workload.

    Who uses it

    Software and systems engineers, site reliability engineers, cloud architects and candidates in system-design interviews use these numbers all the time.

    Key takeaway

    In system-design interviews, say approximate latencies and do quick estimates out loud — interviewers care about the right order of magnitude, not exact figures.

    Going deeper

    • Powers of two approximate powers of ten: 2^10 ≈ 10^3, so 2^20 is about a million, 2^30 about a billion and 2^40 about a trillion — handy for quick memory and address-space estimates.
    • A day has 86,400 seconds (≈ 10^5) and a year about 31.5 million (≈ π × 10^7); dividing a daily total by 10^5 gives a quick average per-second rate.
    • Little's law (items in the system = arrival rate × time each spends there) turns latency into capacity: 1,000 requests per second taking 50 ms each means about 50 requests in flight at once.
    • Bandwidth and latency are independent: a van full of hard drives has huge bandwidth but terrible latency, and the bandwidth-delay product tells you how much data must be in flight to keep a link busy.
    • Since the classic latency list was written, DRAM latency has barely improved, SSDs have become roughly 100× faster than disks, and RDMA networks have cut in-data-centre messages to a few microseconds.
    • Energy follows orders of magnitude too: fetching 64 bits from DRAM costs about a hundred times or more the energy of a 64-bit floating-point operation, so data movement dominates chip power budgets.

    Common mistakes & misconceptions

    • Mixing ms, µs and ns in one estimate: convert everything to a single unit first, because a 1,000× slip reverses conclusions.
    • Assuming network calls are free because the LAN is fast: half a millisecond per call adds up when a page makes hundreds of sequential calls.
    • Treating memorised latency numbers as exact: they shift with each hardware generation, so use them for orders of magnitude and measure real systems.

    Try it: Turn nanoseconds into human time¶

    latency_ns = {
    'L1 cache hit': 1,
    'Main memory (DRAM)': 100,
    'NVMe SSD random read': 100_000,
    'Hard disk seek': 5_000_000,
    'Packet to another continent': 150_000_000,
}
# pretend one 0.3 ns CPU cycle lasts 1 second
for name, ns in latency_ns.items():
    secs = ns / 0.3
    print(f'{name:30} {secs:>14,.0f} s  (~{secs / 86400:,.1f} days)')

    Popularised by Jeff Dean and Peter Norvig · Resource · Current · Intermediate    		 A list of approximate times for common operations, from a cache hit to a cross-continent round trip, used for quick estimates. Engineers recall that RAM is about 100× slower than L1 cache and about 1,000× faster than an SSD read.
    Cache latency (L1, L2, L3)
    Varies by CPU generation · Metric · Current · Intermediate    		 Roughly 1 ns for L1, a few nanoseconds for L2 and about 10-20 ns for L3 on modern processors. A loop over a small array that fits in L1 runs many times faster than one scanning gigabytes of RAM.
    DRAM access latency
    Varies by memory type and platform · Metric · Current · Intermediate    		 About 80-120 ns for the CPU to fetch from main memory — hundreds of CPU clock cycles. A cache miss to DRAM on a 4 GHz core costs around 400 cycles, during which that work may stall.
    SSD and hard-disk latency
    Varies by device · Metric · Current · Beginner    		 NVMe SSD reads take tens of microseconds to about a tenth of a millisecond; hard-disk seeks take several milliseconds. Swapping a boot hard disk for an SSD turns a one-minute start-up into seconds because thousands of small reads become about 100× faster.
    Network round-trip time (RTT)
    Measured with ping and similar tools · Metric · Current · Beginner    		 The time for a packet to reach a destination and come back: well under 1 ms inside a data centre, roughly 70-300 ms between continents. A ping from London to New York typically shows around 70-80 ms.
    Speed of light in fibre
    Physics of optical fibre · Concept · Current · Beginner    		 Light in glass fibre travels at about 200,000 km per second, roughly two-thirds of its speed in a vacuum — a hard limit on latency. A 6,000 km fibre route adds at least 30 ms each way, however fast the routers are.
    Throughput scales (Gbit/s, GB/s, TB/s)
    General performance units · Metric · Current · Beginner    		 Data rates range from megabits per second on mobile links to terabytes per second inside AI accelerators. Home broadband might give 1 Gbit/s, a fast NVMe SSD over 10 GB/s, and an AI GPU's HBM memory several TB/s.
    Back-of-the-envelope estimation
    Engineering practice · Concept · Current · Beginner    		 Quickly multiplying rough counts by rough costs to judge whether something is feasible before building it. 1 million users × 10 requests a day ≈ 116 requests per second on average — easy for one modest server.
    Logarithmic scale
    Mathematics · Concept · Current · Beginner    		 A scale where each step is a multiple (such as ×10), making huge ranges readable on one chart. Moore's law is plotted on a log scale, so 50 years of doubling appear as a nearly straight line.
    Exascale
    10^18 FLOPS; first reached on the TOP500 by Frontier (2022) · Metric · Current · Beginner    		 Computing at 10^18 — a billion billion — floating-point operations per second. An exascale machine does in one second what a person doing one sum per second would need about 31.7 billion years to finish.
    Powers-of-two rule (2^10 ≈ 10^3)
    Engineering rule of thumb · Concept · Current · Beginner    		 Because 1,024 is close to 1,000, every extra 10 bits multiplies a count by about a thousand, making binary sizes easy to estimate. A 48-bit address space is 2^8 × 2^40, about 256 trillion bytes (256 TiB), worked out without a calculator.
    Bandwidth-delay product
    Networking and performance engineering · Concept · Current · Intermediate    		 Link speed multiplied by round-trip time: the amount of data that must be in flight to keep a connection fully busy. A 10 Gbit/s link with an 80 ms round trip needs about 100 MB in flight, so TCP windows must grow very large.

    1.15 A Short History of Computing: From Babbage to Exascale¶

    Modern computers are the result of about two centuries of ideas and inventions, from mechanical calculating engines to chips with hundreds of billions of transistors. Each step — theory, vacuum tubes, transistors, integrated circuits, microprocessors, personal computers, smartphones and AI accelerators — made computing smaller, cheaper and more powerful. Knowing the timeline helps you understand why today's machines look the way they do.

    Think of it like…

    Computing history is like a relay race: Babbage and Lovelace drew the map, Turing and von Neumann wrote the rules, and each generation of engineers took the baton and ran faster with smaller, cheaper parts.

    Real-world example

    For a history poster, Aisha picks five milestones: ENIAC (1945), a room-sized machine with about 18,000 vacuum tubes drawing roughly 150 kW; the Intel 4004 (1971), a whole CPU on one chip with about 2,300 transistors; the IBM PC (1981), which made the personal computer an everyday business tool; the iPhone (2007), which put a powerful computer in millions of pockets; and Frontier (2022), the first officially ranked exascale supercomputer. In under 80 years computers went from filling a room to fitting in a palm, while becoming trillions of times faster.

    How it works¶

    1. 1800s: mechanical ideas — Babbage designs the Analytical Engine and Ada Lovelace publishes an algorithm for it.
    2. 1930s-40s: theory and electronics — Turing's universal machine (1936), Shannon's switching circuits, Colossus, ENIAC and the first stored-program computers.
    3. 1947-1971: the transistor, the integrated circuit and the first microprocessor (Intel 4004) shrink computers dramatically.
    4. 1970s-1990s: personal computers (Apple II, IBM PC), Arm's low-power RISC chips and the first GPUs.
    5. 2000s: multicore processors, smartphones (iPhone 2007, Android 2008) and cloud computing.
    6. 2010s-2020s: GPUs power deep learning, exascale supercomputers arrive (Frontier, 2022) and generative AI goes mainstream.

    Who uses it

    Students, teachers, technology journalists, and anyone asked in an interview or exam to explain how computing evolved will find this timeline useful.

    Key takeaway

    Memorise a handful of anchor dates: 1936 Turing, 1945 ENIAC, 1947 transistor, 1971 Intel 4004, 1981 IBM PC, 2007 iPhone, 2022 Frontier exascale.

    Going deeper

    • Computer generations are often grouped by switching technology: vacuum tubes (1940s-50s), discrete transistors (late 1950s-60s), integrated circuits (1960s-70s) and microprocessors (1970s onwards).
    • Colossus stayed secret until the 1970s, which is partly why ENIAC was long called the first electronic computer; the Manchester Baby of 1948 was the first to run a program stored in its own memory.
    • IBM's System/360 (1964) offered one instruction set across a family of compatible machines of different speeds — separating architecture from implementation, the idea that x86 and Arm still follow.
    • Software milestones matter as much as hardware: FORTRAN (1957) made compilers practical, UNIX and C (early 1970s) made operating systems portable, and Linux (1991) now runs most servers and every TOP500 supercomputer.
    • Clock speeds rose roughly a thousandfold from the 1970s to the mid-2000s and then flattened around 3-5 GHz; since then gains have come from more and wider cores, accelerators and smaller number formats.
    • On the June 2026 TOP500 list China's LineShine took first place at about 2.2 exaFLOPS, ending El Capitan's run at the top and becoming the fifth exascale system on the list.

    Common mistakes & misconceptions

    • Calling ENIAC 'the first computer' without qualification: Colossus, the Atanasoff-Berry Computer and Zuse's Z3 came earlier in different forms, so precise claims need qualifiers.
    • Assuming Ada Lovelace built hardware: she analysed and wrote programs for Babbage's Analytical Engine, which was never completed.
    • Thinking history is irrelevant to the job: x86's 1978 roots explain its variable-length instructions and legacy modes that engineers still handle today.

    Try it: Compare then and now with quick arithmetic¶

    eniac_adds_per_s = 5_000      # ENIAC (1945), about 5,000 additions per second
frontier_flops = 1.1e18       # Frontier HPL result (2022)
print(f'{frontier_flops / eniac_adds_per_s:.1e}x faster')    # ~2.2e+14
print(f'{208e9 / 2300:,.0f}x more transistors (4004 -> B200)')
