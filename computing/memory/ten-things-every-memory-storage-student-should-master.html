    Ten Things Every Memory & Storage Student Should Master¶

    If you remember nothing else from this guide, remember these ten ideas. Together they explain how computers keep data fast, safe and affordable — and what employers will expect you to know.

    1. Memory is fast and forgetful; storage is slow and faithful¶

    The hierarchy runs from SRAM caches (about 1 ns) through DRAM (about 100 ns), SSDs (about 100 µs) and hard drives (about 10 ms) to tape, because no single medium is fast, huge, cheap and persistent at once.

    2. Know your units and your three performance numbers¶

    Vendors count in decimal (TB) while many operating systems count in binary (TiB), and every workload is described by latency, IOPS and throughput — linked by block size and queue depth. Always ask for latency percentiles, not just averages.

    3. DRAM and HBM feed the processors¶

    DRAM stores bits as charge that must be refreshed, and DDR5, LPDDR, GDDR and HBM trade capacity, power and bandwidth differently. AI has made stacked HBM the most strategic — and most supply-constrained — memory of the decade.

    4. Flash is fast, but it wears out and needs a translator¶

    NAND erases in large blocks and endures limited program/erase cycles, so the flash translation layer, garbage collection, wear levelling, over-provisioning and TRIM decide an SSD's real speed and life. Check TBW or DWPD, not only the headline MB/s.

    5. Hard drives and tape still hold most of the world's bytes¶

    High-capacity HDDs and LTO tape remain the cheapest way to keep petabytes, and a tape on a shelf is an air-gapped copy that ransomware cannot reach. Every medium needs planned migration before it becomes unreadable.

    6. Interfaces and the software stack shape latency¶

    NVMe's many deep queues replaced the single-queue SATA/AHCI model, NVMe-oF and Fibre Channel extend storage across networks, and CXL extends memory itself. Every layer from the application to the device adds microseconds that add up.

    7. RAID, replication and snapshots are not backups¶

    They protect against hardware failure, but deletion, corruption and ransomware hit every live copy instantly. Only independent, immutable, regularly tested backups — the 3-2-1-1-0 rule with clear RPO and RTO targets — let you truly recover.

    8. Choose block, file or object on purpose¶

    Databases and VM disks want block storage, shared folders want NFS or SMB, and huge write-once datasets belong in S3-style object storage. Matching the model, protocol and storage class to the workload is the core skill of storage design.

    9. Measure before you change anything¶

    Use fio with direct I/O and preconditioning, STREAM for memory bandwidth, and iostat, PerfMon and the USE method for troubleshooting. Numbers gathered before and after a change are what turn opinions into engineering.

    10. Stay curious — the stack is changing fast¶

    CXL memory tiering, new non-volatile memories, computational storage, AI storage for checkpoints and KV cache, and sharp price cycles are reshaping the field. Home labs, certifications and primary sources keep your knowledge current.
