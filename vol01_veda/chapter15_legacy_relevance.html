    Chapter 15: The Legacy and Modern Relevance of the Vedas¶

    Invocatory Verse

    ā no bhadrāḥ kratavo yantu viśvato
    ’dabdhāso aparītāsa udbhidaḥ
    May auspicious intentions come to us from every side,
    undeceived, unobstructed, breaking through.
    — Ṛgveda 1.89.1

    15.1 Introduction¶

    This final chapter considers what became of the Vedas after the Vedic period: how later Indian traditions drew on them, argued with them or rejected them; how they were received in the modern world; and in what sense they remain relevant today. A responsible answer must avoid two temptations—treating the Vedas as a museum piece of no living importance, and claiming for them a scientific or moral authority that the evidence does not support.

    15.2 The Vedas and Later Indian Traditions¶

    For Brahmanical Hinduism the Vedas became the ultimate source of authority. The orthodox philosophical schools, the Dharmaśāstras, the epics and Purāṇas, and many Āgamic and devotional traditions claim to be based on the Veda or consistent with it. In practice, the relationship was often nominal: later Hinduism worships deities such as Śiva, Viṣṇu and the Goddess in forms barely present in the Vedas, and much of its ritual follows the Āgamas and Purāṇas rather than the śrauta texts. The Veda functioned less as a text that people knew than as a symbol of authority and orthodoxy.

    Other Indian traditions defined themselves partly against the Veda. Buddhists and Jains rejected its authority, the efficacy of animal sacrifice and the claim that birth confers religious status; the materialist Cārvākas mocked the priests; and many medieval devotional poets, such as Kabīr, dismissed Vedic learning in favour of devotion. Many regional and tribal traditions had little to do with the Veda at all. India’s intellectual history is therefore not simply a story of descent from the Vedas, but of a long conversation in which the Veda was one of the central voices. Two parts of that conversation, the early Buddhist and Jain critique and the Tamil reception of the Veda, are outlined below.

    15.2.1 The Buddhist and Jain Critique¶

    The earliest sustained criticism of Vedic learning, and of the claims made for birth, comes from the Buddhist and Jain canons, composed in the same eastern Ganga plain in which the late Vedic texts took shape (Section 14.3). The Pali discourses (suttas) make their case in dialogues with Brahmins, often men learned in the “three Vedas” (tevijja), and several of these dialogues are classics (Walshe 1995).

    In the Tevijja Sutta (Dīgha Nikāya 13) two young Brahmins, Vāseṭṭha and Bhāradvāja, dispute which of the paths taught by Brahmin teachers leads to union with Brahmā. The Buddha asks whether any Brahmin learned in the three Vedas, any teacher of theirs back to the seventh generation, or even the ancient seers who made the mantras, has seen Brahmā face to face. The seers are named—Aṭṭhaka, Vāmaka, Vāmadeva, Vessāmitta, Yamataggi, Aṅgīrasa, Bhāradvāja, Vāseṭṭha, Kassapa and Bhagu, that is, Viśvāmitra, Jamadagni, Vasiṣṭha, Kaśyapa, Bhṛgu and others of the families of Section 1.5. None has; their teaching is likened to a file of blind men, each holding on to the one in front, and to a man who builds a ladder at a crossroads to climb to a mansion he has never seen. The Buddha then teaches, as the true way to companionship with Brahmā, the cultivation of friendliness, compassion, sympathetic joy and equanimity. The argument does not reject the goal; it rejects the claim that recited texts give access to it.

    In the Ambaṭṭha Sutta (Dīgha Nikāya 3) a young Brahmin sent by his teacher Pokkhara­sāti insults the Sakyans as menials; the Buddha shows that Ambaṭṭha’s own clan descends from a slave woman of the Sakyan royal house, and teaches that it is knowledge and conduct (vijjācaraṇa), not birth or lineage, that make a person excellent. The Aggañña Sutta (Dīgha Nikāya 27), addressed again to Vāseṭṭha and Bhāradvāja, answers the Brahmins’ claim to be the true sons of Brahmā, born from his mouth, by observing that Brahmins are plainly born from Brahmin women like everyone else—a riposte to the image of the Puruṣa Sūkta (Section 2.5.3). It then tells a counter-myth of social origins, in which beings decline from a luminous state, property and theft appear, the people choose a ruler, the “Great Elect” (mahāsammata), and the four classes arise from occupations, not from a cosmic body. Steven Collins and Richard Gombrich have read the discourse as a sustained parody of Brahmanical cosmogony (Collins 1993; Gombrich 1988). The Kūṭadanta Sutta (Dīgha Nikāya 5) turns to sacrifice: a Brahmin preparing a great animal sacrifice is told of an ancient king whose sacrifice killed no animal and was completed with ghee, oil, butter, curds, honey and molasses, and of higher sacrifices still—gifts, refuge, the moral precepts and meditation—and he sets his animals free. A verse of the Vasala Sutta of the Sutta Nipāta (1.7) puts the principle in a line: na jaccā vasalo hoti, na jaccā hoti brāhmaṇo; kammunā vasalo hoti, kammunā hoti brāhmaṇo, “not by birth is one an outcaste, not by birth a Brahmin; by deeds one is an outcaste, by deeds a Brahmin” (Bhikkhu Bodhi 2017).

    The Jain canon makes the same points in its own idiom. In the Uttarādhyayana Sūtra the monk Hari­keśa, born among the Śvapākas, an outcaste group, comes for alms to a Brahmin sacrifice, is reviled and beaten, and teaches the Brahmins that the true sacrifice is the fire of austerity fed by right conduct (chapter 12); and in chapter 25 the monk Jayaghoṣa, at the sacrifice of the Brahmin Vijayaghoṣa, describes the true Brahmin as one who is free from passion and harms no living being, adding that one does not become a Brahmin by uttering Oṃ (Jacobi 1895).

    These texts do not simply reject the Veda. They take over its vocabulary—the true Brahmin, the true sacrifice, union with Brahmā—and give it an ethical meaning. That strategy, more than outright dismissal, was the most lasting part of the critique, and it has parallels within Brahmanical tradition itself, in the Upaniṣads’ internalised sacrifice (Section 6.6) and in the Bhagavadgītā’s definition of varṇa by “qualities and actions” (Section 14.4). The Buddhist and Jain traditions are treated in Volume 4, Buddha–Jina Prabhā.

    15.2.2 The Veda in Tamil Literature¶

    Tamil literature had its own poetic traditions, with conventions of its own, but it was never isolated from the Veda. The Sangam anthologies of the early centuries CE know Brahmins (antaṇar,pārppār) who tend the sacred fires and recite the “four Vedas” (nāṉmaṟai), and they celebrate kings for their Vedic sacrifices: a Pāṇṭiya king of the Puṟanāṉūṟu is remembered as Palyākacālai Mutukuṭumi Peruvaḻuti, “of the many sacrificial halls”, and a Cōḻa king as Rācacūyam Vēṭṭa Perunaṟkiḷḷi, “who performed the Rājasūya”. George Hart and Kamil Zvelebil have shown how Sanskrit and Tamil conventions met in this literature without either absorbing the other (Hart 1975; Zvelebil 1973). The Tirukkuṟaḷ, a work on ethics usually dated to about the fifth century CE, respects the Brahmin’s learning but values conduct above it: a Brahmin who forgets the Veda can learn it again, but one who fails in conduct is ruined (134); and in its chapter on abstaining from meat it declares that not to kill and eat a living creature is better than a thousand sacrifices with oblations poured into the fire (259). In the epic Cilappatikāram the marriage of Kōvalaṉ and Kaṇṇaki is solemnised by an aged Brahmin, the couple walking round the fire according to the Vedic rite.

    The devotional movements took the relationship further. The Śaiva poets of the Tēvāram praise Śiva as the substance and meaning of the Vedas, and the Śrīvaiṣṇava tradition gave the hymns of the Āḻvārs, collected by Nāthamuni in the tenth century as the Nālāyira Tivviyap Pirapantam (“Four Thousand Sacred Compositions”), the status of a Veda in Tamil. Nammāḻvār’s Tiruvāymoḻi was called the “Tamil Veda”, its author was praised as the one who made the Veda into Tamil, and from the twelfth century Śrīvaiṣṇava theologians commented on it alongside the Sanskrit Upaniṣads, a doctrine of “dual Vedānta” (ubhaya-vedānta); in temples such as Śrīraṅgam the Tamil hymns are recited in the liturgy beside the Sanskrit Veda (Carman and Narayanan 1989; Narayanan 1994). The claim was at once a tribute to the Veda’s prestige, since a text was exalted by being called a Veda, and a challenge to the Brahmin monopoly of revelation, since the Tamil hymns could be learnt and sung by people to whom the Sanskrit Veda was closed; Nammāḻvār himself was remembered as a man of the Vēḷāḷa cultivator community, not a Brahmin. Other Tamil voices were openly critical: the Siddhar poets mocked ritual and caste, and in the twentieth century the Self-Respect movement of E. V. Ramasamy (“Periyar”) attacked the authority of the Brahmanical texts. Tamil literature thus contains the whole range of responses to the Veda, from patronage and adaptation to appropriation and rejection.

    15.3 The Later History of the Vedic Gods¶

    The gods of the hymns did not vanish when the great theistic traditions of Viṣṇu, Śiva and the Goddess took shape. They were given new places in a reordered pantheon: some rose, some became guardians of the directions or heavenly officials, some were absorbed into other gods, and a few were all but forgotten. The epics and Purāṇas, treated in Volume 7, Itihāsa–Purāṇa, are the main witnesses to this change; E. Washburn Hopkins’s Epic Mythology remains the fullest survey of the epic forms of the Vedic gods (Hopkins 1915). Table 15.1 summarises the fates of the principal deities.

    15.3.1 The Gods Relativised¶

    The change began within the Vedic period. In the Brāhmaṇas, Prajāpati, the creator who is also the sacrifice and the year, stands above the older gods (Chapter 6). The early Upaniṣads go further. The Bṛhadāraṇyaka (1.4.10) says that one who worships a deity as other than himself, thinking “he is one, I am another”, does not understand: he is like a domestic animal to the gods, who would not like human beings to learn the truth. In the Kena Upaniṣad (sections 3–4) Agni cannot burn and Vāyu cannot blow away a single blade of grass placed before them by a mysterious being, and it is the goddess Umā Haimavatī who tells Indra that the being was brahman. The gods remain real, but they are no longer ultimate. Buddhists and Jains took a similar view from their own premises: the gods are beings in the cycle of rebirth, enjoying a long and pleasant life as the reward of past deeds, and in need of liberation like everyone else.

    15.3.2 Indra¶

    Indra, the most praised god of the Ṛgveda, had the most varied later history. Already in the Taittirīya Saṃhitā (2.5.1) he carries the guilt of killing Viśvarūpa, the three-headed son of Tvaṣṭṛ, who was a Brahmin, and the epic develops the theme: in the Udyogaparvan of the Mahābhārata, Indra hides in the waters after killing Vṛtra, and the mortal king Nahuṣa rules heaven in his place until his arrogance brings his fall. The Rāmāyaṇa tells of his seduction of Ahalyā (Volume 7). In the Purāṇas Indra becomes an office rather than a person: each age of Manu (manvantara) has its own Indra, and the Viṣṇu Purāṇa names the Indra of the present age Purandara, “breaker of forts”, a Ṛgvedic epithet of the god. A well-known story in the Brahmavaivarta Purāṇa, made familiar to Western readers by Heinrich Zimmer, shows Indra humbled by the sight of a procession of ants, each of which had once been an Indra (Zimmer 1946). In the Govardhana episode of the Kṛṣṇa cycle (Harivamśa; Viṣṇu and Bhāgavata Purāṇas; Volume 7), Kṛṣṇa diverts the cowherds’ festival from Indra to the mountain and shelters them from Indra’s storm. The story turns the Vedic rain-god into the rival, and finally the devotee, of the supreme god.

    Indra nevertheless remained a living figure. He is the guardian of the east among the eight guardians of the directions (dikpāla), riding the elephant Airāvata, and he appears in that role on temples across India (Volume 16, Vāstu–Śilpa). Festivals of Indra’s banner (indradhvaja) are described in the Sanskrit manuals; the Tamil epic Cilappatikāram devotes a canto to the festival of Indra at the Cōḻa port of Pukār; the Tolkāppiyam assigns Vēntaṉ, identified by its commentators with Indra, to the agricultural landscape (marutam); and the Indra Jātrā of Kathmandu, with the raising of a great pole, is still one of the main festivals of the Newars of the Kathmandu Valley. Buddhists made him Śakra (Pāli Sakka), king of the gods of the Heaven of the Thirty-three and a devoted follower of the Buddha; for the Jains Śakra, the lord of the first heaven, honours the newborn Tīrthaṅkara with the bath on Mount Meru, and his hymn of praise (Śakrastava) is recited in Śvetāmbara worship (Volume 4, Buddha–Jina Prabhā).

    15.3.3 Varuṇa and Mitra¶

    Varuṇa, the sovereign guardian of ṛta who binds the wrongdoer and forgives the penitent (Section 2.3), was associated with the waters already in the Ṛgveda. In the later texts that association prevails. Varuṇa becomes the lord of the waters and of the ocean and the guardian of the west, holding the noose (pāśa) that recalls his Vedic fetters; but his moral sovereignty, the sense of a god who sees hidden wrongdoing and can forgive it, passed to other gods. Joel Brereton’s study of the Ṛgvedic Ādityas shows how closely Varuṇa and his companions were tied to the principles of social order, which may explain why they declined when that order came to be expressed through dharma rather than through gods (Brereton 1981). Mitra, Varuṇa’s partner, almost disappears as a god of his own; his name survives as one of the names of the sun, and the series of twelve names recited with the Hindu sun-salutation begins with “Mitra”. His Iranian counterpart Mithra, by contrast, had a long career in Iranian religion.

    15.3.4 Other Gods¶

    Agni remained indispensable: every Hindu fire offering (homa) is still made through him, and he guards the south-east. The Mahābhārata makes him the father of the war-god Skanda and tells how he burned the Khāṇḍava forest with the help of Kṛṣṇa and Arjuna. Soma, whose plant was replaced by substitutes in the ritual (Chapter 8), had been identified with the moon already in the Brāhmaṇas; as the moon (Candra) he is the husband of the twenty-seven lunar asterisms, the daughters of Dakṣa, and the ancestor of the lunar dynasty of kings. Sūrya kept a cult of his own. Sun temples were built across India, as at Modhera in Gujarat (eleventh century) and Konārak in Odisha (thirteenth century); the Sāmba and Bhaviṣya Purāṇas tell how Sun-priests called Magas were brought from Śākadvīpa, a reflection of Iranian influence on the northern sun cult, whose images wear boots and a tunic. Prajāpati gave way to Brahmā, the creator, who is honoured in myth but has few temples. Bṛhaspati, the divine priest, became the teacher of the gods and the planet Jupiter, and his name was attached to the materialist school (Volume 3, Darśana Darpaṇa). The Aśvins became the physicians of the gods and, in the Mahābhārata, the fathers of Nakula and Sahadeva; Vāyu the father of Bhīma and of Hanumān; Yama the judge of the dead, with his scribe Citragupta, and the guardian of the south; and Tvaṣṭṛ, the divine craftsman, was largely merged with Viśvakarman, the patron of the craftsmen (Volume 17, Śilpa–Kāru). Sarasvatī, the river goddess already identified with Speech (Vāc) in the Brāhmaṇas, became the goddess of learning and the arts, honoured by Hindus, Jains and Buddhists, and carried by Buddhism as far as Japan. Uṣas, the Dawn, whose hymns are among the most beautiful in the Ṛgveda, has had almost no later cult.

    The two great beneficiaries of the change were Viṣṇu and Rudra. Viṣṇu’s three strides became the story of his dwarf incarnation (Vāmana), and Rudra’s double nature as fierce archer and healer, together with the Yajurvedic Śatarudrīya that is still chanted in Śiva temples, passed into the figure of Śiva (Section 2.3.5; Volume 6, Tantra–Āgama).

    15.3.5 Explaining the Change¶

    Several reasons for this transformation can be given, none sufficient by itself. The Vedic gods were the gods of a sacrificial cult, invoked for cattle, sons, victory and long life; the new religions of devotion looked for a supreme god who could grant grace and final liberation, and they found him in Viṣṇu or Śiva, who absorbed the myths and attributes of many older gods (Gonda 1970). The doctrine of karma and rebirth made the gods themselves into beings who hold their places by merit, which is why the epics and Purāṇas can make Indra an office and Nahuṣa his temporary successor. The decline of the śrauta ritual, the setting in which Indra and Varuṇa were most at home, reduced their occasions of worship. Yet “decline” is not the whole story. Wherever the śrauta rites are still performed, the gods are invoked in their Vedic roles with the Vedic mantras; the guardians of the directions are worshipped at the consecration of every temple and house; and in literature, festival and folk practice Indra and his companions have remained vivid figures for three thousand years.

    15.4 The Six Schools of Philosophy¶

    The six classical schools of philosophy (ṣaḍ darśana) are traditionally called āstika, “affirming” the authority of the Veda, but their actual relationship to it varies greatly. Pūrva Mīmāṃsā, whose root text is ascribed to Jaimini, interprets the ritual injunctions and defends the eternity and authorlessness of the Veda; Uttara Mīmāṃsā or Vedānta, ascribed to Bādarāyaṇa, interprets the Upaniṣads as the means of knowing Brahman. Nyāya (Gautama) accepts the Veda as reliable testimony composed by God; Vaiśeṣika (Kaṇāda) accepts its authority but makes little use of it; Sāṃkhya (Kapila) and Yoga (Patañjali) accept verbal testimony, but their doctrines owe little to the Vedas. Only the two Mīmāṃsās are, strictly speaking, schools of Vedic interpretation. The nāstika schools—Buddhist, Jain, Cārvāka and Ājīvika—rejected Vedic authority, and their debates with the orthodox schools shaped all of Indian philosophy. The schools are treated in Volume 3 of this series, Darśana Darpaṇa, and the Buddhist and Jain traditions in Volume 4, Buddha–Jina Prabhā.

    15.5 Modern Interpreters¶

    The nineteenth and twentieth centuries saw new Indian readings of the Vedas, often in response to colonial scholarship and to Christian missionary criticism. The responses of Dayānanda Sarasvatī, B. G. Tilak and Sri Aurobindo, and the critical editions produced by Indian scholars, are described in Section 1.11. Dayānanda’s call for a “return to the Vedas” also carried a programme of reform: the Ārya Samāj rejected image worship and hereditary caste and promoted the education of women, although its claim that the Saṃhitās contain modern scientific knowledge is not supported by historical or philological evidence. Other influential figures include:

    1. Svāmī Vivekānanda (1863–1902), who presented Vedānta to the world, beginning at the World’s Parliament of Religions in Chicago (1893);

    2. Madhusūdan Ojhā (1866–1939), a Jaipur scholar who wrote extensively on Vedic thought in Sanskrit;

    3. B. R. Ambedkar (1891–1956), who subjected the Vedic and Dharmaśāstra texts to a searching critique as sources of caste inequality (Section 14.4).

    15.6 UNESCO Recognition of Vedic Chanting¶

    In 2003 UNESCO proclaimed the tradition of Vedic chanting a Masterpiece of the Oral and Intangible Heritage of Humanity, and in 2008 it was inscribed on the Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity. The recognition acknowledges the antiquity and continuity of the tradition, the fidelity of its oral transmission, the sophistication of its recitation techniques, and the endangered status of several of its schools.

    15.7 The Vedas and Science: Achievements and Myths¶

    The relationship between the Vedas and science is often discussed, and often distorted. The genuine achievements of the Vedic sciences are considerable and need no exaggeration:

    • Linguistics: the phonetic analysis of the Prātiśākhyas and Pāṇini’s grammar are among the high points of linguistic science in any culture. Modern linguists and computer scientists have found Pāṇini’s rule-system illuminating for the theory of formal grammars—a recognition of its rigour, not evidence that Sanskrit is uniquely suited to computers, a popular myth with no basis.

    • Mathematics: the Śulba Sūtras contain an early general statement of the “Pythagorean” theorem, an accurate approximation of \(\sqrt{2}\) and methods of geometric construction; Piṅgala’s prosody contains early combinatorics (Chapter 7).

    • Calendrical astronomy: the Vedāṅga Jyotiṣa gives a coherent five-year luni-solar calendar, reconciling the movements of sun and moon by intercalation (see Volume 12, Khagola Jyotiṣa).

    Other popular claims do not stand up to examination. The Vedas do not anticipate the Big Bang, quantum physics, aircraft or modern medicine; the Nāsadīya Sūkta is a profound speculative poem, not a cosmological theory. The so-called “Vedic mathematics” popularised since 1965 is a set of mental-arithmetic techniques devised by Bhāratī Kṛṣṇa Tīrtha in the twentieth century; its sixteen “sūtras” are not found in any Vedic text. Such claims do the Vedas no honour: they obscure the real achievements described above and invite justified scepticism.

    15.8 Living Traditions¶

    The Vedas remain living texts in several ways:

    1. Recitation: Vedic schools (pāṭhaśālās) across India continue to train reciters, and Vedic chanting is widely heard in homes, temples and on public occasions.

    2. Ritual: Vedic mantras are used in the domestic life-cycle rites (saṃskāras), especially initiation, marriage and funerals, and in daily worship such as the sandhyā with the Gāyatrī. A small number of families still perform śrauta rituals.

    3. Philosophy: the Upaniṣads, through Vedānta, remain central to Hindu thought, and modern teachers have drawn on them in presenting yoga and meditation, although modern yoga has many other sources as well.

    4. Literature and identity: the Vedas are a powerful symbol of Indian heritage, invoked in politics and education as well as religion—a use that brings both opportunities and the risk of distortion.

    15.9 The Vedas and the Indian Knowledge Systems Initiative¶

    Recent Indian education policy, including the National Education Policy 2020, encourages the study of Indian Knowledge Systems in schools and universities. This creates opportunities: to support endangered oral traditions, to train scholars who can read Vedic texts in the original, and to bring traditional experts and academic researchers into dialogue. It also brings responsibilities. The study of the Vedas in modern institutions should meet the same standards of evidence as any other field: it should rest on critical editions and sound philology, distinguish tradition from history, avoid unfounded claims of scientific priority, and give an honest account of difficult subjects such as caste and gender.

    15.10 Challenges and the Way Forward¶

    1. Declining numbers of reciters: the number of fully trained reciters, especially in rare schools such as the Jaiminīya, Maitrāyaṇī and Paippalāda, is small.

    2. Economic precarity: traditional reciters and teachers often face economic hardship, which discourages new entrants.

    3. Critical editions and translations: several Vedic texts still lack reliable critical editions or modern translations, especially in Indian languages.

    4. Access: the restriction of Vedic learning to particular castes and to men is being questioned within the tradition; how to widen access while respecting living lineages is an open question.

    5. Bridging communities: traditional scholars and academic Indologists still too rarely work together.

    Promising responses include systematic recording of all surviving recitation traditions, support for pāṭhaśālās and their teachers, collaborative editing projects, and the inclusion of rigorous Vedic studies in university curricula.

    15.11 Conclusion¶

    Why do the Vedas matter? They matter historically, as the oldest literature of South Asia and as a source for the language, religion and society of the second and first millennia BCE. They matter intellectually, for the questions their poets and philosophers asked about the origin of the world, the self and the power of speech, and for the sciences of language, metre, geometry and calendar that grew up around them. They matter culturally, as texts still recited and revered by millions, and preserved by one of the most remarkable oral traditions in human history. And they matter ethically, because the social order they helped to sanctify, with its hierarchy of birth and its restrictions on women, has shaped Indian society for better and worse, and is still being argued over. To study the Vedas well is to hold all of these together.

    Final Invocation: The Śānti Mantra

    dyauḥ śāntir antarikṣaṃ śāntiḥ
    pṛthivī śāntir āpaḥ śāntir oṣadhayaḥ śāntiḥ
    vanaspatayaḥ śāntir viśve devāḥ śāntir brahma śāntiḥ
    sarvaṃ śāntiḥ śāntir eva śāntiḥ sā mā śāntir edhi
    Peace in the sky, peace in the intermediate space,
    peace on earth, peace in the waters, peace in the plants,
    peace in the trees, peace in all the gods, peace in Brahman,
    peace in everything; peace itself is peace—may that peace be mine.
    — Vājasaneyi Saṃhitā 36.17
