    Chapter 9: The Gṛhya Ritual: Domestic Rites in the Gṛhyasūtras¶

    Invocatory Verse

    gṛbhṇāmi te saubhagatvāya hastaṃ mayā patyā jaradaṣṭir yathāsaḥ
    bhago aryamā savitā puraṃdhir mahyaṃ tvādur gārhapatyāya devāḥ
    I take your hand for good fortune,
    so that with me as your husband you may reach old age.
    Bhaga, Aryaman, Savitṛ and Puraṃdhi, the gods,
    have given you to me for the householder’s life.
    — Ṛgveda 10.85.36

    9.1 Introduction¶

    Alongside the solemn rites of the previous chapter, which required three fires and a team of priests, the Vedic householder performed a large number of simpler rites in his own house, in a single domestic fire, usually by himself or with one priest. These are the gṛhya (“domestic”) rites. They include daily offerings, rites of the month and the seasons, rites for the house, the cattle and the fields, and the life-cycle rites (saṃskāra) from conception to death. Because they touched every family and every stage of life, they have had a far longer and wider afterlife than the śrauta ritual: much of what Hindus today recognise as “Vedic” ritual—the wedding round the fire, the thread ceremony, the funeral and the offerings to the ancestors—comes down from them.

    The rites are prescribed in the Gṛhyasūtras, the domestic manuals of the Vedic schools, which belong with the Śrauta-, Dharma- and Śulbasūtras to the Kalpa Vedāṅga (Section 7.7). The tradition classes them as smārta, based on remembered tradition rather than directly on the Veda, but their mantras are drawn from the Vedic Saṃhitās and their procedures are modelled on the śrauta rites. This chapter describes the rites as the Gṛhyasūtras present them; the legal side of the life-cycle rites—the age of initiation, the forms of marriage, inheritance and impurity—belongs to the Dharmaśāstras and is treated in Volume 18, Rājanīti and Dharmaśāstra, and the initiation as the beginning of education in Volume 22, Manas and Śikṣā. The standard modern accounts are Jan Gonda’s survey of the “non-solemn” rites, the second volume of P. V. Kane’s History of Dharmaśāstra, and Raj Bali Pandey’s study of the saṃskāras (Gonda 1980; Kane 1941; Pandey 1969).

    9.2 The Gṛhyasūtras¶

    Each Vedic school had, at least in principle, its own Gṛhyasūtra, and the surviving texts are listed in Table 9.1. Most were composed between about the sixth and the third century BCE, and several are closely related: the Khādira Gṛhyasūtra is an abridgement of the Gobhila, and the Taittirīya sūtras share much material. Some schools collected the mantras needed for the domestic rites into separate books, such as the Mantrapāṭha of the Āpastambins and the Mantrabrāhmaṇa of the Sāmavedins, so that the sūtra itself need cite only the opening words. Seven of the most important texts were translated into English by Hermann Oldenberg in the Sacred Books of the East (Oldenberg 1886).

    Table 9.1. The principal Gṛhyasūtras by Veda and school.
    Veda School Gṛhyasūtras
    Ṛgveda Śākala; Kauṣītaki Āśvalāyana; Śāṅkhāyana and Kauṣītaka (Kauṣītaki)
    Sāmaveda Kauthuma, Rāṇāyanīya, Jaiminīya Gobhila (Kauthuma); Khādira (Rāṇāyanīya); Jaiminīya; mantras in the Mantrabrāhmaṇa

    Atharvaveda Śaunaka Kauśika Sūtra

    The sūtras differ in the rites they include, in their order and in many details. Most begin with marriage, because it is at marriage that the domestic fire is normally established; others begin with general rules or with the daily offerings. Their variety is itself significant. The Āśvalāyana Gṛhyasūtra, for example, introduces its account of the wedding by observing that the customs of different regions and villages vary and that they should be followed (1.7.1–2), and several sūtras close a description by referring the householder to the practice of his family or region. The domestic ritual was thus never a single uniform system: the sūtras codified a common Brahmanical frame and left room for local custom within it.

    9.3 The Domestic Fire and the Model Offering¶

    The centre of the domestic cult is a single fire, variously called the gṛhya, aupāsana, āvasathya or “marriage” (vaivāhika) fire. According to Pāraskara and others it is set up at the time of marriage, or, in the view of some teachers, when a son takes his share of the family property on its division. A householder who later established the three śrauta fires kept the domestic fire as well. Into it he and his wife offered every morning and evening a small oblation of grain or rice—the domestic counterpart of the agnihotra—and in it were performed the other domestic rites.

    The basic domestic rite is the pākayajña, the “cooked offering”. Its model is the sthālīpāka, a pot of rice or barley cooked in milk or water on the domestic fire and offered at new and full moon to Agni and other gods, just as the cakes of the śrauta new- and full-moon offerings are offered in the āhavanīya. The frame of the rite is a miniature version of the śrauta iṣṭi: the fire is enclosed and the grass strewn; a Brahmin may be seated in the place of the brahman; two streams of ghee are poured; the two “ghee portions” are offered to Agni and Soma; then come the principal oblations, the offering to Agni “who makes the offering good” (sviṣṭakṛt), and the concluding acts. Once the frame is known, each particular rite is described only by its special features—the same principle of model and variant that governs the śrauta ritual (Section 8.4).

    Āśvalāyana opens his Gṛhyasūtra by distinguishing three kinds of pākayajña: those offered in the fire (huta); those offered outside the fire, as bali portions set down for spirits, animals and beings (prahuta); and those “offered in a Brahmin”, that is, the feeding of Brahmins. The third kind shows how the gift of food to learned men came to be treated as a sacrifice in its own right, a development that shaped later Hindu ritual and charity. The Gautama Dharmasūtra lists seven pākayajñas: the aṣṭakā, the pārvaṇa (new- and full-moon), the śrāddha for the ancestors, and four rites named after the full moons of the months Śrāvaṇa, Mārgaśīrṣa (āgrahāyaṇī), Caitra and Āśvayuja. Alongside them stand the five daily “great sacrifices” to the Veda, the gods, the ancestors, human beings and all creatures, described in Section 6.3.

    9.4 The Domestic Year¶

    The Gṛhyasūtras attach rites to the seasons and to the work of the household and the farm. At the full moon of Śrāvaṇa, at the height of the rains, the householder makes the śravaṇā offering and sets out bali for the serpents, a rite continued each evening until the full moon of Mārgaśīrṣa; then, in the āgrahāyaṇī rite, the family “descends” (pratyavarohaṇa) from the raised beds used during the season of snakes to sleep again on the ground. At the full moon of Āśvayuja offerings are made for the welfare of the cattle, and at harvest the first fruits of the new grain are offered (āgrayaṇa) before it may be eaten. The aṣṭakās, on the eighth days of the dark halves of the winter months, are offerings to the ancestors which in some sūtras include the killing of a cow or goat. The śūlagava, the “spit-ox”, is a sacrifice of an ox to Rudra performed outside the village, an example of the many rites in which the dangerous god is honoured at a distance (cf. the tryambaka rite of the śrauta ritual, Section 8.5).

    The study of the Veda also had its annual calendar. In the rainy season the students and teachers “opened” the year’s study with the upākaraṇa, and some months later closed it with the utsarjana; the sūtras also lay down days on which recitation is forbidden (anadhyāya), such as the days of the new and full moon and times of storm or of impurity. The opening rite survives as the Upākarma, observed in Śrāvaṇa by many Brahmin communities, when the sacred thread (yajñopavīta) is renewed; in Tamil Nadu it is known as Āvaṇi Aviṭṭam. Other rites accompany the building of a house, with offerings to Vāstoṣpati, the “lord of the dwelling” already invoked in the Ṛgveda (RV 7.54–55); the later development of these rites into the vāstu tradition is treated in Volume 16, Vāstu–Śilpa.

    9.5 The Life-Cycle Rites¶

    The number of life-cycle rites varies. The Gautama Dharmasūtra (chapter 8) counts forty “sacraments”, including the śrauta rites, while later tradition settled on sixteen, in lists that differ in detail; a commonly cited list is given in Table 9.2. That list is a later standardisation. The Gṛhyasūtras themselves do not use a fixed number, and they treat some rites at length and others briefly or not at all.

    Table 9.2. A common list of the sixteen saṃskāras (lists vary between texts).
    No. Saṃskāra Purpose and Occasion
    1 Garbhādhāna Rite for conception
    2 Puṃsavana Rite to obtain a male child, in early pregnancy
    3 Sīmantonnayana Parting of the pregnant wife’s hair
    4 Jātakarma Birth rite; honey and ghee given to the newborn
    5 Nāmakaraṇa Naming (commonly on the tenth or twelfth day)
    6 Niṣkramaṇa First outing; the child is shown the sun
    7 Annaprāśana First solid food (sixth month)
    8 Cūḍākaraṇa First tonsure
    9 Karṇavedha Ear-piercing
    10 Vidyārambha Beginning of learning the alphabet
    11 Upanayana Initiation into Vedic study (for males of the three upper varṇas)
    12 Vedārambha Beginning of Vedic study
    13 Keśānta First shaving of the beard
    14 Samāvartana Return home at the end of study
    15 Vivāha Marriage
    16 Antyeṣṭi Funeral rites

    9.5.1 Marriage¶

    Most Gṛhyasūtras describe the wedding first. Its mantras come above all from the great wedding hymn of the Ṛgveda (RV 10.85), which tells of the marriage of Sūryā, the daughter of the Sun, and from the corresponding fourteenth book of the Atharvaveda. The groom takes the bride’s hand with RV 10.85.36, the verse that opens this chapter; she treads on a stone, to be firm; parched grain, poured into her hands by her brother, is offered in the fire; the couple walk round the fire and take seven steps together; and in the evening the groom shows her the pole star (dhruva) and the star Arundhatī as emblems of constancy. These elements, described more fully in Volume 18 (in the chapter on family and law), recur in nearly every sūtra, but their order differs, and the sūtras include many local elements: Āśvalāyana, for instance, describes a test in which the girl chooses one of several lumps of earth taken from different places, from which her future is foretold. After the wedding the couple keep continence for three nights, and the marriage is consummated on the fourth (caturthīkarman) with further verses from RV 10.85.

    9.5.2 Conception, Pregnancy and Birth¶

    The rites of the first years are concerned above all with the safe birth and survival of children, at a time when both were uncertain. The garbhādhāna accompanies conception with verses such as RV 10.184 (“May Viṣṇu prepare the womb, may Tvaṣṭṛ shape the forms”). The puṃsavana, in the third month of pregnancy, is intended to secure a male child: in several sūtras the juice of a banyan shoot is placed in the right nostril of the pregnant woman. In the sīmantonnayana, the “parting of the hair”, the husband parts his wife’s hair with a porcupine quill and other objects, and in some sūtras lute-players are asked to sing. At birth, before the cord is cut, the father performs the jātakarman: he gives the child honey and ghee, sometimes from a golden spoon, whispers into its ear, and prays for its intelligence and long life; a secret name may be given at this time, and the public name on the tenth or twelfth day.

    These rites also show the preferences of the society that produced them. Prayers are overwhelmingly for sons; and several sūtras state that the rites of childhood are to be performed for a daughter without the Vedic mantras. In this the domestic manuals anticipate the Dharmaśāstras’ exclusion of women from Vedic ritual (see Section 14.7 and Volume 18).

    9.5.3 Childhood and Initiation¶

    The first feeding with solid food (annaprāśana) takes place in the sixth month, and the sūtras recommend particular foods for the qualities they are believed to confer. The first tonsure (cūḍākaraṇa), in the third year or according to family custom, leaves locks of hair arranged in the manner of the child’s lineage; the godāna or keśānta, the first shaving of the beard, follows at about sixteen.

    The initiation (upanayana) is described in the Gṛhyasūtras as a rite of passage into the teacher’s care. The teacher asks the boy’s name, takes his hand and entrusts him to Savitṛ and other gods; he invests him with a girdle, a staff and an upper garment or skin, and teaches him the Sāvitrī verse (RV 3.62.10), in some sūtras first by quarter-verses, then by half-verses, then as a whole. The boy then lays fuel on the fire (samidādhāna), a duty he repeats daily as a student, and begins to beg his food. Some sūtras prescribe special observances (vrata) before the study of particular parts of the Veda, such as the Mahānāmnī verses of the Sāmaveda or the esoteric portions of the Āraṇyakas. The return home at the end of study (samāvartana) is marked by a bath, after which the young man is a snātaka, “one who has bathed”, bound by a code of conduct that the Gṛhyasūtras list in detail, and eligible to marry. The legal rules on the age and eligibility for initiation, and its denial to Śūdras and women, are discussed in Volume 18 and Volume 22.

    9.5.4 Death and the Ancestors¶

    The funeral rites draw their mantras from the funeral hymns of the Ṛgveda (10.14–18) and the eighteenth book of the Atharvaveda; their religious background is discussed in Section 13.7. An āhitāgni, one who has kept the śrauta fires, is cremated in them: his body is laid on the pyre between the three fires, and his sacrificial implements are placed on the different parts of his body, so that he goes to the next world equipped for sacrifice. The body may be covered with the limbs and fat of a cow or goat, recalling RV 10.16.7 (“wrap yourself in the armour of Agni with the cows”). The widow lies down beside the body and is called back to life with RV 10.18.8, “Rise, woman, to the world of the living”. In the funeral hymn the fire is asked not to destroy the dead man but to carry him, “cooked”, to the Fathers (RV 10.16.1–2). After the cremation the mourners pour water for the dead, observe a period of impurity, and on a later day gather the bones into an urn, which is buried; the Śatapatha Brāhmaṇa (13.8) describes the building of a burial mound over the remains.

    The dead are then sustained by offerings. The śrauta ritual includes a monthly piṇḍapitṛyajña, in which balls of rice are offered to the father, grandfather and great-grandfather; the domestic śrāddha, at which Brahmins representing the ancestors are fed, developed alongside it and became, in the Dharmaśāstras, one of the most elaborately regulated of all Hindu rites (Volume 18). The Gṛhyasūtras thus mark the point at which Vedic ancestor worship turns into the classical system of śrāddha and sapiṇḍa kinship.

    9.6 Women and the Domestic Rites¶

    The domestic rites gave the wife a larger part than the śrauta ritual. The domestic fire is established at marriage and belongs to the couple; the wife tends it, prepares the offerings and takes part in the daily oblations, and Gobhila’s Gṛhyasūtra permits her to make the morning and evening offerings herself. The bali offerings of food for the household spirits and creatures were in practice largely in women’s hands. Several rites, including parts of the wedding, the rites of pregnancy and those of childhood, were performed in the presence of, and partly by, the women of the family, and their songs and customs accompanied them. The Āpastamba Dharmasūtra closes by reporting the view that whatever has not been laid down should be learned from women and from people of all classes—an acknowledgement that the Sanskrit manuals recorded only part of what was actually done.

    At the same time, the domestic manuals reflect the narrowing of women’s access to the Veda. The Vedic mantras of the rites are recited by men; for daughters several rites are to be performed without mantras; and the later Dharmaśāstras made marriage the only “initiation” for a woman (Section 14.7). Jamison’s study of the wife in Vedic ritual shows how the texts combine a real ritual role for the woman with anxiety about her sexuality and her loyalty, and how the hospitality of the household, over which she presided, was itself conceived as a kind of sacrifice (Jamison 1996). The history of women in the domestic rites is therefore neither one of simple exclusion nor one of equality, but of participation within limits set by men’s control of the sacred word.

    9.7 The Kauśika Sūtra and the Atharvavedic Rites¶

    The domestic manual of the Śaunaka school of the Atharvaveda, the Kauśika Sūtra, is unlike the others. Besides the usual domestic rites it gives the ritual use (viniyoga) of the Atharvavedic hymns: how a charm against fever is to be recited while the patient is sprinkled or given a drink, how an amulet (maṇi) of a particular wood is to be bound on, how rain is to be brought, how a rival is to be driven away or an enemy harmed. It is therefore the key to understanding how the hymns described in Chapter 5 were used in practice. Maurice Bloomfield edited the text with extracts from its medieval commentaries in 1890, and Willem Caland translated its central sections on “magical” ritual (Bloomfield 1890). The sūtra shows that healing, protection and sorcery were part of the same domestic world as the wedding and the funeral, and that the boundary between “religion” and “magic” drawn by nineteenth-century scholars does not match the texts’ own categories.

    9.8 From the Gṛhyasūtras to Living Practice¶

    The Gṛhyasūtras are the remote ancestors of present-day Hindu domestic ritual, but the line of descent passes through two thousand years of change. The Dharmaśāstras and their medieval digests (nibandha) added rules on who might perform which rite; handbooks (prayoga, paddhati) composed for each school and region set out the actual sequence of acts and words; and Purāṇic and Āgamic elements were added, such as the preliminary worship of Gaṇeśa, the invocation of auspiciousness (puṇyāhavācana) and offerings to the Mother goddesses. Regional custom and the songs and practices of women, which the sūtras themselves acknowledged, shaped the rites at least as much as the Sanskrit texts.

    The question of who was entitled to the Vedic mantras of the domestic rites remained contested. Medieval authorities generally held that women’s and Śūdras’ rites should be performed without Vedic mantras, or with Purāṇic substitutes; the seventeenth-century Śūdrakamalākara of Kamalākara Bhaṭṭa is a well-known manual of this kind. In the nineteenth century Dayānanda Sarasvatī’s Saṃskāravidhi, first published in the 1870s, set out the sixteen saṃskāras with Vedic mantras for all members of the Ārya Samāj, women included, and several later reform movements followed this lead. Today the wedding, the thread ceremony, the funeral and the ancestral offerings are the domestic rites most widely performed with Vedic mantras, often in shortened form and by a family priest; their study shows both the remarkable continuity of the Vedic tradition and the many ways in which it has been reshaped.

    9.9 Summary¶

    The domestic manuals are where the Vedic ritual meets the ordinary household. Their authors adapted a priestly system to a single fire and a single family, and in doing so they recorded, more than any other Vedic texts, what people hoped for and feared: the safe birth of children, the marriage of daughters, the health of the herd, the welfare of the dead. They also show the Brahmanical frame tightening around that life, as the Vedic mantras were reserved for twice-born men, and at the same time loosening, as the sūtras themselves conceded that region, family and women’s custom decided much of what was actually done. Both tendencies run through the later history of Hindu domestic ritual.

    Closing Verse: Blessing of the Couple

    ihaiva stam mā vi yauṣṭaṃ viśvam āyur vy aśnutam
    krīḷantau putrair naptṛbhir modamānau sve gṛhe
    Stay here, do not be parted; attain the full span of life,
    playing with sons and grandsons, rejoicing in your own home.
    — Ṛgveda 10.85.42
