    Chapter 9: Dhyāna: Meditation and the Stages of Samādhi¶

    Bhagavad Gītā 6.10

    yogī yuñjīta satatam ātmānaṃ rahasi sthitaḥ
    ekākī yata-cittātmā nirāśīr aparigrahaḥ
    The yogi should constantly engage the mind in meditation, dwelling in solitude, alone, with mind and body controlled, free from expectations and possessiveness.

    9.1 The Meaning of Dhyāna¶

    Dhyāna (meditation) is the seventh limb of Patañjali’s Aṣṭāṅga Yoga and a practice common to almost every form of yoga. The word is derived from the Sanskrit root dhyai (to contemplate, to think deeply, to meditate). Patañjali defines it as the unbroken extension of a single cognition towards the object (tatra pratyayaikatānatā dhyānam, YS 3.2), a definition discussed with the other limbs in Chapter 2 (Section 2.5.6). This chapter looks at meditation as a practice: its stages, objects, methods and obstacles, and its place in the Upaniṣadic, Buddhist and Jain traditions.

    Through Buddhism, the word dhyāna (Pali jhāna) gave rise to the Chinese Ch'an, the Japanese Zen, and the Korean Sŏn — a measure of how widely Indian meditative vocabulary travelled.

    9.2 The Progressive Stages: Dhāraṇā, Dhyāna, Samādhi¶

    Patañjali treats concentration, meditation and absorption as one continuum, the three “internal” limbs that together form saṃyama (Chapter 2, Sections 2.5.6–2.5.7). Seen from the side of practice, the difference between them is the steadiness of attention. In concentration the mind is fixed on one point but still wanders and must be brought back; in meditation the stream of attention flows without interruption; in absorption the object alone remains and the sense of a separate meditator fades. Most meditation manuals, Hindu, Buddhist and Jain, describe some such progression from effortful focus to effortless absorption, and most warn that the later stages cannot be forced.

    9.3 Meditation in the Gheraṇḍa Saṃhitā¶

    The Gheraṇḍa Saṃhitā describes three types of meditation (Table 9.1).

    Table 9.1. Three Types of Dhyāna (Gheraṇḍa Saṃhitā)
    Type Name Description
    Gross (sthūla) Sthūla Dhyāna Meditation on a concrete form (deity, Guru, image)
    Luminous (jyotis) Jyotir Dhyāna Meditation on inner light; the flame of consciousness
    Subtle (sūkṣma) Sūkṣma Dhyāna Meditation on kuṇḍalinī, bindu, or formless Brahman

    9.4 The Stages of Samādhi¶

    The stages of absorption that lie beyond meditation — the cognitive absorptions with and without gross and subtle thought, the truth-bearing insight, the seedless absorption and the “cloud of dharma” that precedes liberation — are set out once, with Patañjali’s sūtras and the commentators’ classification, in Chapter 2 (Section 2.3.6; Figure 2.1). The Haṭha texts, for their part, use samādhi as a synonym of Rāja Yoga, of the “no-mind” state (amanaska) and of the dissolution of the mind in inner sound (Chapters 7 and 10); the Gheraṇḍa Saṃhitā, which closes its sevenfold path with six kinds of samādhi reached through different techniques, shows how far the term had moved from Patañjali’s analysis.

    9.5 Meditation Posture and Environment¶

    The Bhagavad Gītā provides precise instructions for the meditation posture:

    Gītā 6.11–6.13

    śucau deśe pratiṣṭhāpya sthiram āsanam ātmanaḥ
    nāty-ucchritaṃ nāti-nīcaṃ cailājina-kuśottaram
    tatraikāgraṃ manaḥ kṛtvā yata-cittēndriya-kriyaḥ
    upaviśyāsane yuñjyād yogam ātma-viśuddhaye
    samaṃ kāya-śiro-grīvaṃ dhārayann acalaṃ sthiraḥ
    saṃprekṣya nāsikāgraṃ svaṃ diśaś cānavalokayan
    In a clean spot, having established a firm seat for oneself, neither too high nor too low, covered with cloth, deerskin, and kuśa grass; thereon, making the mind one-pointed, with thoughts and senses controlled, one should practise Yoga for self-purification, holding the body, head, and neck erect and still, gazing at the tip of the nose, not looking in any direction.

    9.6 Yoga Nidrā: Yogic Sleep¶

    Yoga Nidrā (“yogic sleep”) is a state of conscious deep sleep — a systematic method of inducing complete physical, mental, and emotional relaxation while maintaining awareness. It is often described as the state between waking and sleeping, where the body is in deep rest but the mind remains fully alert.

    In pre-modern texts yoganidrā denotes either the cosmic sleep of Viṣṇu or, in some Haṭha works, a state of samādhi. The modern guided-relaxation practice of that name was created in the 1960s–70s by Swāmī Satyānanda Sarasvatī of the Bihar School of Yoga, who said he drew upon the Tantric practice of nyāsa (the ritual placing of mantras on different parts of the body) as well as modern relaxation methods.

    Key Point: The Practice of Yoga Nidrā

    A typical Yoga Nidrā session includes the following stages:

    1. Preparation: Lying in Śavāsana; setting a saṃkalpa (resolve)

    2. Rotation of consciousness: Awareness is guided systematically through each part of the body

    3. Breath awareness: Observation of the natural breath

    4. Opposite sensations: Experience of pairs of opposites (heavy-light, hot-cold)

    5. Visualisation: Guided imagery (nature scenes, symbols, cakras)

    6. Saṃkalpa: Repetition of the resolve

    7. Return: Gradual return to external awareness

    Research on Yoga Nidrā is limited. A small brain-imaging study found increased dopamine release in the striatum during the practice (Kjaer et al. 2002), and some trials report reduced anxiety, but larger and better-controlled studies are needed.

    9.7 The Four Attitudes (Bhāvanās)¶

    Among Patañjali’s remedies for the obstacles that scatter the mind (Chapter 2, Section 2.3.5), one became a meditation practice in its own right in several traditions: the cultivation of four attitudes (bhāvanās) towards others (Table 9.2):

    Yoga Sūtra 1.33

    maitrī-karuṇā-muditōpekṣāṇāṃ sukha-duḥkha-puṇyāpuṇya-viṣayāṇāṃ bhāvanātaś citta-prasādanam
    By cultivating friendliness towards the happy, compassion towards the suffering, joy towards the virtuous, and equanimity towards the non-virtuous — the mind becomes serene.

    Table 9.2. The Four Bhāvanās (Attitudes for Mental Serenity), with effects as explained by commentators. The same four are the Buddhist brahmavihāras.
    Attitude Towards Effect
    Maitrī (Friendliness) Happy people Removes jealousy
    Karuṇā (Compassion) Suffering people Removes callousness
    Muditā (Joy) Virtuous people Removes envy
    Upekṣā (Equanimity) Non-virtuous people Removes contempt and agitation

    9.8 Classification of Meditation Objects in the Yoga Tradition¶

    The Yoga, Tantric and Vedāntic traditions offer a wide range of meditation objects (ālambana), suited to different temperaments and usually graded from gross to subtle; Patañjali himself allows meditation on “whatever one likes” (YS 1.39). Table 9.3 groups the principal objects with their sources.

    Table 9.3. Hierarchy of Meditation Objects from Gross to Subtle
    Category Examples Tradition and Textual Source
    Places in the body (deśa) Navel circle; heart lotus; light in the head; tip of the nose; tip of the tongue Vyāsa on Yoga Sūtra 3.1
    Physical form (Sthūla) Mūrti of the chosen deity (Viṣṇu, Śiva, Devī); the guru; flame; yantra; lotus Bhāgavata Purāṇa 3.28; Gheraṇḍa Saṃhitā 6; Āgama texts
    Elements and cakras The five elements with their shapes and colours; the cakras with their bījas and deities Tantric and Haṭha texts; Ṣaṭcakranirūpaṇa
    Breath (Prāṇa) Inhalation-exhalation cycle; pause between breaths Yoga Sūtras 1.34; Haṭha Yoga Pradīpikā
    Mantra Oṃ; so'ham; personal mantra from Guru Yoga Sūtras 1.27–28; Tantra texts
    Inner light (Jyotis) Luminous point in the heart or between the eyebrows Yoga Sūtras 1.36; Śvetāśvatara Upaniṣad
    Sound (Nāda) Inner sounds heard in meditation; anāhata nāda Haṭha Yoga Pradīpikā 4.65–102
    Formless awareness Pure consciousness without object; the witness (sākṣin); the enquiry “Who am I?” Māṇḍūkya Upaniṣad; Yoga Sūtras 1.51; Advaita (Chapter 4)

    9.8.1 The Practice of Antar Trāṭaka — Internal Gazing¶

    The Haṭha Yoga tradition describes Trāṭaka (steady gazing) as a powerful bridge between external practice and internal meditation. After gazing steadily at an external object (typically a small mark or, in modern practice, a candle flame) until the eyes water (HYP 2.31), the practitioner closes the eyes; modern teachers add visualisation of the after-image at the ājñā cakra (the point between the eyebrows). This practice develops Ekāgratā (one-pointedness) and prepares the mind for deeper, objectless meditation.

    9.9 Dhyāna in the Upaniṣadic Tradition¶

    The Upaniṣads contain some of the earliest and most profound instructions on meditation in any tradition. The progression from the older to the younger Upaniṣads reveals an increasingly systematic understanding of contemplative practice (Table 9.4).

    Table 9.4. Meditation Teachings in Major Upaniṣads
    Upaniṣad Key Meditation Teaching
    Bṛhadāraṇyaka Neti Neti — the via negativa: meditation by progressively negating all that is not the Self
    Chāndogya Dāhara Vidyā — meditation on the tiny space within the heart, which contains the entire universe
    Kaṭha The chariot metaphor: the Ātman as the rider, buddhi as the charioteer, manas as the reins, senses as the horses — meditation as the mastery of this inner vehicle
    Śvetāśvatara Detailed meditation posture, breath regulation, and visualisation of inner fire and light
    Māṇḍūkya The four states of consciousness (waking, dreaming, deep sleep, Turīya) as progressive stages of meditative realisation

    The Śvetāśvatara’s instruction on posture (2.8) is quoted in Chapter 1.

    The Dāhara Vidyā — Meditation on the Inner Space

    The Chāndogya Upaniṣad (8.1.3) declares: “As vast as this external space is the space within the heart. Within it are contained both heaven and earth, both fire and wind, both sun and moon, lightning and stars. Whatever is here in the world and whatever is not — all that is contained within the heart.” This Dāhara Vidyā (meditation on the tiny inner space) represents one of the most ancient and profound meditation techniques in the Indian tradition. The practitioner is instructed to contemplate the space within the heart as identical with infinite Brahman — discovering that the microcosm of the individual self contains the entirety of the macrocosm.

    9.10 Buddhist and Jain Yoga¶

    Brahmanical Yoga was never the only Indian science of meditation. Buddhist and Jain renunciants developed their own disciplines from the time of the Buddha and Mahāvīra, and the three traditions borrowed from, argued with and redefined one another for well over a thousand years (Bronkhorst 1993; Samuel 2008). Their doctrines and institutions are treated in Volume 4, Buddha–Jina Prabhā; this section outlines their contribution to the history of yoga.

    9.10.1 Buddhist Meditation¶

    The early Buddhist discourses (the Pali Nikāyas and their parallels in other languages) describe four meditative absorptions, the jhānas (Sanskrit dhyāna). The first is accompanied by applied and sustained thought (vitakka, vicāra) and by the rapture and happiness born of seclusion; in the second, thought falls away and the mind becomes unified; in the third, rapture fades into equanimity and mindfulness; the fourth is beyond pleasure and pain, purified by equanimity. Beyond these lie four formless attainments, from the sphere of infinite space to the sphere of “neither perception nor non-perception”. According to the discourses, the future Buddha learnt the last two from his teachers Āḷāra Kālāma and Uddaka Rāmaputta and found them insufficient; liberation required insight (vipassanā, Sanskrit vipaśyanā) into impermanence, unsatisfactoriness and not-self, joined to calm (samatha, śamatha) (Gethin 1998; Wynne 2007). The Satipaṭṭhāna Sutta organises mindfulness under four headings — body (beginning with the breath), feelings, mind and mental phenomena — and became the charter of later insight meditation.

    The parallels with Patañjali are close and long recognised. The first two stages of cognitive samādhi in YS 1.17 bear the Buddhist names vitarka and vicāra; the five qualities that lead to samādhi in YS 1.20 (faith, energy, mindfulness, concentration and insight) are the Buddhist five “faculties”; and the four attitudes of YS 1.33 (Table 9.2) are the Buddhist brahmavihāras. Buddhist scholasticism produced its own manuals of yoga. Buddhaghosa’s Visuddhimagga (5th century) lists forty meditation subjects — coloured and elemental discs (kasiṇa), the foulness of the body, recollections, the four divine abidings and others — and matches them to the temperaments of different students. In North India the school known as Yogācāra (“practitioners of yoga”) took its name from meditative practice, and its vast Yogācārabhūmi maps the stages of the path. The late Buddhist Tantras taught subtle-body practices of their own: the Kālacakra Tantra (early 11th century) teaches a six-limbed yoga (ṣaḍaṅgayoga) of withdrawal, meditation, breath control, retention, recollection and absorption, and the earliest Haṭha text, the Amṛtasiddhi, comes from a Buddhist milieu (Chapter 7). The Tantras of this class divide practice into a “generation stage” (utpattikrama), in which the practitioner visualises himself as the deity in its maṇḍala, and a “completion stage” (sampannakrama or niṣpannakrama), in which he works directly with the channels, winds and drops (nāḍī, prāṇa, bindu) to bring the winds into the central channel. The completion-stage yogas — among them the “inner heat” (caṇḍālī, Tibetan gtum mo) of the Hevajra Tantra and the cycle later known in Tibet as the Six Yogas of Nāropa — were carried to Tibet from the eleventh century and are still practised there; they are the closest Buddhist relatives of Haṭha Yoga’s aim of forcing the breath into the central channel (Samuel 2008). In the 20th century the Burmese revival of insight meditation, carried to India by S.N. Goenka from 1969, and the secular “mindfulness” programmes derived from it, made Buddhist meditation as global as postural yoga.

    9.10.2 Jain Meditation and Jain Yoga¶

    Jain usage is distinctive. In Jain doctrine yoga first means the activity of body, speech and mind through which karmic matter flows into the soul (kāya-vāṅ-manaḥ-karma yogaḥ, Tattvārthasūtra 6.1); the ascetic’s aim is to bring this “yoga” to rest. The early ideal is the motionless standing posture of “abandoning the body” (kāyotsarga) in which Mahāvīra and the Tīrthaṅkaras are portrayed. Umāsvāti’s Tattvārthasūtra defines meditation (dhyāna) as the restraint of thought on a single object (9.27) and distinguishes four kinds: “sorrowful” and “cruel” meditation, which bind, and “virtuous” (dharma) and “pure” (śukla) meditation, which lead to liberation (Dundas 2002).

    From the 8th century Jain authors engaged directly with Patañjala Yoga. Haribhadra Sūri wrote several works on yoga, among them the Yogabindu and the Yogadṛṣṭisamuccaya, which arranges the path in eight “views” (dṛṣṭi) correlated with Patañjali’s eight limbs and discusses the yogas of other schools with notable generosity (Chapple 2003). Śubhacandra’s Jñānārṇava (11th century) and Hemacandra’s Yogaśāstra (12th century), written for the Caulukya king Kumārapāla, combine the ethics of the Jain layman and monk with breath control and four graded objects of virtuous meditation — the body, syllables and mantras, the form of the Jina, and the formless — while warning that breath control is not needed for liberation and may disturb the mind (Qvarnström 2002). In the 1970s Ācārya Mahāprajña of the Terāpanth order developed Prekṣā Dhyāna, a modern Jain meditation system that draws on these sources.

    9.10.3 Shared Ground¶

    Across the three traditions the vocabulary of practice — dhyāna, samādhi, bhāvanā, the breath, the four divine attitudes — is largely shared, while the goal is understood differently: the isolation of the self (puruṣa) in Sāṃkhya-Yoga, the extinction of craving and ignorance (nirvāṇa) in Buddhism, and the release of the soul (jīva) from karmic matter in Jainism. Christian contemplative prayer, Sufi murāqaba and Daoist inner alchemy also cultivate inner stillness, and interpret it in their own terms.
