    Preface¶

    This is Volume 5 of the series Bhāratīya Jñāna Paramparā: Yoga Taraṅgiṇī: Yoga Traditions. A taraṅgiṇī is a river, “she who has waves”, and the image suits the subject. Yoga has never been a single system. It has meant Patañjali’s meditative discipline, the Gītā’s yogas of action, devotion and knowledge, the subtle-body practices of the Tantras, the forceful techniques of Haṭha Yoga and, in the last century, a posture practice that has spread across the world.

    The chapters follow these currents roughly in the order in which they arose: the Upaniṣadic, epic and Purāṇic background (Chapter 1); Patañjali’s Yoga Sūtra, its eight limbs and its commentators (Chapter 2); the Gītā’s yogas of action and devotion (Chapter 3) and the Advaita tradition’s uneasy relation to yoga (Chapter 4); the yoga of the Tantras and the subtle body they devised (Chapters 5–6); Haṭha Yoga, which inherited that body, with its texts, its ascetic orders and its reception among Sufis, Mughals, Sikhs and poets in Tamil and Marathi (Chapter 7); breath, meditation and sound (Chapters 8–10); and modern yoga (Chapter 11).

    We have tried to combine respect for the tradition with historical honesty. Dates follow the academic chronology, with traditional dates labelled as such. Sanskrit passages are given only where their wording and source could be confirmed. We distinguish what the texts claim, what later tradition added, and what modern research has shown. Practices with health implications carry safety notes; nothing here is medical advice. Caste and the abuse of power by modern gurus are addressed directly.

    This is a work of synthesis for the general reader and student. Every quotation should be checked against a critical edition before it is relied on, and specialists will find much to refine.

    Nikhil Kumar Rajput
    Ramanujan College, University of Delhi
    Guru Pūrṇimā, 2026
