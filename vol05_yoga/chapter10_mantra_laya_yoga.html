    Chapter 10: Mantra Yoga, Laya Yoga, and Nāda Yoga¶

    Part A: Mantra Yoga — The Path of Sacred Sound

    10.1 Mantra¶

    Mantra Yoga is the spiritual discipline based on the repetitive chanting or mental recitation of sacred sound formulas (mantras). Grammatically, mantra is formed from the root man (to think) with the instrumental suffix -tra: “an instrument of thought”. Tantric texts add a traditional etymology, mananāt trāyate, “that which protects when it is meditated upon”.

    Definition: Mantra

    A mantra is a sacred syllable, word, or phrase believed to be endowed with spiritual power (śakti). According to the tradition, when it is repeated with faith, concentration, and proper intonation, it transforms the consciousness of the practitioner, purifies the mind, and leads to the direct experience of the deity or principle that the mantra embodies.

    Several Indian traditions treat sound (śabda) as a primordial creative force. The Upaniṣads speak of a “sound-Brahman” (śabda-brahman) to be known before the supreme Brahman (Maitrī Upaniṣad 6.22); the grammarian Bhartṛhari (5th century) identified Brahman with the eternal Word; and the Tantras describe the universe as unfolding from the Goddess as Speech (vāk) through the letters of the alphabet (see Volume 10, Śabda Vidyā). The Vedic tradition is fundamentally an oral tradition, in which the sacred sounds of the Vedas are transmitted with extraordinary precision from Guru to disciple.

    10.2 Types of Mantras¶

    Table 10.1 lists the principal kinds of mantra.

    Table 10.1. Classification of Mantras
    Type Description and Examples
    Bīja Mantras (Seed) Single-syllable mantras containing the essence of a deity or element: Oṃ, Hrīṃ, Klīṃ, Śrīṃ, Aiṃ
    Vedic Mantras Mantras from the Vedas: Gāyatrī Mantra, Mahāmṛtyuñjaya Mantra (ṚV 7.59.12), Śānti Mantras
    Purāṇic and Āgamic Mantras Formulas of devotional and sectarian worship: Om Namaḥ Śivāya, Om Namo Nārāyaṇāya
    Tāntric Mantras Complex formulas from the Tantra traditions, often with specific nyāsa rituals
    Nāma Mantras Divine names: Rāma, Kṛṣṇa, Hari, Devī

    10.2.1 The Praṇava: Oṃ¶

    The supreme mantra of the Yoga tradition is Oṃ (also written AUM), known as the Praṇava. It is the primordial sound from which all creation emerges and into which it dissolves.

    Māṇḍūkya Upaniṣad 1

    oṃ ity etad akṣaram idaṃ sarvaṃ tasyopavyākhyānaṃ
    bhūtaṃ bhavad bhaviṣyad iti sarvam oṃkāra eva
    yac cānyat trikālātītaṃ tad apy oṃkāra eva
    Oṃ — this syllable is all this. All that is past, present, and future is Oṃ. And whatever transcends the three times — that too is Oṃ.

    The Upaniṣad goes on (verses 8–12) to equate the three constituent sounds A, U and M with the waking, dreaming and deep-sleep states of the self — named Vaiśvānara, Taijasa and Prājña — and the soundless “measureless” syllable with the fourth state, Turīya (see Table 1.5 in Chapter 1). Repetition of Oṃ is thus also a contemplation of the whole range of consciousness.

    10.2.2 The Gāyatrī Mantra¶

    The Gāyatrī Mantra (also called the Sāvitrī) is considered the most sacred mantra of the Vedic tradition. The verse itself is Ṛg Veda 3.62.10; the opening om bhūr bhuvaḥ svaḥ (the syllable Oṃ and the three “utterances”, vyāhṛtis) is prefixed in ritual use. Traditionally its recitation was restricted to initiated twice-born men; in the 19th and 20th centuries reform movements such as the Ārya Samāj and many modern teachers opened it to all.

    Gāyatrī Mantra — Ṛg Veda 3.62.10, with the ritual prefix

    om bhūr bhuvaḥ svaḥ
    tat savitur vareṇyaṃ
    bhargo devasya dhīmahi
    dhiyo yo naḥ pracodayāt
    We meditate upon the glorious splendour of the Divine Light (Savitṛ).
    May that Light illuminate our intellects.

    10.3 Methods of Japa (Mantra Repetition)¶

    Japa is the systematic repetition of a mantra. The tradition distinguishes three modes: aloud (vācika), whispered so that the lips move but no sound is heard (upāṃśu), and mental (mānasa). The Manusmṛti (2.85) already ranks them, declaring the sacrifice of muttered prayer ten times better than the prescribed rites, the whispered form a hundred times and the mental a thousand times better; mental japa is accordingly held by the tradition to be the most powerful. Repetitions are usually counted on a rosary (mālā) of 108 beads, and modern devotional practice adds the writing of a mantra many times over (likhita japa). Patañjali’s own instruction on the praṇava concerns meaning as much as sound:

    Yoga Sūtra 1.28

    taj-japas tad-artha-bhāvanam
    The repetition of that (Praṇava) and meditation on its meaning (is the practice).

    Patañjali emphasises that japa must be accompanied by artha-bhāvanam — deep contemplation of the meaning. Mechanical repetition without awareness yields limited results; it is the union of sound and meaning that produces the transformative power of mantra.

    10.4 The Bīja Mantras and the Cakras¶

    In the Ṣaṭcakranirūpaṇa each of the six cakras carries the seed syllable (bīja) of its element — laṃ (earth), vaṃ (water), raṃ (fire), yaṃ (air) and haṃ (space) — with Oṃ at the ājñā; the syllables are installed and meditated upon in the cakras, together with the letters on the petals of each lotus (Table 6.3 in Chapter 6). The psychological “effects” that modern teachers attach to each bīja (grounding, creativity, confidence and so on) are not found in the classical texts.

    Part B: Laya Yoga — The Yoga of Dissolution

    10.5 The Meaning of Laya¶

    Laya means “dissolution”, “absorption” or “merging”, and layayoga is the yoga in which the mind is dissolved in its object until no separate mind remains. The Haṭha texts define the state precisely:

    HYP 4.34

    layo laya iti prāhuḥ kīdṛśaṃ laya-lakṣaṇam
    apunar-vāsanotthānāl layo viṣaya-vismṛtiḥ
    People say “laya, laya” — but what is the mark of laya? Laya is the forgetting of sense-objects, because latent impressions no longer arise.

    In the medieval yoga texts layayoga is one of four yogas, with mantra, haṭha and rāja. The Dattātreyayogaśāstra and the Amaraughaprabodha arrange the four in a hierarchy of practitioners, in which laya stands above mantra and below haṭha and rāja (Chapter 7, Section 7.3.1), and the Yogabīja makes them successive stages of one “great yoga”. The methods the texts group under laya are varied: fixing the mind on points inside or outside the body, gazes such as the śāmbhavī mudrā, in which the eyes are open but attention is turned inward, and above all listening to the inner sound. The Haṭhapradīpikā says that Ādinātha taught a vast number of methods of laya, but that attention to the inner sound (nādānusandhāna) is the foremost of them (4.66), and it treats laya, the “no-mind” state (amanaska), Rāja Yoga and samādhi as names for one goal (4.3–4) (Birch 2011; Mallinson and Singleton 2017).

    10.5.1 The Dissolution of the Elements¶

    A second meaning of laya comes from Tantric ritual. In the rite of purifying the elements (bhūtaśuddhi), which precedes worship in many Tantras, the practitioner dissolves the elements of his body in imagination, each into its subtler source — earth into water, water into fire, fire into air, air into ether, ether into mind and mind into pure consciousness — and then rebuilds a divine body in which the deity can be worshipped. The ascent of kuṇḍalinī through the cakras, each the seat of an element, was read in the same terms (Chapter 6). The modern presentation of “Laya Yoga” as a systematic dissolution of the elements through the cakras combines this Tantric rite with the medieval yoga of absorption; the classical sources keep them apart.

    Part C: Nāda Yoga — The Yoga of Inner Sound

    10.6 The Meaning of Nāda¶

    Nāda literally means “sound” or “vibration.” Nāda Yoga is the discipline of attuning to the inner, mystical sound (anāhata nāda — the “unstruck sound”) that arises spontaneously within the body, without any external cause. By following this inner sound deeper and deeper, the mind is drawn inward and eventually dissolves in the supreme silence.

    HYP 4.93

    sarva-cintāṃ parityajya sāvadhānena cetasā
    nāda evānusandheyo yoga-sāmrājyam icchatā
    Abandoning all thoughts, with an attentive mind, one who desires the kingdom of Yoga should concentrate solely on Nāda.

    10.7 The Four Stages of Nāda¶

    The Haṭha Yoga Pradīpikā (4.69–76) describes four progressive stages as the breath pierces the three knots, each marked by a characteristic inner sound (Table 10.2).

    Table 10.2. The Four Stages of Nāda (Inner Sound)
    Stage Name Description of the Sound
    1 Ārambha (Beginning) Brahma granthi pierced; tinkling of ornaments heard in the space of the heart
    2 Ghaṭa (Pot) Viṣṇu granthi pierced; the breath enters the middle channel; the sound of a kettledrum in the throat
    3 Paricaya (Familiarity) The sound of a drum (mardala) in the space between the eyebrows
    4 Niṣpatti (Consummation) Rudra granthi pierced; sounds of flute and vīṇā; the state of Rāja Yoga

    Elsewhere in the same chapter (4.85–86) the HYP describes the sounds heard as practice deepens: at first loud sounds like the ocean, thunder and kettledrums; in the middle, drums, conch and bells; at the end, tinkling bells, flute, vīṇā and the humming of bees.

    10.8 The Practice of Nāda Yoga¶

    The HYP’s instructions are brief. The yogin sits in the “liberated” posture (muktāsana), adopts the inward gaze of śāmbhavī mudrā and listens to the sound in the right ear (4.67); later teachers add the closing of the ears, eyes and mouth with the fingers (ṣaṇmukhī mudrā). At first loud, external-seeming sounds are heard, then subtler ones; the mind, the text says, is drawn to the subtlest sound as a bee to nectar and dissolves in it, and when even that sound ceases the yogin is in samādhi.

    The doctrine of inner sound did not stay within the yoga texts. The Sants and the Sikh Gurus spoke of the “unstruck” sound (anahad) as the sign of the divine name realised within (Chapter 7, Section 7.17). Musicians took it over too. Śārṅgadeva’s Saṅgītaratnākara (13th century), the most influential Sanskrit treatise on music, opens its account of musical sound with a description of the body and its centres and derives audible sound (āhata) from the meeting of breath and the body’s fire, beside the unstruck sound (anāhata) of the yogins; “Nāda Yoga” is still the name by which Indian musicians describe their discipline (Volume 9, Saṅgīta–Nāṭya).

    10.9 The Principal Bīja Mantras¶

    The Bīja Mantras (seed syllables) represent the most concentrated form of sacred sound. Each Bīja is understood not as a word with lexical meaning but as a sonic essence — a vibrational pattern that directly embodies the energy of a specific cosmic principle or deity (Table 10.3).

    Table 10.3. Major Bīja Mantras and Their Deities (the cakra assignments reflect modern teaching and vary between sources)
    Bīja Deity/Principle Cakra Significance
    Oṃ Brahman (the Absolute) Sahasrāra The source of all mantras; the praṇava
    Hrīṃ Māyā Śakti Anāhata The heart bīja; veiling and revealing power
    Śrīṃ Lakṣmī Maṇipūra Abundance, auspiciousness, prosperity
    Klīṃ Kāmadeva / Kṛṣṇa Svādhiṣṭhāna Desire, attraction, creative energy
    Aiṃ Sarasvatī Viśuddha Knowledge, speech, learning
    Hūṃ Śiva / Agni Maṇipūra Destruction of obstacles; fierce protective energy
    Krīṃ Kālī Mūlādhāra Transformative power; dissolution of bondage
    Gaṃ Gaṇeśa Mūlādhāra Removal of obstacles; auspicious beginnings

    Tantric texts give many of these syllables traditional names. Oṃ is the praṇava, regarded as the seed from which all others emerge; Hrīṃ is the “seed of Māyā” or of Bhuvaneśvarī; Klīṃ the “seed of desire” (kāmabīja); Śrīṃ the seed of Śrī or Lakṣmī; and Aiṃ the “seed of speech” (vāgbīja), sacred to Sarasvatī.

    In the ritual use of a mantra, the kīlaka (“pin”) is a component said to “lock” its power, which is released by proper initiation. The ending in nasal resonance (anusvāra, ṃ) is interpreted in Tantric theory as the bindu; claims that bījas have been shown to have specific physiological effects are not supported by research.

    10.10 Mantra and the Guru-Śiṣya Paramparā¶

    In the traditional understanding, a mantra is not effective merely by being read from a book or heard casually. It must be received through Dīkṣā (initiation) from a qualified Guru who stands in an unbroken lineage (Paramparā) of transmission. The Guru imparts not merely the sound but the caitanya (consciousness-energy) of the mantra, awakened through his or her own practice (Table 10.4).

    Table 10.4. Stages of Mantra Transmission and Practice
    Stage Sanskrit Term Description
    Initiation Dīkṣā The Guru transmits the mantra with its specific svara (intonation), nyāsa (ritual placement), and dhyāna-śloka (meditation verse)
    Preliminary practice Puraścaraṇa The practitioner completes the prescribed number of repetitions (often 100,000 per syllable of the mantra) to “awaken” the mantra
    Matured practice Mantra Siddhi Through sustained practice, the mantra becomes spontaneously active in the practitioner’s consciousness, arising without effort
    Integration Ajāpā Japa The mantra merges with the breath and continues spontaneously, even during sleep

    Scholarly Note: The Concept of Mantra Caitanya

    The concept of Mantra Caitanya — the “aliveness” or “awakened consciousness” of a mantra — is central to the Tantric understanding of sacred sound. A mantra without Caitanya is compared to a seed that has been roasted: it retains its external form but has lost the capacity to sprout. The Guru’s role in Dīkṣā is held to be precisely to transmit this living energy, which has been maintained through an unbroken chain of teacher-to-student transmission extending, in principle, back to the original Ṛṣi who first “heard” the mantra in deep meditation.

    10.11 The Integration of Mantra, Laya, and Nāda¶

    Mantra Yoga, Laya Yoga, and Nāda Yoga are intimately related. Mantra provides the external vehicle of sound; Laya provides the process of progressive dissolution; and Nāda provides the inner guide that leads the mind to its final dissolution in the Absolute. Together, they constitute a path of liberation through sound — from the grossest audible chant to the subtlest inaudible vibration, and finally, in the tradition’s own terms, to the silence beyond all sound, which is Brahman itself (compare HYP 4.5, quoted in Chapter 7).
