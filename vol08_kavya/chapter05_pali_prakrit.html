    Chapter 5: Pali, Prakrit and Apabhraṃśa Literature¶

    sabbadānaṃ dhammadānaṃ jināti, sabbarasaṃ dhammaraso jināti,
    sabbaratiṃ dhammarati jināti, taṇhakkhayo sabbadukkhaṃ jināti.
    The gift of the Dhamma surpasses all gifts; the taste of the Dhamma surpasses all tastes; delight in the Dhamma surpasses all delights; the destruction of craving overcomes all suffering.
    — Dhammapada 354

    5.1 Introduction: The Middle Indo-Aryan Literatures¶

    The literatures of this chapter are as old as classical Sanskrit kāvya, and in part older: the Pali canon was taking shape before Aśvaghoṣa wrote, and Hāla’s Prakrit anthology was known to Bāṇa and quoted by the theorists of the next chapter. Alongside Sanskrit, the Indo-Aryan languages of the subcontinent developed through a “middle” stage, the Prakrits (prākṛta, “natural” or “derived”), before giving rise to the modern languages. Several of these became literary languages in their own right. Pali became the scriptural language of Theravāda Buddhism; Ardhamāgadhī and other Prakrits served the Jains; Māhārāṣṭrī was the language of lyric poetry and of songs in drama; and Apabhraṃśa, the latest stage, was used for narrative poetry, especially by Jain authors, from about the sixth to the fifteenth century (Table 5.1).

    Table 5.1. Middle Indo-Aryan literary languages
    Language Principal literary use
    Pali The Theravāda Buddhist canon, commentaries and chronicles
    Ardhamāgadhī The canon of the Śvetāmbara Jains
    Jain Śaurasenī Digambara Jain doctrinal texts
    Māhārāṣṭrī Lyric and court poetry (Hāla, Pravarasena); Jain narrative; verse in drama
    Śaurasenī Prose of women and middle-ranking characters in drama
    Māgadhī Speech of some low-status characters in drama
    Paiśācī Guṇāḍhya’s lost Bṛhatkathā (by tradition)
    Apabhraṃśa Jain narrative poetry; Buddhist and other mystical songs; transitional to the modern languages

    5.1.1 Why Not Sanskrit?¶

    The choice of a literary language was never neutral. Early Buddhist tradition records that the Buddha refused a request to put his teaching into Vedic metre and allowed monks to learn it in their own dialects; whatever the historical basis of the story, it expresses an ideal of accessibility. Jain tradition held that Mahāvīra preached in Ardhamāgadhī, a language understood by all beings. Over time, however, both communities adopted Sanskrit as well: northern Buddhist schools composed their scriptures and philosophy in Sanskrit from the early centuries CE, and Jain scholars wrote in Sanskrit from the fifth century onwards. The Prakrits themselves became learned, standardised languages, studied through grammars such as Vararuci’s Prākṛtaprakāśa and Hemacandra’s grammar (see Volume 10, Śabda Vidyā); Hāla’s Māhārāṣṭrī poetry is as artful as any Sanskrit kāvya. The history of the Middle Indo-Aryan literatures is thus not a simple story of “popular” languages opposed to an “elite” one, but of several literary languages, each with its own prestige and its own communities, interacting over many centuries. Andrew Ollett has argued that Prakrit was, from the start, a language of literary art deliberately set beside Sanskrit, and that the two together formed a single “language order” within which poets chose the register that suited their work (Ollett 2017).

    5.2 Pali Literature¶

    5.2.1 The Tipiṭaka¶

    The Pali canon (Tipiṭaka, “Three Baskets”) consists of the Vinaya Piṭaka (monastic discipline), the Sutta Piṭaka (discourses attributed to the Buddha and his disciples, in five collections or nikāyas) and the Abhidhamma Piṭaka (systematic analysis of doctrine). According to Sri Lankan tradition it was written down in the first century BCE, after centuries of oral transmission. The fifth collection of the Sutta Piṭaka, the Khuddaka Nikāya (“Minor Collection”), contains most of the canon’s poetry. K. R. Norman’s Pāli Literature and Oskar von Hinüber’s Handbook of Pāli Literature are the standard surveys (Norman 1983; Hinüber 1996). This chapter treats the canon as literature; its doctrine and history, and those of the Jain canon below, are the subject of Volume 4, Buddha–Jina Prabhā.

    5.2.2 The Dhammapada¶

    The Dhammapada (“Verses on Dhamma”), with 423 verses in 26 chapters (vagga), is the best known of all Pali texts. As literature it belongs to the Indian tradition of gnomic verse: most of its stanzas are in the same śloka metre as the Sanskrit epics, and many have close parallels in the Gāndhārī Dharmapada, the Sanskrit Udānavarga and even in Brahmanical and Jain maxims. Its art is one of compression and pairing. The first chapter, “Pairs” (Yamaka), sets verse against verse, the corrupted mind against the pure one; its famous opening stanza on the primacy of the mind is discussed as psychology in Volume 22, Manas and Śikṣā, and as a specimen of Pali grammar in Volume 10, Śabda Vidyā. A few stanzas later comes the most quoted of all:

    Dhammapada 5

    na hi verena verāni sammantīdha kudācanaṃ |
    averena ca sammanti esa dhammo sanantano ||
    Never here are hatreds stilled by hatred; they are stilled by non-hatred. This is the eternal law.

    The stanza turns on a single word repeated and then negated (vera, avera), and it closes with a phrase, dhammo sanantano, that Brahmanical texts used for their own eternal law. Elsewhere a whole doctrine is distilled into a single observation:

    Dhammapada 277

    sabbe saṅkhārā aniccā ti yadā paññāya passati |
    atha nibbindati dukkhe esa maggo visuddhiyā ||
    “All conditioned things are impermanent”: when one sees this with wisdom, one turns away from suffering. This is the path to purity.

    Table 5.2. Some chapters of the Dhammapada
    Ch. Vagga Verses Theme
    1 Yamaka (Pairs) 20 Paired verses contrasting wholesome and unwholesome minds
    2 Appamāda (Vigilance) 12 Heedfulness as the path to the deathless
    3 Citta (Mind) 11 Taming the restless mind
    5 Bāla (The Fool) 16 The ways of the foolish
    6 Paṇḍita (The Wise) 14 The ways of the wise
    14 Buddha 18 The Buddhas and their teaching
    20 Magga (The Path) 17 The eightfold path
    26 Brāhmaṇa 41 The true brahmin is defined by conduct, not birth

    Some of its chapters are summarised in Table 5.2. The last chapter is notable for redefining the word brāhmaṇa: the true brahmin is not one born of a brahmin mother, but one who is free from attachment and has destroyed the defilements — an implicit challenge to the hereditary claims of the Brahmanical order.

    5.2.3 Theragāthā and Therīgāthā¶

    The Theragāthā (“Verses of the Elder Monks”) and Therīgāthā (“Verses of the Elder Nuns”) are anthologies of poems attributed to early disciples who attained liberation. The Therīgāthā contains 73 poems (about 520 verses) attributed to nuns. Its poems were composed over several centuries and their attributions are traditional, but they form one of the earliest anthologies of poems in women’s voices in any literature. They speak of the hardships of women’s lives — domestic drudgery, marriage, the death of children, ageing — and of the freedom found in renunciation. The mother of Sumaṅgala rejoices at being free of the pestle, the cooking pots and a husband she did not respect; Paṭācārā, who lost her husband, children and parents in a single catastrophe, finds peace; the former courtesan Ambapālī contrasts the beauty of her youth with her aged body in a refrain of impermanence. Charles Hallisey’s translation is the most recent in English (Hallisey 2015).

    The Theragāthā, the larger collection (264 poems, about 1,280 verses), is equally rich. Its monks speak of the joy of solitude in the forest during the rains, with peacocks calling and clouds thundering, of the struggle against desire and pride, and of the peace of liberation. Some poems are vivid autobiographies — of a former brahmin, a former king, a former outcaste scavenger, Sunīta, who tells how he was despised by all until the Buddha received him into the order. Together the two collections form one of the earliest bodies of religious lyric in India, and their imagery of nature and of inner struggle anticipates much later Indian poetry.

    5.2.4 The Jātaka¶

    The Jātaka (“Birth Stories”) are tales of the previous lives of the Buddha as a bodhisatta, in which he appears as a king, a merchant, a brahmin, an outcaste, an animal or a tree-spirit. Only the verses are canonical; the prose stories in which they are set are preserved in a commentary of about the fifth century CE, which contains 547 stories. Each story follows a set pattern: a “story of the present” that gives the occasion for telling; the “story of the past”; the verses; a word commentary; and a final “connection” in which the Buddha identifies the characters of the past with people of his own time.

    Many Jātaka stories are folk tales and fables shared with the Pañcatantra and other collections. They were carved on the railing of the stūpa at Bharhut (second century BCE) and the gateways of the great stūpa at Sāñcī (first century BCE), painted in the caves of Ajaṇṭā, and illustrated at Borobudur in Java. The last ten stories, the “Great Birth Stories” (Mahānipāta), are each associated in Theravāda tradition with one of the ten perfections (pāramī; Table 5.3).

    Table 5.3. The ten “Great Birth” stories and their perfections (Theravāda tradition)
    Jātaka Perfection Story
    Temiya Renunciation (nekkhamma) A prince feigns dumbness for years to avoid becoming king
    Mahājanaka Energy (viriya) A shipwrecked prince swims for seven days
    Sāma Loving-kindness (mettā) A devoted son, caring for his blind parents, is shot by a king
    Nemi Resolution (adhiṭṭhāna) A righteous king is shown the heavens and hells
    Mahosadha Wisdom (paññā) A brilliant counsellor outwits rival ministers and a hostile king
    Bhūridatta Morality (sīla) A serpent prince keeps his vows though captured by a snake-charmer
    Candakumāra Patience (khanti) A prince is to be sacrificed through a wicked priest’s scheming
    Nārada Equanimity (upekkhā) A Brahmā god cures a king of a false doctrine
    Vidhura Truthfulness (sacca) A wise minister, lost at dice, teaches his captor
    Vessantara Generosity (dāna) A prince gives away his possessions, his children and his wife

    The Vessantara Jātaka, the longest, is the most popular story in the Theravāda world, recited and performed at festivals in Sri Lanka and mainland Southeast Asia. Its hero’s gift of his wife and children to a cruel brahmin has troubled readers for two thousand years, and the story has generated a long tradition of debate on the ethics of giving.

    5.2.5 An Example: The Hare in the Moon¶

    The Sasa Jātaka (no. 316) shows how simple and how powerful these stories can be. The future Buddha is born as a hare who lives in a forest with three friends, a monkey, a jackal and an otter. On a holy day the four resolve to give alms to anyone who asks. The god Sakka (Indra), wishing to test them, comes in the form of a hungry brahmin. The otter offers fish, the jackal meat and curds, and the monkey mangoes; but the hare, who eats only grass, has nothing to give, and so tells the brahmin to light a fire, into which he leaps to offer his own body as food. The fire does not burn him. Sakka reveals himself and, so that the hare’s virtue may be known throughout the age, paints his image on the face of the moon. The story explains the familiar “hare in the moon” of Indian folklore, and it was retold in Sanskrit by Āryaśūra and depicted in Buddhist art across Asia.

    5.2.6 The Sutta Nipāta¶

    The Sutta Nipāta, another collection of the Khuddaka Nikāya, contains some of the oldest Buddhist poetry. Its fourth and fifth books, the Aṭṭhakavagga and Pārāyanavagga, are considered by many scholars to be among the earliest strata of the canon; they present a radical ideal of the wandering sage free from all views and attachments. The collection also includes the Mettasutta on loving-kindness, still recited daily in Theravāda countries, and verse narratives of the Buddha’s early life that differ from the later biographies.

    5.2.7 Other Pali Literature¶

    The Milindapañha (“Questions of Milinda”), a dialogue between the Indo-Greek king Menander (second century BCE) and the monk Nāgasena, explains Buddhist doctrine through vivid similes; the famous image of the chariot, which is nothing apart from its parts, illustrates the doctrine of no-self. In Sri Lanka, the commentaries of Buddhaghosa (fifth century) and the chronicles Dīpavaṃsa and Mahāvaṃsa (fourth to sixth century) carried Pali literature into history-writing, and Pali kāvya continued to be composed there and in Myanmar and Thailand into modern times.

    5.2.8 The Chronicles of Sri Lanka¶

    The Mahāvaṃsa (“Great Chronicle”), composed in Pali verse by the monk Mahānāma in perhaps the fifth or sixth century, tells the history of Buddhism and of the kings of Sri Lanka from the Buddha’s legendary visits to the island to the fourth century CE. Its central hero is King Duṭṭhagāmaṇī, who defeats the Tamil king Eḷāra and builds the great stūpas of Anurādhapura. The chronicle was continued by later authors (the Cūḷavaṃsa) down to the end of the Kandyan kingdom in 1815. Like Kalhaṇa’s Rājataraṅgiṇī (Chapter 2), it is a work of history written within a religious and literary frame; in modern times its account of the war between Duṭṭhagāmaṇī and Eḷāra has been read as a charter for ethnic and religious nationalism, a use its monastic authors could not have foreseen.

    5.2.9 Sanskrit Buddhist Literature¶

    Buddhists also wrote extensively in Sanskrit. Besides Aśvaghoṣa (Chapter 2), notable authors include Mātṛceṭa, whose hymns to the Buddha were widely recited; Āryaśūra, whose Jātakamālā retold thirty-four birth stories in polished prose and verse; and Śāntideva (eighth century), whose Bodhicaryāvatāra (“Entering the Path of Awakening”) combines philosophy with intense devotional poetry. The avadāna literature, such as the Divyāvadāna, collects edifying stories, including legends of Aśoka. Much of this literature survives only in Tibetan and Chinese translation.

    5.3 Jain Literature in Prakrit¶

    5.3.1 The Canon¶

    The Śvetāmbara Jains preserve a canon of some forty-five texts in Ardhamāgadhī, fixed, according to tradition, at a council at Valabhī in the fifth century CE: eleven Aṅgas (a twelfth is said to have been lost), twelve Upāṅgas, ten Prakīrṇakas, six Chedasūtras, four Mūlasūtras and two independent texts. The Digambaras do not accept this canon as authentic; their authoritative texts, such as the Ṣaṭkhaṇḍāgama and the works of Kundakunda, are in Jain Śaurasenī. Umāsvāti’s Tattvārthasūtra (c. fourth–fifth century), the systematic summary of Jain doctrine accepted by both traditions, is written in Sanskrit.

    The canonical texts include passages of literary quality: the Ācārāṅga’s description of Mahāvīra’s austerities, the parables of the Uttarādhyayana, and the stories of the Nāyādhammakahāo.

    5.3.2 Jain Narrative¶

    Jain authors produced an immense narrative literature, retelling the epic stories in Jain terms and creating new romances to illustrate the workings of karma. Vimalasūri’s Paümacariya (date disputed, perhaps the early centuries CE), the earliest Jain Rāmāyaṇa, rejects as absurd the idea that Rāvaṇa had ten heads or that monkeys built a bridge: the rākṣasas and vānaras are human clans of vidyādharas, Rāvaṇa is a pious but flawed king killed by Lakṣmaṇa, and Rāma ends as a Jain monk who attains liberation. Other major works include Saṅghadāsa’s Vasudevahiṇḍī, a Prakrit version of the Bṛhatkathā material; Haribhadra’s Samarāiccakahā (eighth century), a story of enmity pursued through nine births; and Uddyotana’s Kuvalayamālā (779 CE), a romance famous for its use of many languages and its descriptions of the speech of eighteen regional peoples.

    5.3.3 Women in Jain Literature¶

    Jain literature gives women a prominent but contested place. The Jain order admitted nuns from the beginning, and nuns have generally outnumbered monks, both in the traditional figures for Mahāvīra’s community and today; stories of exemplary women — Candanā, the slave girl who became the head of Mahāvīra’s order of nuns; Rājīmatī, the bride abandoned by the tīrthaṅkara Neminātha on their wedding day, who followed him into renunciation and rebuked his brother’s advances; the sixteen “virtuous women” (satī) of later tradition — are retold in Prakrit, Sanskrit and the regional languages. Yet the two main Jain traditions disagreed fundamentally about women’s spiritual capacity. The Śvetāmbaras held that women can attain liberation and even that the nineteenth tīrthaṅkara, Mallī, was a woman; the Digambaras held that liberation requires nudity, impossible for women, so that a woman must first be reborn as a man. The debate produced a substantial literature from the early centuries CE onwards. Much Jain narrative also contains warnings against women as sources of attachment, so that its picture of women combines reverence for female ascetics with ascetic misogyny.

    5.3.4 Jain Literature in the Regional Languages¶

    Jain writers played a major part in the early history of several regional literatures. In Tamil, Jain authors are credited with the Nālaṭiyār, the epic Cīvakacintāmaṇi, and grammars and lexicons; in Kannada, the classical campū poets Pampa, Ponna and Ranna were Jains, and the tenth-century Jain laywoman Attimabbe, a patron of literature, is said to have commissioned a thousand copies of Ponna’s Śāntipurāṇa for distribution; in Gujarat and Rajasthan, Jain monks wrote the earliest poems in the emerging regional language, the narrative and didactic rāsas, and Jain manuscript libraries (bhaṇḍār) at Patan, Jaisalmer and elsewhere preserved not only Jain texts but also many works of Brahmanical and secular literature that might otherwise have been lost. The contribution of Jain scholarship and patronage to Indian literature as a whole is out of all proportion to the size of the community.

    5.4 Prakrit Court Poetry¶

    5.4.1 Hāla’s Sattasaī¶

    The Gāhāsattasaī (Sanskrit Gāthāsaptaśatī, “Seven Hundred Verses”), an anthology of short poems in Māhārāṣṭrī Prakrit in the gāthā (āryā) metre, is attributed to Hāla, a king of the Sātavāhana dynasty of the Deccan. The anthology grew over time, and its present form probably dates from around the third to fifth century, though it may contain older material; its recensions differ widely in content. Each stanza is a tiny drama, usually spoken by a woman — a young wife, a mother-in-law, a friend, a village girl — in a world of fields, rivers, threshing floors and hunting, in which love, desire, jealousy and wit are expressed obliquely. Sanskrit theorists of suggestion drew many of their examples from it (Section 6.5), and Govardhana’s Sanskrit Āryāsaptaśatī and Bihārī’s Hindi Satsaī follow its model. Peter Khoroche and Herman Tieken have translated it in full (Khoroche and Tieken 2009).

    5.4.2 Prakrit Mahākāvyas and Romances¶

    Prakrit was also used for court epics and romances on the Sanskrit model; some of the principal works are listed in Table 5.4.

    5.5 Apabhraṃśa Literature¶

    Apabhraṃśa (“fallen away”, a grammarians’ term for language that deviates from Sanskrit norms) is the name given to the last stage of Middle Indo-Aryan, used in literature from about the sixth century. Its literature is largely Jain, but it also includes the mystical songs and couplets (dohā) of Buddhist Tantric siddhas such as Saraha, which are related to the Old Bengali Caryāpada (Chapter 11), and secular poetry (Table 5.5).

    Table 5.5. Major Apabhraṃśa works
    Work Author Date Remarks
    Paümacariu, Riṭṭhaṇemicariu Svayambhū 9th c. (?) Jain Rāmāyaṇa and Jain Kṛṣṇa–Neminātha story
    Mahāpurāṇa Puṣpadanta 10th c. Lives of the 63 great men of Jain tradition; written at the Rāṣṭrakūṭa capital Mānyakheṭa
    Ṇāyakumāracariu, Jasaharacariu Puṣpadanta 10th c. Shorter Jain narratives
    Bhavisattakaha Dhanapāla c. 10th c. The adventures of a merchant’s son
    Dohā collections Saraha and other siddhas c. 8th–12th c. Buddhist mystical couplets
    Saṃdeśarāsaka ʿAbd ur-Raḥmān (Addahamāṇa) 12th–13th c. A messenger poem in the tradition of the Meghadūta, by a Muslim poet

    The Apabhraṃśa epic used a new verse form, the kaḍavaka, a group of rhyming couplets closed by a refrain-like stanza; rhyme, almost absent from classical Sanskrit verse, became a characteristic of Apabhraṃśa and of the modern Indo-Aryan languages that followed. The Saṃdeśarāsaka is notable as a work of the twelfth or thirteenth century by a Muslim author writing within the Indian poetic tradition.

    5.6 Hemacandra¶

    The Jain monk Hemacandra (1088/89–1172/73), who enjoyed the patronage of the Caulukya kings Jayasiṃha Siddharāja and Kumārapāla of Gujarat, was one of the most prolific scholars of medieval India. His grammar, the Siddhahemaśabdānuśāsana, treats Sanskrit in its first seven chapters and the Prakrits and Apabhraṃśa in the eighth; its illustrations of Apabhraṃśa include many fine dohās, some of which are among the earliest poetry in a form close to Gujarati and Hindi. He also wrote a work on poetics (Kāvyānuśāsana), lexicons, a treatise on yoga, a “double-purpose” poem (Dvyāśraya) that illustrates his grammar while narrating the history of his patrons’ dynasty, and the vast Sanskrit Triṣaṣṭiśalākāpuruṣacarita, the lives of the sixty-three great men of Jain tradition.

    5.7 Prakrit and Apabhraṃśa in the Later Literatures¶

    These literatures reappear throughout the rest of the book. The theorists of the next chapter took many of their finest examples of suggestion from Hāla’s anthology. The Jātaka shares its stories with the Pañcatantra (Section 8.12). The Apabhraṃśa dohā and the rhyming kaḍavaka passed into the devotional poetry of Kabīr and Tulsīdās (Chapter 9). And the Jain poets of Gujarat, Rajasthan and Karnataka wrote the first works of several regional literatures (Chapters 10 and 11).
