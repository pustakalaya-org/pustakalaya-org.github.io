    Chapter 4: Sanskrit Prose: Gadya Kāvya, Story Literature and Campū¶

    4.1 Prose as Art¶

    Prose was used in Sanskrit from the Vedic Brāhmaṇas onwards for ritual exegesis, philosophy, grammar and commentary. Artistic prose (gadya kāvya), however, is a classical creation. A well-known saying calls prose “the touchstone of poets” (gadyaṃ kavīnāṃ nikaṣaṃ vadanti): without the support of metre the writer must sustain rhythm, sound and imagery through word-choice and syntax alone. The great works of Sanskrit prose are few, but they are among the most demanding and distinctive works of the literature.

    The theorists distinguished two kinds of prose narrative. Bhāmaha describes the ākhyāyikā as a story told by the hero himself, based on real events, divided into chapters called ucchvāsa and containing verses in particular metres; the kathā is told by someone other than the hero, is invented, and lacks these features. Daṇḍin rejected the distinction as artificial, observing that the supposed rules are not followed in practice and that the two are a single genre under two names. In later usage Bāṇa’s Harṣacarita was regarded as the model ākhyāyikā and his Kādambarī as the model kathā.

    4.2 Subandhu: Vāsavadattā¶

    The earliest surviving prose romance is the Vāsavadattā of Subandhu (sixth century), who is praised by Bāṇa and therefore preceded him. Its slight plot — Prince Kandarpaketu sees a princess in a dream, finds and elopes with her, loses her when she is turned to stone, and recovers her — serves as a frame for descriptions saturated with double meanings. Subandhu boasts in his introduction that his work is a composition with a pun in every syllable (pratyakṣaraśleṣamayaprabandha). The result is a virtuoso display of śleṣa that delighted learned readers and set a standard for ornate prose.

    4.3 Bāṇa¶

    Bāṇa (Bāṇabhaṭṭa), who lived at the court of Harṣavardhana of Kanauj (r. 606–647), is the greatest master of Sanskrit prose. His two works are very different in subject but share a style of extraordinary richness: long compounds that can extend over many lines, chains of similes and double meanings, sustained alliteration, and a constant play of learned allusion.

    4.3.1 Harṣacarita¶

    The Harṣacarita (“The Deeds of Harṣa”), in eight chapters (ucchvāsa, “breaths”), is the earliest major Sanskrit prose biography of a historical ruler and an important source for seventh-century history. It is unfinished in the sense that it stops long before Harṣa’s reign is fully described.

    The first two chapters tell of Bāṇa’s mythological ancestry and of his own early life. Orphaned young, he wandered with a band of friends that included poets, musicians, a snake-doctor and ascetics of several sects, until he was summoned to Harṣa’s court, where he was at first received coolly; this is one of the few extended autobiographical narratives in Sanskrit. The third and fourth chapters turn to the ancestry of the Puṣyabhūti kings of Thanesar, the birth of Rājyavardhana, Harṣa and their sister Rājyaśrī, and Rājyaśrī’s marriage to the Maukhari king Grahavarman. Then disaster follows disaster: their father Prabhākaravardhana dies and their mother burns herself; Grahavarman is murdered; Rājyavardhana defeats the king of Mālava but is treacherously killed by the king of Gauḍa; and Harṣa vows to rid the earth of the Gauḍa king. The last two chapters follow Harṣa’s march and the search for Rājyaśrī, who has fled to the Vindhya forest and is rescued, with the help of the Buddhist teacher Divākaramitra, at the moment she is about to enter the funeral fire.

    The work’s set descriptions — of a royal camp, of a dying king’s bedside, of a forest settlement of hunters, of Divākaramitra’s hermitage where followers of many sects live together — give an unrivalled picture of the society of the time. Cowell and Thomas’s translation (1897) is still useful (Cowell and Thomas 1897).

    As a historical source the Harṣacarita must be used with care: it is a panegyric, written to glorify Harṣa, and it presents events through the conventions of kāvya. Its account can, however, be checked against inscriptions of Harṣa and against the record of the Chinese Buddhist pilgrim Xuanzang, who visited Harṣa’s court in the 640s and describes his great religious assemblies at Kanauj and Prayāga. The two sources agree on the main outlines of the reign and differ in emphasis — the pilgrim stressing Harṣa’s patronage of Buddhism, the poet his Brahmanical and royal virtues — and together they make Harṣa one of the best-documented rulers of early India.

    4.3.2 Kādambarī¶

    The Kādambarī, a kathā based loosely on a tale from the Bṛhatkathā, is a romance spanning several lifetimes, told through narratives nested inside one another. Bāṇa died before completing it; the second part (Uttarabhāga) was written by his son, whose name is given variously as Bhūṣaṇabhaṭṭa or Pulinda, and who imitates his father’s style closely. Gwendolyn Layne’s translation conveys the whole (Layne 1991). Table 4.1 sets out its nested narratives.

    Table 4.1. The nested narratives of the Kādambarī
    Level Narrator Content
    1 Bāṇa A Caṇḍāla maiden brings a learned parrot, Vaiśampāyana, to the court of King Śūdraka of Vidiśā
    2 The parrot His birth in the Vindhya forest, his rescue as a fledgling, and his upbringing in the hermitage of the sage Jābāli
    3 Jābāli (as reported by the parrot) The story of Prince Candrāpīḍa of Ujjayinī and his friend Vaiśampāyana, the minister’s son
    4 Mahāśvetā (within Jābāli’s story) Her love for the young ascetic Puṇḍarīka, who died of longing for her, and her vow to wait for his return
    3 (cont.) Jābāli Candrāpīḍa’s love for Mahāśvetā’s friend, the Gandharva princess Kādambarī; the deaths of Vaiśampāyana and Candrāpīḍa
    Resolution (Uttarabhāga) Śūdraka is revealed as Candrāpīḍa reborn, the parrot as Puṇḍarīka (reborn as Vaiśampāyana), and the Caṇḍāla maiden as the goddess Lakṣmī; the curses end and both pairs of lovers are united

    The Kādambarī is a work about longing, memory and the persistence of love across death and rebirth. Its descriptions — of the Vindhya forest, of Ujjayinī, of the lake Acchoda and of Mahāśvetā practising austerities in a cave — became models for later writers, and the title itself became a common noun (see below). Comparisons with modern European novelists, often made, should not obscure how different the work’s premises are: its interest lies less in individual psychology than in the play of emotion and description within a cosmic order of curses and rebirths.

    4.3.3 Bāṇa’s Style¶

    Bāṇa’s sentences can extend over several pages: a single main verb may be preceded by dozens of descriptive compounds, each a miniature image, arranged in patterns of sound and sense. A description of a forest, a city or a woman is built up by accumulation, each phrase adding a simile, a double meaning or a learned allusion, until the object described is seen from every side. Poets and critics of later centuries counted Bāṇa among the masters, and a well-known saying claims that “all the world is Bāṇa’s leftovers” (bāṇocchiṣṭaṃ jagat sarvam), meaning that he left nothing for later writers to describe; modern readers have sometimes found the style exhausting. Read slowly and aloud, as it was meant to be, it achieves effects of richness and rhythm unlike anything else in Indian literature. Table 4.2 compares his prose with Subandhu’s.

    Table 4.2. Subandhu and Bāṇa compared
    Feature Subandhu, Vāsavadattā Bāṇa, Kādambarī
    Length Short Long; completed by the author’s son
    Narrative Simple, linear plot Several lives and nested narrators
    Style Dominated by śleṣa Long compounds, simile, description; śleṣa used more selectively
    Characters Thinly drawn Fuller portrayal of emotion, especially in Mahāśvetā
    Influence Model of ornate wordplay Model for later romance; word for “novel” in Marathi and Kannada

    4.4 Daṇḍin: Daśakumāracarita¶

    The Daśakumāracarita (“The Adventures of the Ten Princes”) is generally ascribed to Daṇḍin, author of the Kāvyādarśa (c. 700), though the identity of the two has been questioned. The core of the work survives with an introduction (Pūrvapīṭhikā) and conclusion (Uttarapīṭhikā) written by other hands to complete it. The defeated King Rājahaṃsa of Magadha takes refuge in the Vindhya forest, where his son Rājavāhana grows up with nine companions. The ten young men are separated and, on meeting again, each tells his adventures. The narrators include Rājavāhana, Apahāravarman, Upahāravarman, Arthapāla, Pramati, Mitragupta, Mantragupta and Viśruta; the episodes of Somadatta and Puṣpodbhava are told in the added introduction.

    The stories take place in a world of gamblers, thieves, courtesans, merchants, corrupt ascetics and scheming ministers. The princes win kingdoms and wives by trickery as often as by valour, and the narrative treats this with relish. Daṇḍin’s prose is lighter and more varied than Bāṇa’s; the most famous tour de force is Mantragupta’s narrative, which avoids all labial consonants because, the story explains, his lips are sore from his lover’s kisses. Isabelle Onians’s translation brings out the work’s wit (Onians 2005).

    4.5 Story Literature¶

    Beside the ornate prose of the court there flourished a vast literature of tales (kathā), simpler in style, often mixing prose with verse maxims, and connected with oral storytelling.

    4.5.1 The Bṛhatkathā and Its Descendants¶

    The Bṛhatkathā (“Great Story”) of Guṇāḍhya, said to have been composed in the Paiśācī Prakrit, is lost; its date is uncertain, perhaps the early centuries CE. It is known through later adaptations: the Nepalese Bṛhatkathāślokasaṃgraha of Budhasvāmin (date uncertain, perhaps eighth or ninth century), and two eleventh-century Kashmiri versions, Kṣemendra’s Bṛhatkathāmañjarī and Somadeva’s Kathāsaritsāgara. The frame story concerns Naravāhanadatta, son of King Udayana of Vatsa, and his rise to become emperor of the semi-divine vidyādharas.

    4.5.2 Somadeva’s Kathāsaritsāgara¶

    The Kathāsaritsāgara (“Ocean of the Rivers of Story”), composed in verse by Somadeva between about 1063 and 1081 for Queen Sūryamatī of Kashmir, is divided into eighteen books (lambaka) and 124 chapters (taraṅga, “waves”), with over 21,000 verses. Around the frame story it gathers hundreds of tales of every kind: romances, adventures, fables, stories of clever thieves and foolish brahmins, and tales of magic and the supernatural. C. H. Tawney’s translation, edited with extensive notes by N. M. Penzer, runs to ten volumes (Tawney 1924).

    The best-known cycle within it is the Vetālapañcaviṃśati (“Twenty-Five Tales of the Vetāla”). King Trivikramasena is asked by an ascetic to bring him a corpse hanging from a tree in a cremation ground. The corpse is possessed by a vetāla, a spirit, which tells the king a story as he carries it and ends with a riddle; if the king knows the answer and stays silent his head will burst, but if he speaks the vetāla returns to its tree. The riddles turn on questions of justice, obligation and identity. The cycle also circulated separately and was retold in many languages, in later versions with King Vikramāditya as hero.

    4.5.3 An Example: The Transposed Heads¶

    One of the best-known riddles of the Vetālapañcaviṃśati shows how these stories turn on questions of identity and law. A young wife, travelling with her husband and her brother, stops at a temple of the goddess. The husband, overcome by devotion, cuts off his own head as an offering; the brother, finding him, does the same. The wife, about to kill herself in despair, is stopped by the goddess, who tells her to put the heads back on the bodies and they will live. In her haste she puts each head on the wrong body. The vetāla asks: which of the two men is now her husband? The king answers that the one with the husband’s head is her husband, since the head is the chief of the limbs and the seat of identity. The tale was retold many times; Thomas Mann’s novella Die vertauschten Köpfe (1940) and Girish Karnad’s Kannada play Hayavadana (1971) are modern versions.

    4.5.4 Jain Story Literature¶

    Jain monks were among the most prolific writers of narrative, in Prakrit (Chapter 5) and in Sanskrit alike. Haribhadra’s Dhūrtākhyāna (eighth century, in Prakrit) is a satire in which five rogues compete in telling impossible tales, each of which turns out to have a parallel in the Purāṇas — a pointed critique of Brahmanical mythology. Siddharṣi’s Upamitibhavaprapañcā Kathā (906 CE) is a vast allegory in which the soul’s wanderings through the cycle of rebirth are told as the adventures of a beggar in a city whose inhabitants are the passions and virtues. Hemacandra’s Pariśiṣṭaparvan (twelfth century) collects legends of the Jain patriarchs, including stories of Candragupta Maurya and Cāṇakya. Jain narrative literature, often addressed to merchant audiences, is notable for its realism about trade, travel and family life.

    4.5.5 Other Story Collections¶

    Other widely read collections include the Siṃhāsanadvātriṃśikā (“Thirty-Two Tales of the Throne”), in which statuettes on Vikramāditya’s throne tell of his virtues, and the Śukasaptati, described below. The Pañcatantra and Hitopadeśa, the most influential of all, are discussed with the wisdom literature in Chapter 8.

    Frame Narratives The “frame story”, in which a narrative contains stories told by its characters, which may themselves contain further stories, is characteristic of Indian story literature from the Mahābhārata onwards. Through Persian and Arabic translations of the Pañcatantra the technique reached the Islamic world and Europe. The frame of the Thousand and One Nights is often traced to Indian models, and it has been argued that Indian collections influenced medieval European frame-tale collections; direct lines of influence on works such as Boccaccio’s Decameron remain debated.

    4.5.6 The Parrot’s Seventy Tales¶

    The Śukasaptati (“Seventy Tales of the Parrot”) shows the frame story used with comic ingenuity. A merchant’s son goes abroad on business, leaving his young wife with a learned parrot. Bored and tempted, the wife sets out each evening to meet a lover; each evening the parrot, rather than forbidding her, asks how she would extricate herself from a situation like that of some heroine of a story — and then tells the story, so absorbingly that dawn comes before she can leave. Many of the tales concern clever women who escape discovery by quick wit, so that the parrot teaches resourcefulness even while preventing infidelity. The collection, which survives in a “simple” and an “ornate” recension, was translated into Persian as the Ṭūṭīnāma (“Parrot Book”; Section 12.5), of which a richly illustrated copy was made for the young Akbar, and from Persian into Turkish and other languages.

    4.5.7 Prose in the Regional Languages¶

    Artistic and narrative prose appeared early in several regional languages. The Kannada Vaḍḍārādhane (tenth century), nineteen stories of Jain ascetics, is a landmark of early Dravidian prose; the Marathi Līḷācaritra (c. 1278), episodes from the life of the Mahānubhāva founder Cakradhar, preserves a lively colloquial idiom; Tamil developed a sophisticated prose in the commentaries on classical poetry, such as Naccinārkkiṉiyar’s, and in the maṇipravāḷam commentaries of the Śrīvaiṣṇavas on the Āḻvār hymns; and Braj and Rajasthani prose chronicles (khyāt, vārtā) recorded dynastic history and the lives of Vaiṣṇava devotees. These traditions, rather than Sanskrit court prose alone, formed the background against which the modern prose of the nineteenth century developed.

    4.6 The Campū: Prose and Verse Combined¶

    The campū is a narrative kāvya in which prose and verse alternate, both being fully ornate. Daṇḍin’s Kāvyādarśa already names it as a mixed composition of prose and verse, but the earliest surviving examples date from the tenth century; from then on it remained popular in Sanskrit until the nineteenth century, and in Kannada and Telugu several early classics take this form (Table 4.3).

    The form is not simply prose interrupted by verse. In a well-made campū the two media divide the work between them. The prose, in long periods rich in compounds like Bāṇa’s (Section 4.3), carries extended description — a city, a forest, a season, a royal assembly — and often sustains a double meaning through whole sentences; the verses, in a variety of classical metres, mark the points of emotional intensity, sum up a scene, give a speech its edge, or move the story quickly over ground that the prose would linger on. Readers trained in both kinds of kāvya could enjoy the changes of pace and texture, and the campū was regarded as a test of a poet’s command of both.

    Table 4.3. Some notable campū works
    Work Author Date Subject
    Nalacampū Trivikramabhaṭṭa early 10th c. The story of Nala and Damayantī
    Yaśastilaka Somadeva Sūri 959 CE Jain romance of King Yaśodhara, with a manual of Jain lay ethics
    Vikramārjunavijaya Pampa (Kannada) 941 CE The Mahābhārata (Chapter 10)
    Rāmāyaṇacampū attributed to Bhoja 11th c. The Rāma story
    Varadāmbikāpariṇaya Tirumalāmbā 16th c. The marriage of the Vijayanagara king Acyutarāya, by a woman poet of his court
    Gopālacampū Jīva Gosvāmin late 16th c. The life of Kṛṣṇa, as Gauḍīya Vaiṣṇava devotional narrative
    Nīlakaṇṭhavijaya Nīlakaṇṭha Dīkṣita 1637 The churning of the ocean and Śiva’s drinking of the poison
    Viśvaguṇādarśa Veṅkaṭādhvarin 17th c. Two celestial travellers debate the merits and faults of the places and people of India

    Trivikramabhaṭṭa’s Nalacampū, also called the Damayantīkathā and the earliest surviving Sanskrit campū, was written at the Rāṣṭrakūṭa court in the early tenth century; its author is probably the Trivikrama who composed a copper-plate grant of the Rāṣṭrakūṭa king Indra III dated 915. It retells the story of Nala and Damayantī from the Mahābhārata in prose so saturated with double meaning (śleṣa) that a great many of its sentences can be read in two ways, and it breaks off in the seventh chapter, long before the end of the story. It shows the campū at its most virtuosic, a display of what Sanskrit could be made to do.

    Somadeva Sūri’s Yaśastilaka (959), written by a Digambara Jain monk at the court of a Cāḷukya feudatory of the Rāṣṭrakūṭas in present-day Telangana, is the most ambitious. Its story concerns King Yaśodhara, who, to avert the omen of a bad dream, sacrifices not a living animal but a cock made of flour, and is nonetheless condemned, with his mother, to a long series of animal births for the intention of killing; the tale thus dramatises the Jain teaching that the intention to harm is itself violence. Around this narrative Somadeva built an encyclopaedia: descriptions of court life, debates between the religious and philosophical schools of his day, discussions of polity and the arts, and, in the last three of its eight books, a full manual of the duties of the Jain layman. K. K. Handiqui’s study of the work remains a basic source for the cultural history of tenth-century India (Handiqui 1949). Somadeva also wrote the Nītivākyāmṛta, a treatise on polity in aphorisms (see Volume 18, Rājanīti and Dharmaśāstra).

    The Rāmāyaṇacampū (also called Campūrāmāyaṇa), attributed to the Paramāra king Bhoja of Dhārā (Chapter 6), covers the first five books of Vālmīki’s epic; the war book was supplied by later poets, of whom Lakṣmaṇasūri’s continuation became the usual one. Its smooth verses and moderate prose made it a favourite text for teaching kāvya, especially in South India, where it was long read in traditional schools as an introduction to ornate Sanskrit. Anantabhaṭṭa’s Bhāratacampū, of uncertain date, did the same for the Mahābhārata.

    The later Sanskrit campūs, many of them from South India, show the range of the form. Tirumalāmbā’s Varadāmbikāpariṇaya, on the marriage of her own king, is one of the few surviving works of Sanskrit court literature by a woman (see Section 2.11). Jīva Gosvāmin’s Gopālacampū, written at Vṛndāvana, made the campū a vehicle for the theology of the Gauḍīya Vaiṣṇavas (compare Rūpa Gosvāmin’s theory of devotional rasa, Chapter 6). Nīlakaṇṭha Dīkṣita of Madurai, whose satires are mentioned in Chapter 2, dated his Nīlakaṇṭhavijaya in its opening verses to a year of the Kali era corresponding to 1637, and filled the myth of the churning of the ocean with the wit of a court intellectual. Veṅkaṭādhvarin’s Viśvaguṇādarśa (“Mirror of the Virtues of the World”) turns the form to social observation: two celestial beings travel over India, one finding fault with everything he sees — temples, towns, scholars, priests, the customs of each region — and the other answering by pointing out its merits, so that the work becomes a dialogue on the virtues and vices of the society of its day.

    The campū had an equally important life in the Dravidian languages. In Kannada it was the dominant form of the classical age, from Pampa’s Ādipurāṇa and Vikramārjunavijaya (941) to the works of Ponna, Ranna and Janna (Chapter 10), and its prestige was such that the Vīraśaiva poets who rejected court literature defined their own forms against it. Telugu adopted the mixture of verse and prose from Nannaya’s Mahābhāratamu onwards, though verse predominates; and in Malayalam the maṇipravāḷam campūs of the fourteenth to sixteenth centuries, among them Punam Nampūtiri’s Rāmāyaṇam, were closely tied to the recitation and exposition of the Cākyār performers of the temple theatre (compare Section 3.14). In each language the form offered what it had offered in Sanskrit: room both for the expansive pleasures of descriptive prose and for the concentrated effect of the stanza.

    4.7 From Kathā to Novel¶

    When the novel arrived in Indian languages in the nineteenth century, writers and critics looked back to Sanskrit prose for precedents. The word kādambarī became the ordinary term for “novel” in Marathi and Kannada, and in Bengali the novel was at first called upanyāsa, a Sanskrit word for a kind of narrative. Early novelists such as Bankimcandra drew on the descriptive and romantic conventions of Sanskrit prose and drama as well as on Walter Scott. Translations and adaptations of the Kādambarī, the Daśakumāracarita, the Vetālapañcaviṃśati and the Pañcatantra were among the first printed books in many languages. Scholars debate how far the Indian novel grew from these indigenous roots and how far it was a colonial import; most now see a combination of the two, in which a European form was naturalised through older Indian narrative traditions.

    4.8 Prose and Its Readers¶

    Who read Bāṇa and Subandhu? Their works presuppose readers with a thorough training in Sanskrit grammar, lexicography and poetics, able to resolve long compounds and to catch double meanings and allusions — in practice, a small élite of court poets, scholars and connoisseurs. The story collections reached a far wider audience, both in their Sanskrit forms and, above all, through oral retelling and translation into the regional languages, so that their tales became part of the common stock of Indian storytelling. The contrast between the two kinds of prose — the one an exercise of supreme verbal art for a few, the other a vehicle of narrative for many — mirrors a wider contrast in Indian literary culture between the courtly and the popular, which the devotional poets and, later, the modern novelists would each in their own way try to bridge.

    4.9 An Outline of Sanskrit Prose¶

    Table 4.4 summarises the main phases of Sanskrit narrative prose discussed in this chapter.

    Period Authors and works Characteristics

    6th–8th c. Subandhu, Bāṇa, Daṇḍin High literary prose; long compounds, śleṣa, description
    10th–12th c. Campū authors; Kṣemendra, Somadeva Mixed forms; verse retellings of story literature
