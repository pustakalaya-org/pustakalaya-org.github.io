    Chapter 13: The Modern Period: From Print to the Present¶

    Where the mind is without fear and the head is held high;
    Where knowledge is free; …
    Into that heaven of freedom, my Father, let my country awake.
    — Rabindranath Tagore, Gitanjali (English, 1912), poem 35

    13.1 Introduction¶

    The nineteenth and twentieth centuries transformed Indian literature more rapidly than any earlier period. Print, the periodical press, English education and new reading publics created new genres — the novel, the short story, the essay, the modern drama — and new ideas of what literature was for. Literature became a vehicle of social reform, of nationalism and of the struggles of those whom the older literary cultures had excluded. At the same time the classical and medieval literatures surveyed in this volume were rediscovered, edited, printed and re-read, and became symbols of cultural identity. The modern history of each language has been traced in its own chapter (Chapters 7, 10, 11 and 12); this chapter follows the themes that cross all of them. Fuller histories are Das (1991) and Das (1995).

    13.2 Print and the Colonial Encounter¶

    The founding of the Asiatic Society at Calcutta by William Jones in 1784 began a century of European scholarship on Sanskrit and other Indian languages. Jones’s translation of Śakuntalā (1789) and later translations of the Bhagavadgītā, the Gītagovinda and the Upaniṣads made Indian literature known in Europe (Chapter 3). Fort William College in Calcutta (1800) commissioned prose works in Hindustani and Bengali for the training of British officials. Missionary and Indian presses printed vernacular texts, lithography made cheap books in the Perso-Arabic and other scripts possible (Section 12.10), and by the mid nineteenth century newspapers and journals in many languages had created reading publics on a new scale.

    Indian writers responded creatively to European models. Bankimcandra wrote one novel in English, Rajmohan’s Wife (1864), before turning to Bengali; Michael Madhusūdan Datta began as an English poet and became the innovator of Bengali verse; and in every language the novel arrived, often first as historical romance and social reform fiction, between about 1860 and 1900. Women’s writing took new forms: Rāssundarī Devī’s Āmār Jīban (1876) was the first autobiography in Bengali, and Tārābāī Shinde’s Strī Puruṣ Tulanā (1882), a sharp comparison of men’s and women’s conduct, is a founding text of Marathi feminist writing. In 1913 Rabindranath Tagore became the first writer from outside Europe to receive the Nobel Prize in Literature (Section 11.4.3).

    13.3 Nationalism, Reform and the Literary Languages¶

    The struggle for independence shaped literature in every language. Poets wrote songs that became national hymns, from Bankimcandra’s Vande Mātaram to Iqbāl’s early Sāre jahāṉ se acchā; novelists such as Premchand and Raja Rao (Kanthapura, 1938) wrote of the Gandhian movement in the villages (Section 11.14); and reformers wrote against caste, child marriage and the oppression of women, from Gurajada Apparao’s Kanyāśulkam to Kumāran Āśān’s poems. The Progressive Writers’ Association, whose first conference was held at Lucknow in 1936, brought writers of many languages together around a programme of social realism. The same period also saw the hardening of linguistic and religious identities: the Hindi–Urdu controversy (Section 11.13), Tamil movements against Sanskrit and Hindi (Section 7.12), and finally the Partition of 1947.

    13.4 The Literature of Partition¶

    The Partition of 1947, which displaced some fifteen million people and cost perhaps a million lives, divided the literatures of the Punjab and Bengal and produced a literature in Urdu, Punjabi, Hindi, Bengali, Sindhi and English that is among the most powerful of the century. Saʿādat Ḥasan Manṭo’s Urdu stories, such as Toba Tek Singh and Khol Do, depict the violence and absurdity of the division with unsparing irony; Bhīṣma Sāhnī’s Hindi novel Tamas (1974) traces the outbreak of communal riots in a Punjab town; Amritā Prītam’s novel Piñjar (1950) tells of a woman abducted during the riots, and her lament addressed to Wāris Shāh became one of the most famous Punjabi poems (Section 11.7); Intizār Ḥusain wrote of loss and memory in Urdu from Pakistan; and Bengali writers such as Jyotirmoyee Devi and Ritwik Ghatak, in fiction and film, recorded the long aftermath of the partition of Bengal. Much of this writing gives special attention to the suffering of women, whose bodies became the terrain of communal violence. It also records the shared Punjabi and Bengali cultures that the new borders divided, and the displacement of Sindhi writers, who after 1947 sustained a literature without a homeland (Section 11.9).

    13.5 Institutions and Awards¶

    The Sahitya Akademi, India’s national academy of letters, was founded in 1954. It gives annual awards for works in twenty-four languages, including English, and publishes translations between Indian languages, histories and encyclopaedias of Indian literature. The Jnanpith Award, instituted by the Bharatiya Jnanpith trust in 1961 and first given in 1965, is the most prestigious Indian literary award; the Saraswati Samman (1991) is another major award.     13.6 Indian Writing in English¶

    Indian poetry in English goes back to Henry Derozio (1809–1831) and Toru Dutt (1856–1877); the novel in English came of age in the 1930s with Mulk Raj Anand (Untouchable, 1935), R. K. Narayan, creator of the fictional town of Malgudi, and Raja Rao. After independence poets such as Nissim Ezekiel, A. K. Ramanujan and Kamala Das, who also wrote in Malayalam (Section 10.7), and later Arun Kolatkar, created a distinctive Indian English poetry. Salman Rushdie’s Midnight’s Children (1981, Booker Prize) opened a new phase of international attention, continued by Vikram Seth, Amitav Ghosh, Arundhati Roy (The God of Small Things, Booker Prize 1997) and Jhumpa Lahiri (Pulitzer Prize 2000). Debates continue over the relationship between writing in English, with its large international market, and writing in the Indian languages, much of which remains untranslated.

    13.6.1 Movements in Modern Poetry¶

    Poetry in most Indian languages passed through similar phases in the twentieth century: a romantic phase influenced by English Romanticism and by Tagore (the Hindi Chāyāvād, the Kannada Navodaya, the Telugu bhāva kavitvam); a progressive phase committed to social realism; and a modernist phase, from the 1940s and 1950s, that experimented with free verse, irony and the language of the city, from the Hindi Nayī Kavitā and the Marathi poetry of B. S. Mardhekar to the Bengali poetry of Jībanānanda Dāś. Urdu poetry developed the nazm (the poem on a single theme) alongside the ghazal, in the work of Faiz, N. M. Rāshid and Mīrājī. Later decades saw the rise of Dalit, feminist and regional voices, and a renewed interest in older forms such as the ghazal, the dohā and devotional song.

    13.7 Dalit Literature¶

    One of the most important new movements in post-independence Indian literature is Dalit writing: literature by and about communities formerly treated as “untouchable”. Its roots lie in the bhakti poetry of Cokhāmeḷā and Ravidās, in the anti-caste writing of Jotirao Phule, and above all in the thought of B. R. Ambedkar. It emerged as a movement in Marathi in the 1950s and 1960s and gained force with the Dalit Panthers (1972), whose founders included the poet Nāmdeo Ḍhasāḷ (Golpīṭhā, 1972). Autobiography became its signature genre: Dayā Pavār’s Balutā (1978), Sharankumar Limbale’s Akkarmāśī (1984), Omprakash Valmiki’s Hindi Jūṭhan (1997) and Bama’s Tamil Karukku (1992), which tells of a Dalit Christian woman’s experience of caste within the church as well as outside it. Dalit literature now flourishes in Kannada (Siddalingaiah, Devanoor Mahadeva), Telugu, Gujarati, Punjabi and other languages.

    Dalit critics have argued that the aesthetics of rasa and of the cultivated connoisseur cannot simply be applied to this writing, whose purpose is to bear witness to suffering and to demand justice, and whose readers include those whom classical theory never imagined as readers. Sharankumar Limbale’s Towards an Aesthetic of Dalit Literature sets out these arguments (Limbale 2004). They are an important challenge to any account, including this one, that presents Indian literature as a single continuous tradition.

    13.8 Women’s Writing¶

    Women’s writing in the modern period builds on earlier voices — the Therīgāthā nuns, the Caṅkam women poets, Kāraikkāl Ammaiyār, Āṇṭāḷ, Akka Mahādēvi, Lal Ded, Janābāī, Mīrā — but it has a new scope. Among many important writers are Lalithambika Antharjanam in Malayalam, who wrote of the lives of Nampūtiri women; ʿIṣmat Chughtāʾī in Urdu, whose story Lihāf (“The Quilt”, 1942) was prosecuted for obscenity; Amritā Prītam in Punjabi; Kṛṣṇā Sobtī and Mahādevī Varmā in Hindi; Mahāśvetā Devī in Bengali, whose stories of tribal and Dalit women are among the most powerful political fiction in India; Vaidehi in Kannada; and Ambai (C. S. Lakshmi) in Tamil. Their work has questioned the representations of women that dominate much classical literature, from the idealised heroines of kāvya to the devoted wives of the epics. Susie Tharu and K. Lalita’s anthology recovered many of these writers, and their predecessors, for modern readers (Tharu and Lalita 1991).

    13.9 Literature, Song and Cinema¶

    In the twentieth century cinema became a major medium for Indian literature. Early films drew on the epics and the lives of the saints — Dadasaheb Phalke’s Raja Harishchandra (1913) was the first Indian feature film, and films on Tukārām, Mīrā and other saints were among the most popular of the 1930s. Novels were frequently adapted: Śaratcandra’s Devdās has been filmed many times, and Satyajit Ray’s Pather Panchali (1955), based on Bibhūtibhūṣaṇ’s novel, together with his films of Tagore’s stories, brought Bengali literature to international audiences. Film songs carried poetry to the widest audiences in Indian history: Urdu poets such as Sāhir Ludhiyānvī and Kaifī Āẓmī wrote lyrics for Hindi films, the songs of Tagore and Nazrul are sung throughout Bengal, and the poetry of Kabīr, Mīrā and the Sufi saints lives on in popular music, continuing the performed life of so much earlier Indian literature (Section 1.12).

    13.10 The Literatures of the North-East and of Tribal Communities¶

    Any account of modern Indian literature must also note the literatures of languages that had little or no written tradition before the nineteenth or twentieth century. In the north-east, Manipuri (Meitei) has a written literature going back several centuries, including the royal chronicle Cheitharol Kumbaba and Vaiṣṇava texts, and a vigorous modern literature: Lamabam Kamal’s Mādhabī (1930) is regarded as the first Manipuri novel, and Hijam Anganghal’s Khamba Thoibi Sheireng (1940) recast the most famous Manipuri love legend as a long epic poem. Manipuri was added to the Eighth Schedule of the Constitution in 1992, and Bodo in 2003. Khasi, Mizo, Bodo, Garo and other languages acquired written forms, often in the Roman script through missionary activity, and produced modern poets such as the Khasi Soso Tham. Since the 1990s writers from the region have also become prominent in English, among them Temsula Ao and Easterine Kire, who write of Naga village life, history and violence, and Mamang Dai, whose The Legends of Pensam (2006) draws on the oral traditions of the Adi of Arunachal Pradesh. Across central and eastern India, tribal communities possess extensive oral epics, songs and myths, and in recent decades writers from Santal, Gond, Bhil, Munda and other communities have begun to publish in their own languages and in Hindi, Bengali, English and other languages, often addressing displacement, forest rights and cultural survival. Santali, written in the Ol Chiki script devised by Raghunath Murmu in the twentieth century, was added to the Eighth Schedule of the Constitution in 2003. These literatures, and the oral traditions behind them, are treated in Volume 23 of this series.

    13.11 Sanskrit and Persian in the Modern Period¶

    The colonial period changed the position of the two older languages of high culture. In 1837 Persian was replaced by English and the regional languages as the language of the courts and of administration in British India, and the patronage on which Indo-Persian literature had depended disappeared with the Mughal and regional courts. Persian did not vanish at once — Ghālib regarded his Persian poetry as his best, and Iqbāl wrote much of his philosophical poetry in Persian (Section 12.19) — but by the twentieth century it had become a language of scholarship rather than of living literature in India.

    Sanskrit fared differently. The East India Company’s Sanskrit colleges at Banaras (1791) and Calcutta (1824), the work of European and Indian Sanskritists, and later the universities, made Sanskrit an academic discipline and printed its classics on a large scale. Sanskrit also continued to be written. Journals in Sanskrit appeared from the late nineteenth century; poets wrote on modern subjects, as Paṇḍitā Kṣamā Rāo did in her Satyāgrahagītā (1932) on Gandhi’s movement; and modern Sanskrit drama, fiction and poetry, though read by a small public, have continued to the present, recognised by the Sahitya Akademi’s annual award for Sanskrit and by the Jnanpith Award to Satya Vrat Shastri (2006). The history of this “modern Sanskrit” literature is still little known outside specialist circles, although it shows that the classical language went on being a medium of new writing long after the narrative of modern literature usually leaves it.

    13.12 Translation and the Unity of Indian Literature¶

    The phrase “Indian literature” has been used since the nineteenth century both as a statement of cultural unity and as a programme for building it. In practice Indian literature exists in many languages, and most readers know only one or two. Translation — between Indian languages and into English — is therefore essential to any shared literary culture. The Sahitya Akademi, the National Book Trust and independent publishers have made many classical and modern works available. Much remains untranslated, and many manuscripts of earlier literature, in Sanskrit, Tamil and other languages, are still unedited; the National Mission for Manuscripts, established in 2003, has begun to survey and conserve them.

    13.12.1 The Translation of the Classics¶

    The translation of classical Indian literature into English has a history of its own. The first generation of translators — William Jones, Charles Wilkins (Bhagavadgītā, 1785), H. H. Wilson — worked in the context of the East India Company’s search for knowledge of Indian law and culture. In the nineteenth century the fifty volumes of the Sacred Books of the East, edited by Max Müller, and the many volumes of the Harvard Oriental Series presented religious and philosophical texts to European scholars, while Indian scholars such as R. C. Dutt and Ganganath Jha produced translations of their own. In the twentieth century poet-translators — A. K. Ramanujan for Tamil and Kannada, Daniel Ingalls for Sanskrit court poetry, Barbara Stoler Miller for Kālidāsa and Jayadeva, Arun Kolatkar and Dilip Chitre for Tukārām — sought to convey the literary quality of the originals rather than only their content. In the twenty-first century bilingual series such as the Clay Sanskrit Library and the Murty Classical Library of India have made a wide range of texts available with the original on the facing page. Every translation is an interpretation, and readers who compare several versions of the same text quickly learn how much depends on the translator.

    13.12.2 Readers, Publishers and Festivals¶

    The conditions of literary life have changed again in recent decades. Literacy, which stood at under twenty per cent at independence, now reaches the great majority of the population, and newspapers, magazines and books in Indian languages have larger readerships than ever before. Regional publishers, little magazines and writers’ cooperatives sustain literatures in languages with millions of speakers but little international visibility. Literary festivals, from the large international events at Jaipur and elsewhere to regional festivals in Kerala, Karnataka and the north-east, bring writers and readers together, and digital platforms have created new spaces for poetry, especially in Hindi and Urdu, where recitation at the mushāʿira and kavi sammelan remains a living tradition.

    13.13 Indian Literature in the World¶

    Indian literature has travelled widely. The Pañcatantra reached Europe through Persian and Arabic (Chapter 8); the Rāmāyaṇa and Mahābhārata were adapted across Southeast Asia, in the Thai Ramakien, the Javanese wayang and the Old Javanese kakawin; Buddhist literature was translated into Chinese, Tibetan and other languages; and from the late eighteenth century Sanskrit literature influenced European Romanticism and, through the Bhagavadgītā and Upaniṣads, writers from Emerson and Thoreau to T. S. Eliot. Claims of Indian influence should, however, be precise: where transmission can be documented, as with the Pañcatantra, it is a remarkable story; where it cannot, parallel developments are more likely.

    13.14 The Theory of Literature Today¶

    The concepts of alaṅkāraśāstra remain alive in Indian criticism and have attracted interest elsewhere. Literary scholars have used dhvani to discuss implicature and suggestion, rasa to discuss emotional response, and aucitya and vakrokti in the analysis of style, and Sanskrit poetics has been taught alongside Western theory in many Indian universities. Critics differ over whether these concepts can be applied directly to modern literature, especially to genres such as the novel and to writing, such as Dalit literature, whose aims differ from those of classical kāvya.

    A different challenge came from critics of the regional literatures. In After Amnesia (1992) G. N. Devy argued that modern Indian criticism suffered from a double loss of memory: colonial education had taught critics to measure their literatures by English standards, while the revival of Sanskrit poetics offered a tradition that had long since lost touch with the literatures of the bhāṣās, the regional languages, in which most Indians actually read (Devy 1992). Devy called for a criticism grounded in the bhāṣā traditions themselves, and his book became a reference point for the “nativism” (deśīvād) associated with the Marathi novelist and critic Bhalchandra Nemade, which defended the forms and values of the regional literatures against both Western and Sanskritic models. Its critics replied that nativism could harden into a cultural chauvinism of its own. The debate, like the Dalit critique of rasa, shows that the classical theories are now best treated not as timeless truths but as one rich body of thought among several, able to enter into dialogue with modern criticism.

    13.15 Conclusion¶

    This volume has surveyed more than three thousand years of literature in more than a dozen languages, from the hymns of the Ṛgveda and the Sanskrit epics, court poetry, drama and theory, through the love and war poetry of the Tamil anthologies, the verses of Buddhist nuns and Jain monks, the songs of devotees and Sufis, and the epic retellings and court poetry of the regional languages and of Persian and Urdu, to the literatures of the modern period. What unites this material is not a single essence but a long history of interaction — of borrowing, translation, rivalry and response — among languages, communities and traditions. The image of the kalpalatā, the wish-fulfilling creeper, serves best if it suggests growth and branching rather than a single root: a living literature that continues to put out new shoots in every language of the subcontinent.
