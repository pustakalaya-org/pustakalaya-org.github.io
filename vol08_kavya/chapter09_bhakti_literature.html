    Chapter 9: Bhakti and Sufi Poetry¶

    pothī paḍhi paḍhi jaga muā, paṃḍita bhayā na koi |
    ḍhāī ākhara prema kā, paḍhai so paṃḍita hoi ||
    The world has died reading books, and no one has become learned. Whoever reads the two and a half letters of “love” becomes learned.
    — A sākhī widely attributed to Kabīr (wording varies between collections)

    9.1 Introduction¶

    Bhakti, from the root bhaj, “to share, to partake”, means participation in and loving devotion to a deity. The idea is old: it appears in the Śvetāśvatara Upaniṣad and is central to the Bhagavadgītā. What is new from about the sixth century CE is a great body of poetry, composed first in Tamil and later in almost every language of the subcontinent, in which devotees address God directly, in their own languages, with an intensity of personal feeling unknown to earlier Sanskrit literature.

    Textbooks often speak of a single “bhakti movement” that began in the Tamil south and spread northward over a thousand years. John Stratton Hawley has shown that this narrative is largely a twentieth-century construction, shaped by nationalist and Hindi literary historians, and that the various devotional traditions had their own histories and connections (Hawley 2015). This chapter keeps the regional traditions distinct while noting their links. Good anthologies of translations are Hawley and Juergensmeyer (1988), Ramanujan (1973), Ramanujan (1981) and Peterson (1989).

    It is also often said that bhakti “democratised” religion and literature. There is truth in this: bhakti poets composed in spoken languages, many of them were women or came from low-ranked castes, and much of their poetry criticises ritualism, caste pride and religious hypocrisy. But the relation of bhakti to social hierarchy was complex, and is discussed at the end of the chapter.

    9.1.1 Sanskrit Sources and Bridges¶

    Two Sanskrit texts were especially important to the devotional literatures of the second millennium. The Bhagavadgītā provided the classical statement of devotion as a path to liberation and was commented on and retold in the regional languages, most famously in Jñāneśvar’s Marathi Jñāneśvarī. The Bhāgavata Purāṇa, composed probably in the Tamil country around the ninth or tenth century and deeply influenced by the Āḻvārs, told the life of Kṛṣṇa — his childhood in Braj and his love for the cowherd women — with an intensity of emotion that made it the scripture of Kṛṣṇa devotion across India. Its tenth book was retold in almost every Indian language, from Pōtana’s Telugu and Jagannātha Dāsa’s Odia to Śaṅkaradeva’s Assamese. Through such texts, and through pilgrimage and the movement of teachers, the regional traditions were linked to one another.

    9.2 Tamil Śaiva Poetry¶

    9.2.1 Kāraikkāl Ammaiyār¶

    The earliest Tamil devotional poet whose works survive as a distinct corpus is a woman, Kāraikkāl Ammaiyār, “the Mother of Kāraikkāl”, probably of the sixth century. According to the hagiography in Cēkkiḻār’s twelfth-century Periya Purāṇam, she was Punitavati, the devout wife of a merchant of the port of Kāraikkāl. When a mango she served to her husband was miraculously replaced by Śiva, her frightened husband abandoned her and married again. She then asked Śiva to take away her beauty and give her the body of a pēy, a ghoulish spirit, and in that form she went to Tiruvālaṅkāṭu to sing of Śiva dancing in the cremation ground.

    Her works — the Arputat Tiruvantāti (“Wondrous Linked Verses”, 101 stanzas), the Tiruvirattai Maṇimālai and two hymns on Tiruvālaṅkāṭu — are preserved in the eleventh book of the Śaiva canon. They are among the first poems to use forms that became standard in Tamil devotion, such as the antāti, in which the last word of each verse begins the next. Their imagery is unforgettable: Śiva dances among burning corpses, jackals and ghouls, and the poet, emaciated and fearless, delights in the scene. Her iconography as a skeletal woman with drooping breasts and wild hair, seated at the foot of the dancing Śiva, appears in Cōḻa bronzes and temple sculpture. Elaine Craddock’s study and translation examine her poetry and the ways in which her renunciation of conventional femininity has been read (Craddock 2010). She is honoured among the sixty-three Nāyaṉmārs, one of only three women in that list.

    9.2.2 The Nāyaṉmārs and the Tēvāram¶

    The sixty-three Nāyaṉmārs (“leaders”) are the saints of Tamil Śaivism, listed by Cuntarar in his Tiruttoṇṭattokai and celebrated in the Periya Purāṇam. They include kings, brahmins, merchants, a hunter, a washerman, a potter and the untouchable Nantaṉār. Three poets of the seventh and eighth centuries — Tirunāvukkaracar (Appar), a former Jain monk; Tiruñāṉacampantar (Campantar), a brahmin child prodigy; and Cuntarar, whose relationship with Śiva is that of an intimate friend — composed the hymns collected as the Tēvāram. These hymns (about 800 patikams, generally of ten or eleven stanzas) form the first seven books of the twelve-book Śaiva canon, the Tirumuṟai, which was arranged by Nampi Āṇṭār Nampi around the tenth to eleventh century, with the Periya Purāṇam later added as the twelfth.

    The Tēvāram hymns are tied to places: they sing of Śiva at particular shrines, and the 270-odd temples so celebrated (pāṭal peṟṟa talam) form a sacred geography of the Tamil country that pilgrims still follow. They also contain polemic against Jains and Buddhists, reflecting the religious competition of the period. Indira Peterson’s Poems to Śiva translates a wide selection with a study of their performance as temple song (Peterson 1989).

    9.2.3 Māṇikkavācakar¶

    Māṇikkavācakar (“he whose words are rubies”; probably ninth century) composed the Tiruvācakam (“Sacred Utterance”) and the Tirukkōvaiyār, which together form the eighth book of the canon. The Tiruvācakam expresses with great intensity the poet’s sense of unworthiness, his melting love and his gratitude for Śiva’s grace. A Tamil saying holds that “one who does not melt at the Tiruvācakam will not melt at any words” (tiruvācakattukku urukātār oru vācakattukkum urukār).

    9.3 Tamil Vaiṣṇava Poetry: The Āḻvārs¶

    The twelve Āḻvārs (“those immersed” in God), who lived between about the sixth and ninth centuries, are the poet-saints of Tamil Vaiṣṇavism. Their hymns were collected around the tenth century by Nāthamuni as the Nālāyira Divyaprabandham (“Four Thousand Divine Compositions”), which the Śrīvaiṣṇava tradition of Yāmuna and Rāmānuja treats as a Tamil Veda equal to the Sanskrit (Table 9.1).

    9.3.1 Nammāḻvār¶

    Nammāḻvār’s Tiruvāymoḻi is the theological and poetic centre of the canon. It moves between ecstatic union and the anguish of separation, often in the voice of a girl in love with Viṣṇu, and draws on the conventions of classical akam poetry — the lovesick heroine, her friend, her worried mother — while transforming them into devotion. A. K. Ramanujan’s Hymns for the Drowning is a fine selection in English (Ramanujan 1981).

    9.3.2 Āṇṭāḷ¶

    Āṇṭāḷ (Kōtai), of the eighth or ninth century, was according to tradition a foundling raised by Periyāḻvār at Śrīvilliputtūr, who refused all human suitors and was united with Viṣṇu at Śrīraṅgam. Her Tiruppāvai (thirty verses) is sung in the voice of a girl waking her friends at dawn in the month of Mārkaḻi for a vow (pāvai) to Kṛṣṇa; it is recited in South Indian homes and temples during that month to this day. Her Nācciyār Tirumoḻi (143 verses) speaks with startling frankness of her bodily longing for the god, including a famous dream of her wedding to him.

    9.4 Siddhas, Nāths and Yogic Poetry¶

    Alongside devotional poetry there developed a poetry of yogic and Tantric adepts, often iconoclastic and enigmatic. The Buddhist siddhas of eastern India composed Apabhraṃśa couplets and the Old Bengali songs of the Caryāpada (Chapters 5 and 11) in a deliberately obscure “twilight language” (sandhyābhāṣā) whose images had both everyday and esoteric meanings. The Nāth yogīs, followers of the legendary Gorakhnāth, left Hindi verses (bānī) on the disciplines of the body and breath that strongly influenced Kabīr and the Sants.

    In Tamil, the Tirumantiram of Tirumūlar (perhaps sixth to eighth century), the tenth book of the Śaiva canon, combines yoga, Tantric ritual and devotion in some three thousand verses. Later poets known as cittar (siddhas), such as Civavākkiyar and Pāmpāṭṭi Cittar, whose dates are uncertain, mocked idol-worship, caste and the authority of the Vedas in plain and forceful verse, and were associated with alchemy and medicine. Their poetry, popular among ordinary people, was long ignored by literary scholars and has been revalued in modern times.

    9.5 The Vīraśaiva Vacanas of Karnataka¶

    In twelfth-century Karnataka, a community of devotees of Śiva gathered around Basavaṇṇa, a brahmin who became chief minister to the Kalacuri king Bijjala at Kalyāṇa. Its members composed short, rhythmic prose-poems called vacanas (“sayings”) in colloquial Kannada, each ending with the poet’s chosen name for Śiva. The vacanas reject temple worship, sacrifice, caste and the authority of the Vedas in favour of the inner experience of the devotee, who wears Śiva’s emblem (the iṣṭaliṅga) on the body. In one of Basavaṇṇa’s best-known vacanas the poet contrasts the rich, who build temples for Śiva, with himself, who has nothing — and declares that his own body will be the temple, its legs the pillars and its head the golden dome — for what is built and stands will perish, while what moves endures (Ramanujan 1973).

    Besides Basavaṇṇa (twelfth century) the best-known vacana poets are Allama Prabhu, a mystic of paradox; Dāsimayya; and Akka Mahādēvi, a young woman who abandoned marriage and, according to tradition, clothing, to wander as a devotee of her divine lover “Cennamallikārjuna, lord white as jasmine”. Tradition also preserves vacanas by washermen, ferrymen, cobblers and women of many backgrounds. According to the hagiographies the community at Kalyāṇa was broken up by violence after an inter-caste marriage between the son of a former untouchable and the daughter of a brahmin. A. K. Ramanujan’s Speaking of Śiva introduced the vacanas to readers of English.

    9.6 Lal Ded of Kashmir¶

    Lal Ded (“Granny Lal”), also known as Lallā or Lalleśvarī, a Kashmiri woman mystic of the fourteenth century, is the first known poet of the Kashmiri language. Almost nothing certain is known of her life. Tradition says that she was married young, suffered at the hands of her mother-in-law, left home to become a wandering ascetic in the tradition of Kashmiri Śaivism, and went about naked, singing her verses. She is revered by Hindus as Lalleśvarī and by Muslims as Lal Ārifa, and is linked in legend with the Sufi Sayyid ʿAlī Hamadānī and with Nund Ṛṣi (Shaikh Nūr ud-Dīn), the founder of the Kashmiri Ṛṣi order of Sufis, who is said to have been nursed by her.

    Her verses, called vākh (“speech”), are short four-line poems on the search for the self, the futility of ritual, the body as the site of spiritual discipline and the unity of the divine. They draw on the vocabulary of Kashmiri Śaiva yoga — breath, the inner channels, the experience of Śiva as consciousness — but address ordinary listeners in plain, vivid language. They were transmitted orally for centuries before they were written down, so that their wording and even their authorship are uncertain; the first scholarly edition was published by George Grierson and Lionel Barnett in 1920 (Grierson and Barnett 1920). Ranjit Hoskote’s translation and introduction discuss both the poetry and the contested ways in which Lal Ded has been claimed by Hindu, Muslim and Kashmiri nationalist narratives (Hoskote 2011). Her vākhs remain part of everyday Kashmiri speech.

    9.7 The Vārkarī Poets of Maharashtra¶

    The Vārkarī tradition centres on the god Viṭṭhala (Viṭhobā) of Paṇḍharpūr and on the pilgrimage (vārī) that devotees make there twice a year, singing the songs of the saints. Its first great poet, Jñāneśvar (Jñānadev, traditionally 1275–1296), composed the Jñāneśvarī (1290), a Marathi verse commentary on the Bhagavadgītā in the ovī metre that is regarded as the first great classic of Marathi literature, and the independent poem Amṛtānubhava. His contemporary Nāmdev (traditionally 1270–1350), a tailor, composed abhaṅgas in Marathi and songs in Hindi, some of which entered the Sikh scripture. Around Nāmdev gathered poets whom the brahmin literary world would never have admitted. Janābāī, a servant woman in his household, wrote abhaṅgas that speak with intimacy and humour of a Viṭṭhala who helps her with her grinding and sweeping. Cokhāmeḷā (fourteenth century), a Mahar, of a caste treated as untouchable, wrote of the pain of being kept outside the temple and of the “impurity” imposed on his body; his wife Soyarābāī, his sister Nirmalā and his son Karmameḷā were poets too.

    Two centuries later the brahmin Eknāth (1533–1599) of Paithan wrote the Eknāthī Bhāgavata, a commentary on the eleventh book of the Bhāgavata Purāṇa, and comic devotional songs (bhāruḍ) in the voices of village characters. The best loved of all the Vārkarī poets, Tukārām (traditionally 1608–1649/50), was a Śūdra grain dealer from Dehu whose thousands of abhaṅgas turn on his failures, doubts and quarrels with God as much as on devotion. According to tradition the brahmins of his village forced him to sink his manuscripts in the river Indrāyaṇī, and they were restored after he fasted on its bank. His disciple Bahiṇābāī, a brahmin woman, wrote an autobiography in verse that speaks candidly of her husband’s anger at her devotion.

    9.8 The Sants and Devotional Poets of North India¶

    From the fifteenth century the Hindi-speaking regions produced devotional poetry of two broad kinds. The “Sants” addressed a God beyond form and attributes (nirguṇa), while the poets of Kṛṣṇa and Rāma sang of God with form and attributes (saguṇa). The distinction is useful but not absolute.

    9.8.1 Kabīr¶

    Kabīr, a weaver of the Julāhā caste, a Muslim community, of Banaras, is the most famous of the Sants. His dates are uncertain: tradition gives 1398–1518, a span of 120 years that is hard to credit. Modern scholars most often place him c. 1440–1518, though an earlier death, around 1448, has also been argued, notably by Charlotte Vaudeville, whose study sets out the evidence (Vaudeville 1993). His poems — couplets (sākhī), songs (pad) and longer ramainīs — survive in three main collections: the Sikh Ādi Granth, the Rajasthani Dādūpanthī manuscripts and the Bījak of the Kabīrpanth. They differ considerably, and much that is popularly attributed to Kabīr cannot be traced to early sources. The Kabīr of the poems mocks both Hindu and Muslim religious authorities, rejects caste, idol-worship and pilgrimage, and points to the God within, reached through the guru, the Name and love.

    Kabīr’s poetry is famous for its “upside-down” language (ulaṭbāṃsī): riddling verses in which the cow sucks the calf, the boat sails on dry land or the fish climbs a tree, images inherited from the Nāth yogīs that unsettle the listener’s assumptions and point to a reality beyond ordinary reason. Equally characteristic are his blunt challenges to religious authority — asking the brahmin why, if he is so pure, he was born by the same path as everyone else, or the mullah why he must shout from the minaret as if God were deaf. His images are drawn from the weaver’s loom, the market and the cremation ground, and his couplets entered the proverbial speech of North India.

    9.8.2 Ravidās and Other Sants¶

    Ravidās (Raidās; fifteenth–sixteenth century), a leather-worker (Camār) of Banaras, sang of a city without sorrow, “Begampura”, where there is no tax, no fear and no ranking of people, and is today revered by a large community of Dalit followers. Other Sants include Dādū Dayāl (sixteenth century), a cotton-carder of Rajasthan, and Guru Nānak (1469–1539), founder of the Sikh tradition, whose hymns in a mixed Punjabi and Hindi idiom form the core of the Sikh scripture.

    9.8.3 Sūrdās¶

    Sūrdās, traditionally said to have been blind and to have lived c. 1478–1583, is the great poet of Kṛṣṇa in Braj Bhāṣā. The Sūrsāgar (“Ocean of Sūr”) attributed to him grew over the centuries to thousands of poems; the earliest manuscript, of 1582, contains only a few hundred. The poems describe Kṛṣṇa’s childhood in Braj, the love of the cowherd women (gopīs), and their longing after he leaves for Mathurā (Hawley and Juergensmeyer 1988).

    9.8.4 Tulsīdās¶

    Tulsīdās (c. 1532–1623) composed the Rāmcaritmānas (begun 1574), the story of Rāma in Avadhi, in seven books, in stanzas of four-line caupāīs punctuated by dohās. It presents Rāma as the supreme God who became man, and the recitation and staging of the Mānas (as in the Rāmlīlā of Banaras) became central to North Indian religious life. A well-known caupāī from the first book addresses Rāma as a child:

    Rāmcaritmānas, Bālakāṇḍa, before dohā 112

    maṃgala bhavana amaṃgala hārī |
    dravau so dasaratha ajira bihārī ||
    May he who is the abode of blessing and the remover of ill, who played in Daśaratha’s courtyard, be gracious.

    Tulsīdās’s other works include the Vinaya Patrikā, a collection of devotional petitions, and the Kavitāvalī. His work upholds the order of caste and the duties of women as prescribed in Brahmanical texts, even while it makes devotion to Rāma the supreme value; it has been both venerated and sharply criticised for this.

    9.8.5 Mīrābāī¶

    Mīrābāī, a Rajput princess of Merta, traditionally dated c. 1498–1546, is said to have refused to treat her marriage into the royal family of Mewar as binding, regarding Kṛṣṇa as her true husband, and to have defied her in-laws’ attempts to poison her. Her songs survive in oral tradition and in late manuscripts in Rajasthani, Braj and Gujarati; few can be securely attributed to her. One of the most famous begins:

    A song attributed to Mīrābāī

    mere to giradhara gopāla dūsaro na koī |
    jāke sira mora mukuṭa mero pati soī ||
    Mine is Giridhar Gopāl, and no one else; he who wears the peacock-feather crown, he is my husband.

    The life of Mīrā illustrates the difficulty of separating poet from legend. Almost all that is told of her — the poisoned cup that turned to nectar, the snake in a basket that became a garland, her meeting with Tulsīdās or with the Bengali theologian Jīva Gosvāmī, her final merging into the image of Kṛṣṇa at Dvārakā — comes from hagiographies written long after her death, and her songs were sung and composed by many singers, especially women, in her name. For many of these singers Mīrā was the model of a woman who defied family and husband for the sake of devotion, and her songs have served as a vehicle for women’s protest as well as for piety.

    9.9 Bengal, Odisha and Assam¶

    In Bengal the lyric tradition of Jayadeva was continued by Vidyāpati, who wrote in Maithili in the early fifteenth century, and by the poets known as Caṇḍīdās, whose songs on Rādhā and Kṛṣṇa became the basis of the Vaiṣṇava padāvalī. The ecstatic devotion of Caitanya (1486–1534), who popularised the congregational singing of Kṛṣṇa’s names (nāmasaṅkīrtana) and who left only eight Sanskrit verses (the Śikṣāṣṭaka), inspired Bengali biographies such as Kṛṣṇadāsa Kavirāja’s Caitanyacaritāmṛta and the theology of his followers, the Gosvāmins of Vṛndāvana. In Odisha the “five companions” (pañcasakhā), among them Jagannātha Dāsa, author of the Odia Bhāgavata, composed devotional works in the sixteenth century.

    In Assam, Śaṅkaradeva (traditionally 1449–1568) founded a Vaiṣṇava community devoted to the chanting of the Name (ekaśaraṇa nāma dharma), with prayer halls (nāmghar) and monasteries (sattra) that are still central to Assamese life. He and his disciple Mādhavadeva both composed devotional songs (borgīt) and one-act plays (aṅkīyā nāṭ) mixing Assamese and Brajabuli. Each also left a book of devotional verse that is still read in the prayer halls: Śaṅkaradeva the Kīrtana-ghoṣā, on the deeds of Kṛṣṇa, and Mādhavadeva the Nām-ghoṣā, on the chanting of the Name.

    The Vaiṣṇava lyric of Bengal, the padāvalī, reached its height in the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries with poets such as Jñānadās and Govindadās, who wrote in Bengali and in Brajabuli, a literary language modelled on Vidyāpati’s Maithili. Their songs follow the phases of the love of Rādhā and Kṛṣṇa — first attraction, meeting, quarrel, separation and reunion — classified according to the theology of the Gosvāmins, and are still sung in the kīrtan of Bengal, Odisha and Manipur. In Manipur, Vaiṣṇavism adopted in the eighteenth century inspired the rāslīlā dance-dramas, one of the most refined forms of devotional theatre in India.

    9.10 The Haridāsas of Karnataka¶

    The Haridāsas (“servants of Hari”) were Kannada devotional singers associated with the Dvaita Vedānta of Madhva and the worship of Kṛṣṇa at Uḍupi and of Viṭṭhala. After Śrīpādarāya and Vyāsatīrtha (fifteenth–sixteenth century) came Purandaradāsa (1484–1564), a wealthy merchant turned mendicant, whose songs in colloquial Kannada, signed “Purandara Viṭṭhala”, are the foundation of the Carnatic musical repertoire; tradition credits him with an impossible 475,000 songs, of which perhaps a thousand survive. His contemporary Kanakadāsa, from the Kuruba shepherd community, wrote songs and narrative poems, including the Rāmadhānyacaritre, an allegory in which the millet eaten by the poor triumphs over the rice of the rich, and the Nalacaritre. A famous legend tells how, refused entry to the Uḍupi temple because of his caste, he sang at the rear wall until the image of Kṛṣṇa turned to face him through a crack.

    9.11 The Sikh Scripture¶

    The Ādi Granth (later the Guru Granth Sahib), compiled by Guru Arjan in 1604, contains the hymns of the first five Sikh Gurus and, from the time of Guru Gobind Singh, those of the ninth, arranged by rāga. It also includes compositions of fifteen “Bhagats” of diverse backgrounds, among them Kabīr, Nāmdev, Ravidās, the Sufi Shaikh Farīd and Trilocan. It is thus an anthology of North Indian devotional poetry as well as a scripture (Table 9.2).

    Table 9.2. Some contributors to the Ādi Granth (approximate numbers of compositions)

    Guru Arjan Fifth Guru and compiler The largest number of any contributor

    The core of the scripture is the poetry of Guru Nānak (1469–1539), a Khatrī accountant from the Punjab who, after a mystical experience, travelled widely and founded a community of disciples at Kartārpur. His Japjī, recited each morning by Sikhs, opens with a statement of the oneness of God (ik oaṅkār) and meditates on the divine Name, the order of creation (hukam) and the stages of spiritual ascent. Nānak rejected the authority of both brahmins and mullahs, the observance of caste purity and the value of ritual and pilgrimage without inner devotion, and his successors institutionalised these teachings in the communal kitchen (laṅgar), where all sit and eat together. Later Sikh literature includes the vārs of Bhāī Gurdās (c. 1551–1636), which have been called the key to the scripture, and the Dasam Granth associated with Guru Gobind Singh, which contains devotional, martial and narrative poetry in Braj and Punjabi.

    9.12 Sufi Poetry in the Indian Languages¶

    The Sufi orders established in India from the twelfth century onwards, notably the Chishtīs, used music (samāʿ) and poetry as means of devotion. Sufi poets wrote in Persian (Chapter 12), but also in the spoken languages of India, and they used the language of human love — often with the soul as a woman longing for the divine beloved — in ways that parallel Vaiṣṇava devotion. The first known poet of Punjabi is a Sufi, Shaikh Farīd (Bābā Farīd, twelfth–thirteenth century), whose verses were preserved in the Sikh scripture. Amīr Khusrau, the greatest Persian poet of the Delhi Sultanate and a disciple of Niẓām al-Dīn Awliyāʾ, is credited with a large popular corpus of Hindavi verses and riddles, most of it of uncertain authenticity (Section 12.4). In Avadhi, Sufi poets from Mullā Dāʾūd to Jāyasī wrote long romances that can be read both as love stories and as allegories of the soul’s quest (Section 11.3). In the Punjab, Shāh Ḥusain, Sulṭān Bāhū and Bulleh Shāh (1680–1757) wrote kāfīs of great power, and Wāris Shāh’s Hīr (1766) became the classic Punjabi romance; in Sindh, Shāh ʿAbd al-Laṭīf Bhiṭāʾī (1689–1752) composed the Shāh jo Risālo, the greatest work of Sindhi literature (Section 11.9).

    Scholarly Note: Bhakti and Sufism

    Bhakti and Sufi poetry share an emphasis on love as the way to God, a critique of external ritual, devotion to the spiritual guide (guru or pīr), and the use of song. Poets and audiences crossed between the traditions, and figures such as Kabīr, Lal Ded and Nund Ṛṣi are claimed by both. Scholars caution, however, against treating these parallels as a simple “synthesis”: each tradition had its own theology, institutions and literary models, and their interaction took many different forms in different regions (Hawley 2015).

    9.13 Bhakti, Caste and Gender¶

    Many bhakti poets attacked caste pride and the exclusiveness of brahmin ritual, and the presence among them of women and of people from low-ranked and untouchable castes — Kāraikkāl Ammaiyār and Āṇṭāḷ, Tiruppāṇ and Nantaṉār, Cokhāmeḷā and Janābāī, Ravidās and Kabīr — is one of the most remarkable features of Indian literary history. Their songs gave a voice to people who had no place in Sanskrit learning.

    The record is nevertheless mixed. The hagiographies that preserved these poets often domesticated their challenge. In the Periya Purāṇam, the untouchable Nantaṉār is admitted into the presence of Śiva at Chidambaram only after passing through fire and emerging as a brahmin sage; in the legends of Tiruppāṇ the Āḻvār is carried into the temple on a brahmin’s shoulders. Cokhāmeḷā’s abhaṅgas themselves voice the pain of being kept outside the temple door, and the caste rules that excluded him were not overturned by the devotional communities that revered him. Women poets frequently paid for their devotion with social exclusion, and their songs often speak of conflict with husbands and in-laws. Bhakti communities themselves sometimes developed into new castes.

    In the twentieth century B. R. Ambedkar argued that the saints, while proclaiming the equality of all before God, had not campaigned against caste as a social system (Ambedkar 1936). Dalit writers and scholars have since reclaimed figures such as Cokhāmeḷā and Ravidās as forerunners of their own literature, while criticising the ways in which upper-caste traditions appropriated them (Limbale 2004). Cokhāmeḷā’s songs were sung for six centuries at Paṇḍharpūr, while he himself, by tradition, lies buried at the foot of the temple steps he was not allowed to climb.

    9.14 The Character of Bhakti Poetry¶

    Bhakti poetry differs from classical kāvya in language, form and setting: it is composed in the vernacular, usually in short song forms with a refrain and a signature line naming the poet, and it is meant to be sung, often in groups. But the contrast should not be exaggerated. Many bhakti poets were learned, drew on classical conventions — the akam love situations in Tamil, the heroines and seasons of Sanskrit poetry in Braj — and were themselves drawn into courtly culture. The Gītagovinda stands at the meeting point of the two worlds.

    9.14.1 Song and Performance¶

    Devotional poetry was composed to be sung, and its forms reflect this. Most songs have a refrain, a short sequence of stanzas and a closing “signature” (mudrā, chāp or bhaṇitā) in which the poet names himself or herself, often in the third person (“Kabīr says”, “Tukā says”, “Mīrā’s lord is Giridhar”). Signature lines made it easy for later singers to compose in a famous poet’s name, which is one reason why the corpora of Kabīr, Sūrdās and Mīrā grew so large. The Tamil hymns were assigned to musical modes (paṇ) and sung in temples by hereditary singers (ōtuvār); the Vārkarī abhaṅgas are sung by pilgrims in procession and in all-night bhajan and kīrtan sessions; the Sikh hymns are arranged by rāga and sung as kīrtan; the Haridāsa songs became the core of Carnatic music; and in Assam the songs and plays of Śaṅkaradeva are performed in the monasteries as part of the Sattriya tradition. Sufi poetry lives in the qavvālī of the shrines. To read these poems only on the page is to miss much of their life. Table 9.3 summarises the major poets discussed in this chapter.
