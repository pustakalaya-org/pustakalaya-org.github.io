    Chapter 8: Nīti Literature: Fable, Maxim and Anthology¶

    8.1 Introduction: Worldly Wisdom¶

    Nīti (from the root nī, “to lead”) means “guidance”: prudent conduct, policy, the art of getting on in the world and, for a king, statecraft. A large literature in Sanskrit and the regional languages is devoted to it. It takes three main forms: the fable collection, in which stories, often of animals, illustrate maxims of policy; the gnomic verse (subhāṣita, “well said”), a single stanza expressing a general truth; and the didactic sections of the epics.

    Nīti literature is closely related to the science of political economy (arthaśāstra), whose classic text, the Arthaśāstra ascribed by tradition to Kauṭilya (Cāṇakya), is a composite work dated by its most recent translator to c. 50 BCE–300 CE (Olivelle 2013); the text and its political theory are treated in Volume 18, Rājanīti and Dharmaśāstra. Kāmandaka’s Nītisāra (perhaps sixth to eighth century) restates its doctrines in verse. The fables and maxims, however, address a wider audience than kings, and their outlook is practical rather than moralising: they teach how to secure friends, defeat enemies, avoid being deceived and survive misfortune.

    8.1.1 The Arthaśāstra and the Literature of Policy¶

    The Arthaśāstra itself is a technical treatise, not a work of literature, but its influence on literature was profound. It analyses the state into seven “limbs” — the king, ministers, territory, fort, treasury, army and ally — and treats foreign relations through the “circle of kings” (maṇḍala), in which a king’s neighbour is his natural enemy and his neighbour’s neighbour his natural friend. It discusses the training of princes, the employment of spies, the testing of ministers’ loyalty and the means of dealing with enemies: conciliation, gifts, dissension and force. These categories reappear in the Pañcatantra, in Viśākhadatta’s Mudrārākṣasa (Chapter 3), in the debates of the Kirātārjunīya (Chapter 2), and in the court episodes of countless poems. Kāmandaka’s Nītisāra put this political science into easy verse, and it was studied as far afield as Java and Bali.

    8.2 The Pañcatantra¶

    The Pañcatantra (“Five Books”) is the most widely travelled work of Indian literature. Its original text is lost; it survives in many recensions, of which the Kashmiri Tantrākhyāyika is considered the oldest, and the work as a whole is usually dated to around the third to fifth century CE. Arthur Ryder’s lively translation of a later recension and Patrick Olivelle’s translation of a reconstructed text are both widely read (Ryder 1925; Olivelle 1997).

    8.2.1 Frame Story and Structure¶

    A king, Amaraśakti of Mahilāropya, despairs of his three dull-witted sons. The aged brahmin Viṣṇuśarman undertakes to make them expert in policy within six months, and composes the five books for the purpose. Each book has a frame story into which further stories are inserted, often several levels deep, and the prose narrative is punctuated by verse maxims that characters quote to one another as arguments (Table 8.1).

    Table 8.1. The five books of the Pañcatantra
    Book Title Frame story and theme
    I Mitrabheda (“Breaking of Friends”) The jackal Damanaka, against the advice of his brother Karaṭaka, sets the lion-king Piṅgalaka against his friend the bull Sañjīvaka, who is killed. How enmity is sown between allies
    II Mitrasamprāpti (“Winning of Friends”) A crow, a mouse, a tortoise and a deer become friends and rescue one another from hunters. The value of alliance among the weak
    III Kākolūkīya (“Crows and Owls”) A crow minister pretends to defect to the owls, learns their weaknesses and destroys them. War, peace and stratagem
    IV Labdhapraṇāśa (“Loss of What Was Gained”) A crocodile, urged by his wife, tries to get the heart of his friend the monkey, who escapes by his wits. How gains are lost through folly
    V Aparīkṣitakāraka (“Ill-Considered Action”) Stories of rash acts, including the brahmin’s wife who kills the faithful mongoose that has saved her child from a snake

    Well-known stories include the lion and the hare (who tricks the lion into leaping at his reflection in a well), the talkative tortoise carried through the air by two geese, and the blue jackal who passes himself off as king of the beasts until he betrays himself by howling — all from the first book.

    The third book applies the six measures of foreign policy (ṣāḍguṇya) of the arthaśāstra tradition: peace (sandhi), war (vigraha), marching (yāna), waiting (āsana), alliance (saṃśraya) and the double policy of peace with one and war with another (dvaidhībhāva).

    8.2.2 The Travels of the Pañcatantra¶

    The westward journey of the Pañcatantra can be traced through a chain of translations (Table 8.2).

    Table 8.2. Some stages in the transmission of the Pañcatantra
    Date Language Version
    6th c. Middle Persian (Pahlavi) Translation by the physician Burzōy for the Sasanian king Khusraw I (r. 531–579); lost
    c. 570 Syriac Kalīlag wa-Damnag, from the Pahlavi
    c. 750 Arabic Kalīla wa-Dimna of Ibn al-Muqaffaʿ, a classic of Arabic prose and the source of most later versions
    c. 1080 Greek Stephanitēs kai Ichnēlatēs of Symeon Seth
    12th c. Hebrew Version attributed to Rabbi Joel
    1251 Castilian Calila e Dimna
    c. 1270 Latin Directorium vitae humanae of John of Capua, from the Hebrew
    c. 1480 German Das Buch der Beispiele der alten Weisen, one of the early printed German books
    1570 English Sir Thomas North’s The Morall Philosophie of Doni, from an Italian adaptation

    Persian versions such as the Anvār-i Suhaylī (fifteenth century) carried the stories into Turkish and back into India, and the French fabulist La Fontaine acknowledged a debt to “Pilpay”, a name derived from the Arabic versions, in his later fables. The stories also travelled east, into Southeast Asian, Tibetan and Mongolian literatures.

    8.2.3 The Ethics of the Pañcatantra¶

    Readers since the nineteenth century have been struck by how little the Pañcatantra moralises. Its heroes are often the clever rather than the good: the jackal Damanaka wins by slander, the crow minister of the third book wins by treachery, and the hare kills the lion by deceit. Its verses frequently advise distrust — of kings, of enemies who seem friendly, of women, of servants. This is because the book belongs to the tradition of nīti and arthaśāstra, whose subject is how to succeed and survive in a world of competing interests, not how to attain moral perfection. As Patrick Olivelle’s introduction emphasises, the work is a textbook of nīti: its aim is practical intelligence — the ability to read situations, weigh advice and act at the right moment — and its characters use stories and maxims as arguments rather than as simple moral lessons (Olivelle 1997). Its teachings are not without ethical limits: the second book praises steadfast friendship, and many verses condemn greed, cruelty and ingratitude. But its ethics are those of a courtly and commercial world in which survival depends on prudence.

    8.3 The Hitopadeśa¶

    The Hitopadeśa (“Beneficial Instruction”), by Nārāyaṇa, a poet patronised by a king Dhavalacandra, probably of eastern India, reworks Pañcatantra material into four books: Mitralābha (“Winning of Friends”), Suhṛdbheda (“Separation of Friends”), Vigraha (“War”) and Sandhi (“Peace”). Its date is uncertain; the oldest manuscript is of 1373, and the work is usually placed between the ninth and fourteenth centuries. With its simple prose and abundant verse maxims it became a standard first reader for students of Sanskrit, in India and later in Europe. Among its introductory verses is a famous chain of causes:

    Hitopadeśa, introductory verses

    vidyā dadāti vinayaṃ vinayād yāti pātratām |
    pātratvād dhanam āpnoti dhanād dharmaṃ tataḥ sukham ||
    Learning gives modesty; from modesty one becomes worthy; from worthiness one gains wealth; from wealth, righteousness; and from that, happiness.

    8.4 Subhāṣita: The Single Stanza and the Anthologies¶

    The single, self-contained stanza was a favourite form of Sanskrit poetry. Such verses, whether on love, nature, the seasons, kingship, poverty or the follies of mankind, were quoted in conversation and in learned discussion, and collected in anthologies arranged by topic. The anthologies preserve the work of hundreds of poets, many otherwise unknown, and include women poets such as Vijjakā (Vijjā) and Śīlābhaṭṭārikā. The major anthologies are listed in Table 8.3.

    Table 8.3. Major Sanskrit anthologies
    Anthology Compiler Date Remarks
    Subhāṣitaratnakoṣa Vidyākara c. 1100 About 1,730 verses; compiled in a Buddhist monastery in Bengal
    Saduktikarṇāmṛta Śrīdharadāsa 1205 About 2,370 verses; Bengal, court of Lakṣmaṇasena
    Sūktimuktāvalī Jalhaṇa 1258 Yādava court of Devagiri
    Śārṅgadhara­paddhati Śārṅgadhara 1363 Over 4,000 verses on a wide range of subjects
    Subhāṣitāvali Vallabhadeva 15th c. (?) Over 3,500 verses; Kashmir
    Subhāṣita­ratna­bhāṇḍāgāra K. P. Parab (ed.) 1886 A modern printed compilation, often reprinted

    Many subhāṣitas circulated anonymously and are still widely quoted. Two examples:

    Anonymous; also in the Cāṇakyanīti (vulgate 14.1)

    pṛthivyāṃ trīṇi ratnāni jalam annaṃ subhāṣitam |
    mūḍhaiḥ pāṣāṇakhaṇḍeṣu ratnasaṃjñā vidhīyate ||
    There are three jewels on earth: water, food and a well-turned saying. Only fools give the name “jewel” to bits of stone.

    Anonymous

    paropakārāya phalanti vṛkṣāḥ paropakārāya vahanti nadyaḥ |
    paropakārāya duhanti gāvaḥ paropakārārtham idaṃ śarīram ||
    Trees bear fruit for the good of others; rivers flow for others; cows give milk for others: this body too exists for the good of others.

    8.4.1 Vidyākara’s Treasury¶

    The Subhāṣitaratnakoṣa (“Treasury of Well-Turned Verse”) shows what an anthology was and how one was made. Its compiler, Vidyākara, was a Buddhist scholar of the Jagaddala monastery in northern Bengal, a late Pāla foundation (see Volume 4, Buddha–Jina Prabhā), and he made it around 1100, apparently revising and enlarging it later. An earlier and shorter form was published in 1912 from a Nepalese manuscript under the title Kavīndravacanasamuccaya; the full work was edited by D. D. Kosambi and V. V. Gokhale from a palm-leaf manuscript preserved in Tibet (Kosambi and Gokhale 1957), and translated by Daniel Ingalls (Ingalls 1965).

    Its some 1,730 stanzas are arranged in fifty sections (vrajyā). It opens, as a Buddhist compiler’s work would, with verses to the Buddha and the bodhisattvas, but passes at once to Śiva, Pārvatī and Viṣṇu, and then through the seasons, the many phases of love, the beauties of women, sunset and moonrise, the court, the village, the virtuous and the wicked, and the poets themselves. That a Buddhist monk should collect erotic and Śaiva verses in this way shows how far kāvya was a shared culture that crossed religious boundaries. The anthology preserves the work of more than two hundred poets, among them Kālidāsa, Bhavabhūti and Rājaśekhara, but many are known only from such collections. Ingalls drew particular attention to a group of poets of Pāla Bengal, such as Yogeśvara, who described the countryside with unusual realism: the village in the rains, the farmer’s fields, the poor man’s household with its leaking roof and hungry children, subjects that the court epic seldom admitted.

    The anthologies were also a working tool. Compilers chose and arranged stanzas by topic so that a reader could find a verse for an occasion or a figure; they recorded authors’ names, though the same stanza is often ascribed to different poets in different collections; and theorists and commentators quoted from them. Many poets, including most of the women poets of Sanskrit (Section 2.11), survive only because an anthologist chose one or two of their verses.

    8.5 Bhartṛhari’s Three Centuries¶

    The best-known collection of single stanzas is the Śatakatraya (“Three Centuries”) attributed to Bhartṛhari: the Nītiśataka on worldly wisdom, the Śṛṅgāraśataka on love and the Vairāgyaśataka on renunciation. Tradition identifies the poet with a king who renounced his throne, and sometimes with the grammarian-philosopher Bhartṛhari (fifth century), author of the Vākyapadīya; neither identification can be confirmed. The collections exist in many recensions with widely varying contents. D. D. Kosambi’s critical edition (1948) showed that only a core of about two hundred verses is common to the manuscript traditions, and that the rest was added over time (Kosambi 1948). The verses move between ironic observation of human folly, celebration of women’s beauty and desire, and a weary longing for release, and the arrangement in three centuries invites readers to see these as stages of a life.

    The Nītiśataka mocks the fool who cannot be taught, the arrogance of the rich, the servility of courtiers and the injustice of fate, and praises learning, courage, friendship and the steadfastness of the good, who do not abandon a task once begun however great the obstacles. The Śṛṅgāraśataka alternates celebration of women’s beauty with warnings against desire as a snare. The Vairāgyaśataka speaks of the humiliations of seeking patronage, the decay of the body, the passing of time that devours all, and the peace of the ascetic who lives in the forest with the earth for his bed and the sky for his garment. Many verses are among the most quoted in Sanskrit, and they were among the first Sanskrit poems translated into European languages, appearing in Dutch as early as 1651.

    8.6 Satire: Kṣemendra and Others¶

    Worldly wisdom shades into satire, and Sanskrit has a lively satirical literature. The Kashmiri polymath Kṣemendra (eleventh century), already met as a theorist of propriety (Chapter 6), wrote a series of satirical poems on the society of his time: the Narmamālā (“Garland of Jests”) on corrupt scribes and officials, the Deśopadeśa (“Advice to the Country”) on rogues, misers, quack doctors, false ascetics and procuresses, and the Kalāvilāsa (“Sport of the Arts”) on the many arts of deception practised by merchants, goldsmiths, astrologers and others. His Samayamātṛkā follows the career of a courtesan guided by a scheming old bawd. These works, with their unsparing portraits of the Kashmiri bureaucracy and of hypocrisy in every class, complement Kalhaṇa’s history and show that Sanskrit literature was not confined to idealised kings and lovers. Later satirists include Nīlakaṇṭha Dīkṣita in the seventeenth century (Chapter 2), and the farces of the theatre (Chapter 3) belong to the same tradition.

    8.7 The Cāṇakya Collections¶

    Collections of maxims under the name of Cāṇakya, the minister of Candragupta Maurya to whom tradition ascribes the Arthaśāstra, circulated widely in many versions (Cāṇakyanīti, Vṛddhacāṇakya and others). They are medieval compilations, not works of the historical Cāṇakya, and their contents vary greatly. They deal with friendship, family, wealth, learning, women and the conduct of the wise, often in blunt and sometimes misogynistic terms. A typical verse:

    Cāṇakyanīti 3.15 (vulgate)

    ekena śuṣkavṛkṣeṇa dahyamānena vahninā |
    dahyate tad vanaṃ sarvaṃ kuputreṇa kulaṃ yathā ||
    As a whole forest is burned by a single dry tree set on fire, so is a family by a single bad son.

    8.8 Nīti in the Mahābhārata¶

    The Mahābhārata contains two great bodies of nīti. The Viduranīti (Udyogaparvan, chapters 33–40) is the counsel given by the wise Vidura to the sleepless king Dhṛtarāṣṭra on the eve of the war: a series of maxims on the marks of the wise and the foolish, the duties of kings, and the dangers of greed and anger, framed by Vidura’s vain attempt to persuade the king to restrain his son.

    Far longer are the teachings of the dying Bhīṣma to Yudhiṣṭhira in the Śāntiparvan and Anuśāsanaparvan: on the duties of kings (rājadharma), conduct in times of distress (āpaddharma) and liberation (mokṣadharma), and, in the Anuśāsanaparvan, on gifts, the duties of women and much else. These books are an encyclopaedia of the political and ethical thought of their time, including some of its harshest teachings on caste and gender, and the commentarial and scholarly literature on them is large.

    Every Indian literature has its own wisdom verse, often among its most popular and most quoted works (Table 8.4).

    8.10 Oral Wisdom: Proverbs, Riddles and Sayings¶

    Beside the written collections there has always been a vast oral literature of proverbs, riddles and sayings in every Indian language, much of it never recorded until modern times. Sanskrit theorists recognised the riddle (prahelikā) as a minor poetic form, and riddling verses appear in the epics and the anthologies. In Bengal, sayings ascribed to Khanā, a legendary woman astrologer, give practical advice on agriculture, weather and household life (“Khanār bacan”); in the Hindi region similar agricultural and weather sayings are attributed to Ghāgh and Bhaḍḍarī. Proverbs about castes, women and neighbouring peoples record social attitudes, sometimes harsh, that rarely appear so openly in written literature. Folklorists since the nineteenth century, Indian and European, have collected such material, and it forms an important link between written literature and the oral traditions discussed in Volume 23 of this series.

    8.11 Women, Caste and the Limits of Proverbial Wisdom¶

    Wisdom literature records the assumptions of the people who composed and transmitted it, and these were overwhelmingly men of the upper castes. Many nīti verses in Sanskrit and in the regional languages describe women as fickle, deceitful and in need of constant guardianship, echoing the prescriptions of the Dharmaśāstras; others treat low-caste people as naturally base, or wealth and birth as the measure of worth. Modern editions and popular anthologies often omit such verses, so that the tradition appears more humane than its manuscripts show. The same collections that teach shrewd advice on friendship and enmity also teach contempt for women and the low-born, often in the same metre and with the same wit.

    The tradition also contained its own counter-voices. Bhartṛhari’s Vairāgyaśataka mocks the pride of wealth and birth; the Telugu verses of Vēmana and the Hindi couplets of Kabīr ridicule caste distinctions and ritual purity; and the Tirukkuṟaḷ declares that all living beings are alike by birth and differ only in their deeds. Proverbial wisdom, in India as elsewhere, was never a single consistent body of teaching but a store of contradictory sayings, each available to be quoted when it suited.

    8.12 Shared Stories: Jātaka and Pañcatantra¶

    Many stories occur in both the Buddhist Jātaka and the Pañcatantra family, as well as in Jain collections and in the epics, which shows that all of them drew on a common stock of Indian folk narrative. The tale of the talkative tortoise carried by geese, for example, appears in the Pañcatantra as a warning against ignoring the advice of friends and in the Jātaka as a lesson told to a king who talked too much; the tale of the ass in the lion’s skin, betrayed by its braying, appears in the Jātaka and has close parallels in the Aesopic collections. Each tradition framed the shared stories for its own ends: the Buddhist collection made them illustrations of the virtues of the future Buddha, the Pañcatantra made them arguments in a debate on policy, and the Jain collections made them demonstrations of the workings of karma. The same story could thus carry very different morals in different hands — a fact that warns against reading any one moral as the “meaning” of an Indian tale.

    8.13 Indian and Aesopic Fable¶

    The Indian fable is often compared with the Greek fables ascribed to Aesop. The two traditions share some stories, and scholars have long debated the direction of borrowing; in most cases it cannot be determined. The most obvious difference is structural: Aesop’s fables are short, self-contained stories with an appended moral, whereas the Pañcatantra embeds its stories in frames and has characters argue with stories and verses, so that each story serves as a move in a debate. Its “morals” are also more pragmatic: cleverness is rewarded even when it is ruthless, and the clever jackal Damanaka is, for much of the first book, its hero. On the Jātaka versions of the shared stories see the previous section and Chapter 5.
