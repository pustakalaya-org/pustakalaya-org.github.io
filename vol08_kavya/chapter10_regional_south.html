    Chapter 10: Literatures of South India: Kannada, Telugu and Malayalam¶

    10.1 Introduction¶

    Tamil, discussed in Chapters 7 and 9, has the oldest literature among the Dravidian languages. Kannada and Telugu became literary languages in the late first millennium, and Malayalam emerged as a distinct literary language in the early second millennium. In each case the first literary works were produced by poets who knew Sanskrit well and who consciously fashioned a “Sanskrit-like” literary idiom for their own language, adopting Sanskrit genres such as the campū and mahākāvya while developing native metres and forms. Sheldon Pollock has made this process of “vernacularisation” central to his account of Indian literary history; the chapters on Kannada, Telugu and Malayalam in Pollock (2003), by D. R. Nagaraj, Velcheru Narayana Rao and Rich Freeman, are a good introduction (Nagaraj 2003; Narayana Rao 2003; Freeman 2003). The three literatures followed broadly similar paths: an early courtly phase in which Jain or brahmin poets adapted Sanskrit genres; a devotional phase in which saint-poets turned to native metres and spoken idioms, often in conscious opposition to the courts; epic retellings that became the most widely read works of each language; close ties with theatre and recitation; and, from the nineteenth century, a modern literature shaped by print, social reform and the movements against caste.

    10.2 Kannada Literature¶

    10.2.1 Beginnings¶

    The earliest Kannada inscription, at Halmiḍi, is usually dated to about the fifth century, and literary Kannada verse appears in inscriptions from the seventh century. The earliest surviving literary work is the Kavirājamārga (“The Royal Road of Poets”, c. 850), a treatise on poetics produced at the court of the Rāṣṭrakūṭa king Amoghavarṣa Nṛpatuṅga (r. 814–878) — probably by the court poet Śrīvijaya rather than the king himself. Adapting Daṇḍin’s Kāvyādarśa, it celebrates the land “between the Kāvēri and the Godāvari” and its people, skilled in poetry, and shows that a literary culture in Kannada already existed. The prose Vaḍḍārādhane (tenth century), a collection of Jain stories, is the earliest surviving Kannada prose work.

    10.2.2 The “Three Jewels”: Pampa, Ponna and Ranna¶

    The classical age of Kannada literature was dominated by Jain poets writing in the campū form. The first and greatest, Pampa (born 902), called the ādikavi (“first poet”) of Kannada, served Arikesari II, a Cāḷukya chief of Vemulavāḍa and feudatory of the Rāṣṭrakūṭas. In 941 he completed both the Ādipurāṇa, on the life of the first Jain tīrthaṅkara Ṛṣabha, and the Vikramārjunavijaya (or Pampabhārata), a retelling of the Mahābhārata centred on Arjuna, whom Pampa identifies with his patron. Ponna, at the court of the Rāṣṭrakūṭa Kṛṣṇa III, wrote the Śāntipurāṇa on the sixteenth tīrthaṅkara; and Ranna, patronised by the Cāḷukyas of Kalyāṇa, wrote the Ajitapurāṇa (993) and the Sāhasabhīmavijaya or Gadāyuddha (c. 982). The last concentrates on the final day of the Mahābhārata war and the mace-fight between Bhīma and Duryodhana, and like Pampa it gives the defeated Duryodhana a tragic dignity.

    10.2.3 Pampa’s Mahābhārata¶

    Pampa’s Vikramārjunavijaya illustrates how a regional poet could transform an epic. Its story follows the Mahābhārata, compressed into fourteen cantos of mixed prose and verse, but its hero is Arjuna, who is identified throughout with the poet’s patron Arikesari; the wedding of Arjuna and Subhadrā, the burning of the Khāṇḍava forest and the war are told so as to celebrate a tenth-century Deccan chief. At the same time Pampa gives unusual depth to the defeated. His Karṇa, who learns the secret of his birth on the eve of the war and still refuses to abandon Duryodhana, is a figure of tragic loyalty, and his Duryodhana dies with dignity. The work thus combines panegyric of a living king with a moral seriousness that has made it the foundation of Kannada literature. Pampa’s other work, the Ādipurāṇa, written as a religious duty, shows the Jain world-view within which he wrote: the same poet could compose a Hindu epic for his patron and a Jain sacred biography for his own salvation.

    10.2.4 Romance, Grammar and the “Pure” Kannada¶

    The Jain court tradition continued for three centuries after Pampa, and it became more varied. Durgasiṃha’s Kannada Pañcatantra (c. 1031) carried the fables into the language in campū form. Nāgacandra (c. 1100), called “the new Pampa”, wrote a Jain Rāmāyaṇa, the Rāmacandracaritapurāṇa, in which Rāvaṇa is a tragic figure and Rāma ends as a Jain ascetic. Nemicandra’s Līlāvati (c. 1170) is a romance of a prince and princess who fall in love through dreams, a Kannada kathā in the manner of Subandhu and Bāṇa (Chapter 4), and Janna’s Yaśodharacarite (1209) retold the Jain tale of the king who sacrificed a cock of flour (Section 4.6) with a sharpness of feeling that made it a school classic. The Kannada language itself became a subject. Āṇḍayya’s Kabbigara Kāva (“The Poets’ Defender”, thirteenth century) was written in a deliberately “pure” Kannada without words borrowed unchanged from Sanskrit, a first sign of the linguistic self-assertion that later histories made much of, and Keśirāja’s Śabdamaṇidarpaṇa (1260), a grammar written in Kannada verse, remained the authority on the classical language into the nineteenth century.

    10.2.5 The Vacanas and After¶

    The Vīraśaiva vacanas of the twelfth century (Chapter 9) broke decisively with courtly campū. Vīraśaiva poets of the following centuries adapted courtly forms to Śaiva devotion and developed native ones. Harihara (c. 1200) wrote the Girijākalyāṇa, on the marriage of Śiva and Pārvatī, as a campū in the classical manner, but told the lives of the Śaiva saints in a sequence of poems in the native ragaḷe metre; his nephew Rāghavāṅka’s Hariścandrakāvya (thirteenth century) established the six-line ṣaṭpadi as the great narrative form of Kannada. In the fifteenth century Cāmarasa’s Prabhuliṅgalīle retold the life of Allama Prabhu at the Vijayanagara court. The Jain tradition, too, adopted the new metres: Ratnākaravarṇi’s Bharateśavaibhava (sixteenth century), on the life of the emperor Bharata, son of the first tīrthaṅkara, is a long poem in the song-like sāṅgatya metre. The Haridāsas (Chapter 9), the tripadi verses of Sarvajña, Lakṣmīśa’s Jaimini Bhārata and the Yakṣagāna dance-drama texts (Section 10.6) complete the pre-modern picture (Table 10.1); at the Mysore court of the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries poets and scholars wrote both in the old campū manner and in prose.

    10.2.6 Kumāravyāsa and the Village Audience¶

    The most widely recited Kannada poem is the Karṇāṭa Bhārata Kathāmañjari (popularly the Gadugina Bhārata) of Kumāravyāsa (Nāraṇappa of Gadag, fifteenth century), a Mahābhārata covering the first ten books of the epic in the six-line bhāminī ṣaṭpadi, a metre that lends itself to musical recitation. Kumāravyāsa presents Kṛṣṇa as the true hero and the epic as a work of devotion, while retaining the full drama of the war, and his vivid metaphors and rapid narrative made the poem a favourite of the gamaka reciters who perform Kannada epics before village and temple audiences, one singing the verses and another explaining them. The gamaka tradition, still alive in Karnataka, shows how the “classics” of the regional literatures reached most of their audience: less through private reading than through public performance.

    10.3 Telugu Literature¶

    10.3.1 The Telugu Mahābhārata¶

    Telugu appears in inscriptions from the sixth century, but its literary history begins with the Āndhra Mahābhāratamu. Nannaya, court poet of the Eastern Cāḷukya king Rājarājanarendra (r. 1019–1061) at Rājamahendravaram, began the work; Tikkana (thirteenth century) translated fifteen books, from the Virāṭaparvan to the end; and Errana (fourteenth century) completed the unfinished Āraṇyaparvan. The three are honoured as the “poet-trinity” (kavitrayam). Their Mahābhārata is not a literal translation but a recreation in the campū manner, and it fixed the literary language for centuries (Table 10.2).

    Table 10.2. The kavitrayam of the Telugu Mahābhārata
    Poet Date Contribution
    Nannaya 11th c. The Ādi and Sabhā books and part of the Āraṇya; honoured as ādikavi
    Tikkana 13th c. The fifteen books from Virāṭa to Svargārohaṇa; celebrated for dramatic dialogue
    Errana 14th c. The remainder of the Āraṇya book

    10.3.2 Śaiva and Vaiṣṇava Poetry¶

    Pālkuriki Somanātha (thirteenth century) wrote the Basavapurāṇamu on the Vīraśaiva saints in the native dvipada metre, a deliberate choice of a “people’s” form over the courtly campū. Bammera Pōtana (fifteenth century) wrote a Telugu Bhāgavatamu much loved for its devotional sweetness, and Annamācārya (1408–1503) of Tirupati composed thousands of songs to Veṅkaṭeśvara, preserved on copper plates. Later, Tyāgarāja (1767–1847) made the Telugu kṛti the centre of Carnatic music.

    Pōtana, a farmer-poet who according to legend refused to dedicate his work to a king, made the tenth book of the Bhāgavata, with its stories of the child Kṛṣṇa, and the stories of Prahlāda and of the elephant Gajendra rescued by Viṣṇu, into the most widely memorised Telugu verse. Annamācārya’s songs, addressed to the god of the Tirumala hills in the voices of devotee, lover and philosopher, range from the erotic to the austere, and include songs that declare that the god makes no distinction between the brahmin and the outcaste. Their recovery from copper plates in the twentieth century made them a central part of the Carnatic concert repertoire.

    The same temple and court world produced the Telugu padam, a short song for dance. Its master, Kṣetrayya (seventeenth century), who signed his songs with the name of the god Muvvagōpāla, wrote them in the voice of a courtesan addressing the god as her lover and customer: by turns jealous, mocking, tender and businesslike, they make the conventions of the Sanskrit heroine (Section 6.2) speak in colloquial Telugu. They became the core of the repertoire of the temple and court dancers and, later, of Bharatanāṭyam; A. K. Ramanujan, Velcheru Narayana Rao and David Shulman have translated them with an essay on their double world of devotion and the market (Ramanujan et al. 1994).

    10.3.3 Śrīnātha and the Vijayanagara Court¶

    Śrīnātha (c. 1365–1441), the most admired poet between the epic and the prabandha periods, wrote the Śṛṅgāranaiṣadhamu (from Śrīharṣa’s Naiṣadhīyacarita), the Kāśīkhaṇḍamu (from the Skanda Purāṇa) and the Palnāṭi Vīracaritramu, a heroic narrative of a local war.

    Under the Vijayanagara king Kṛṣṇadevarāya (r. 1509–1529), who himself wrote the Āmuktamālyada — the story of the Tamil saint-poet Āṇṭāḷ, interwoven with a section on statecraft — the Telugu prabandha reached its classic form. The prabandha is a long narrative poem, usually on a Purāṇic theme, rich in description and wordplay. Tradition speaks of “eight elephants” (aṣṭadiggajas) at Kṛṣṇadevarāya’s court, though several of the poets usually named lived later in the sixteenth century (Table 10.3).

    Peddana’s Manucaritramu, from a story in the Mārkaṇḍeya Purāṇa, tells of the pious brahmin Pravara, who rejects the love of the celestial woman Varūthinī, and of the birth of Svarociṣ, father of the Manu Svārociṣa, from her later union with a Gandharva disguised as Pravara. Piṅgaḷi Sūranna’s Rāghavapāṇḍavīyamu tells the Rāmāyaṇa and Mahābhārata simultaneously through sustained double meaning, while his Kaḷāpūrṇodayamu, with its intricate plot of mistaken identities, has been called the first Telugu novel in verse. Velcheru Narayana Rao and David Shulman’s anthology gives a vivid picture of this literature (Narayana Rao and Shulman 2002); Yigal Bronner’s study discusses the double-narrative poems (Bronner 2010).

    10.3.4 Śataka, Nāyaka Courts and the Modern Period¶

    The śatakamu, a century of verses with a common refrain, was a popular form. The anonymous Sumati Śatakamu and the verses of Vēmana (perhaps seventeenth century), which attack caste, idol-worship and religious hypocrisy in plain language, are still widely quoted (compare Section 8.11).

    After the fall of Vijayanagara the centre of Telugu court poetry moved south, to the Nāyaka kings of Tanjavur and Madurai in the Tamil country. At Tanjavur under Raghunātha Nāyaka (r. c. 1600–1634) and his son Vijayarāghava, the king and his poets wrote in Telugu and Sanskrit, and the court developed a new, more playful prabandha, of which Cēmakūra Veṅkaṭakavi’s Vijayavilāsamu, on Arjuna’s three marriages during his pilgrimage, is the classic, as well as Telugu dance-dramas (yakṣagāna) for court performance. Velcheru Narayana Rao, David Shulman and Sanjay Subrahmanyam have reconstructed this court culture, in which the king appeared as a connoisseur and lover rather than a conqueror (Narayana Rao et al. 1992). Women had an unusual place in it. Rāmabhadrāmbā wrote the Sanskrit Raghunāthābhyudaya on her patron, and Raṅgājamma wrote Telugu poetry at Vijayarāghava’s court. Under the Maratha kings who succeeded the Nāyakas at Tanjavur, the courtesan-poet Muddupaḷani wrote the Rādhikāsāntvanamu (eighteenth century), an erotic poem on Rādhā’s jealousy when Kṛṣṇa takes the young Iḷā as his bride, told from the women’s side. When the singer Bangalore Nagarathnamma republished it in 1910, the colonial government banned the edition as obscene, and the ban was lifted only after Independence.

    Modern Telugu literature begins with the social reformer Kandukuri Veeresalingam and with Gurajada Apparao’s play Kanyāśulkam (1892), a satire on the sale of child brides; twentieth-century landmarks include Viswanatha Satyanarayana’s Rāmāyaṇa Kalpavṛkṣamu and Sri Sri’s revolutionary poetry.

    10.4 Malayalam Literature¶

    10.4.1 Beginnings and Maṇipravāḷam¶

    The origin of Malayalam is debated. The commonest view is that it developed from the western dialects of Tamil after about the ninth century; others hold that Malayalam and Tamil descend together from a common ancestor and diverged gradually, so that early Malayalam is a sister rather than a daughter of literary Tamil. Whatever its origin, its early literature followed two streams. One, pāṭṭu (“song”), used a Tamil-like idiom and Dravidian metres; its earliest major work is the Rāmacaritam, attributed to Cīrāman and dated variously between the twelfth and fourteenth centuries. The other, maṇipravāḷam (“rubies and coral”), mixed Malayalam and Sanskrit, using Sanskrit metres and inflected Sanskrit words. Its rules were set out in the Līlātilakam (late fourteenth century), a treatise in Sanskrit. Maṇipravāḷam literature includes campūs and messenger poems such as the Uṇṇunīlisandeśam (fourteenth century), and poems in praise of courtesans such as the Uṇṇiyacci Caritam.

    10.4.2 Eḻuttacchaṉ and the Bhakti Poets¶

    Ceṟuśśēri’s Kṛṣṇagātha (fifteenth century), a life of Kṛṣṇa in a simple, musical idiom, prepared the way for Tuñcattu Eḻuttacchaṉ (sixteenth century), traditionally regarded as the father of modern Malayalam. His Adhyātma Rāmāyaṇam and Mahābhāratam are composed in the kiḷippāṭṭu (“parrot song”) form, in which a parrot is invited to narrate the story. The Adhyātma Rāmāyaṇam is still read aloud in Kerala homes during the month of Karkkaṭakam. His contemporary Pūntānam Nampūtiri wrote the devotional Jñānappāna, and Mēlpattūr Nārāyaṇa Bhaṭṭatiri composed the Sanskrit Nārāyaṇīyam (1586), a condensed Bhāgavata still recited at Guruvāyūr.

    10.4.3 Āṭṭakkatha and Tuḷḷal¶

    In the seventeenth century the dramatic poems known as āṭṭakkatha (“stories for enactment”) were composed for the new dance-drama Kathakaḷi. The prince of Koṭṭārakkara is credited with the Rāmanāṭṭam plays from which Kathakaḷi developed; the Kōṭṭayam Tampurān composed four plays on Mahābhārata themes; Uṇṇāyi Vāriyar’s Naḷacaritam (eighteenth century), in four parts, is regarded as the finest literary text of the genre; and Irayimman Tampi (1783–1856) wrote the popular Kīcakavadham. In the eighteenth century Kuñcan Nampyār created tuḷḷal, a solo performance of Purāṇic stories in rapid Malayalam verse, full of satire on brahmins, landlords and the foibles of Kerala society (Table 10.4).

    10.4.4 Arabi-Malayalam and Christian Writing¶

    Kerala’s long connections across the Arabian Sea gave it literatures that the Sanskrit–Malayalam scheme leaves out. The Māppiḷa Muslims of the Malabar coast developed Arabi-Malayalam, Malayalam written in an adapted Arabic script and rich in Arabic, Persian and Tamil words, and in it a large literature of song (māppiḷappāṭṭu). Its earliest known work is the Muhyiddīn Māla (1607) of Qāḍī Muḥammad of Kozhikode, a song in praise of the Sufi saint ʿAbd al-Qādir al-Jīlānī that is still sung in Māppiḷa homes. Later genres included songs for weddings, songs of battle against the Portuguese and the British, and long verse romances, of which Moyinkutty Vaidyar’s Badar ul-Munīr Husn ul-Jamāl (nineteenth century) is the best known. The community’s learned writing was in Arabic: Zayn al-Dīn al-Maʿbarī’s Tuḥfat al-Mujāhidīn (late sixteenth century), a history of the Muslims of Malabar and of their war with the Portuguese, is one of the principal works of Arabic writing in India (Section 12.12). Stephen Dale’s study of the Māppiḷas places this literature in its social history (Dale 1980). The Syrian Christians of Kerala, for their part, kept their liturgy in Syriac and had their own songs in Malayalam, and the German Jesuit Johann Ernst Hanxleden (“Arnos Pāthiri”, d. 1732) wrote the Puttenpāna, a life of Christ in Malayalam verse modelled on the Hindu devotional pāna.

    10.4.5 The Modern Period¶

    Modern Malayalam literature was closely linked to social reform. Kumāran Āśān (1873–1924), a disciple of the reformer Śrī Nārāyaṇa Guru, wrote poems such as Duravastha and Caṇḍālabhikṣuki that attacked caste oppression, and Vaḷḷattōḷ Nārāyaṇa Mēnōn (1878–1958) founded the Kerala Kalamandalam to revive Kathakaḷi; the novel and the later movements are described below (Section 10.7). K. M. George’s survey covers the whole history of the literature (George 1968).

    10.5 Epic Retellings Compared¶

    The retelling of the epics was a defining enterprise of every South Indian literature, and comparing the versions shows how each poet adapted the story to a new audience. Pampa’s Kannada Mahābhārata glorifies a living patron and gives the defeated Karṇa and Duryodhana tragic dignity; the Telugu Mahābhārata of the kavitrayam recreates the epic in the courtly campū manner while remaining close to the story; Kumāravyāsa’s Kannada version (Section 10.2.6) is suffused with devotion to Kṛṣṇa; Eḻuttacchaṉ’s Malayalam Adhyātma Rāmāyaṇam follows a Sanskrit devotional retelling in which Rāma is the supreme God and every episode an occasion for worship; and Kampaṉ’s Tamil Irāmāvatāram (Chapter 7) combines devotion with a sympathetic, almost heroic portrayal of Rāvaṇa. Jain authors in each language produced their own versions of both epics, rejecting what they saw as the irrational elements of the Brahmanical stories. A. K. Ramanujan’s essay on “three hundred Rāmāyaṇas” famously argued that there is no single original against which these versions are deviations, but a whole family of tellings, each of which reshapes a common pool of story (Richman 1991). Beside these written retellings stood oral epics of local heroes, such as the Telugu cycle of the heroes of Palnāḍu and the Tamil story of the twin brothers of the Kongu country (Section 11.10).

    10.6 Literature for Performance: Yakṣagāna¶

    Yakṣagāna, the dance-drama of coastal and upland Karnataka, has its own literature of prasaṅgas: verse plays on episodes from the epics and Purāṇas, composed in Kannada song metres for a lead singer (bhāgavata) who sings the narrative while costumed actors dance and improvise spoken dialogue. The earliest surviving texts date from about the sixteenth century, and Pārti Subba of Kumbla (eighteenth century) is the most celebrated composer. A related Telugu yakṣagāna literature flourished at the Nāyaka courts of Tanjavur. Because the dialogue is improvised by the actors, often with considerable learning and wit, the written prasaṅga is only the framework of a performance whose verbal art is largely oral, and which no edition can fully record.

    10.7 The Modern Southern Literatures¶

    In all three languages the modern period brought prose fiction, the periodical press and new literary movements. In Kannada, the Navodaya (“new dawn”) movement of the 1920s to 1940s — Kuvempu, whose epic Śrī Rāmāyaṇa Darśanam won the first Jnanpith Award for Kannada, D. R. Bendre and Masti Venkatesha Iyengar — combined romantic lyricism with regional pride. It was followed by the modernist Navya writers, among them Gopalakrishna Adiga and U. R. Ananthamurthy, whose novel Saṃskāra (1965) dissects the crisis of a brahmin community; by Girish Karnad’s plays on historical and mythological themes; and from the 1970s by the Dalita–Bandāya (“Dalit and rebel”) movement of Siddalingaiah and Devanoor Mahadeva. Kannada writers have received eight Jnanpith Awards, more than writers in any other language except Hindi.

    In Telugu, the reformist writing of Kandukuri Veeresalingam and Gurajada Apparao was followed by the romantic bhāva kavitvam of the early twentieth century, the revolutionary poetry of Sri Sri (Mahāprasthānam, 1950), and later by feminist and Dalit poetry. Gudipati Venkatachalam (“Chalam”) scandalised his contemporaries with stories and essays defending women’s freedom in love and marriage; the Digambara poets of the 1960s attacked literary and social complacency in deliberately shocking verse; and the Revolutionary Writers’ Association (Virasam), founded in 1970, linked literature to the radical left movements of Andhra. From the 1990s feminist poets and Dalit writers, drawing on the anti-caste politics of the region, became central to Telugu letters.

    In Malayalam, the novel flourished from the late nineteenth century. Thakazhi Sivasankara Pillai’s Cemmīn (1956), a tragedy of a fishing community, became the best-known Malayalam novel in India and abroad; Vaikom Muhammad Basheer’s stories and short novels, written in a deceptively simple colloquial style, brought Muslim village life and the experience of prison and poverty into literature; O. V. Vijayan’s Khasākkinte Itihāsam (1969) opened a modernist phase; and M. T. Vasudevan Nair, Uroob and others explored the decline of the matrilineal joint family and the changes in Kerala society. Kamala Das’s autobiography Ente Katha (1973) caused a sensation by its frank treatment of a woman’s desire. Malayalam writers have received six Jnanpith Awards, from G. Sankara Kurup (1965) to Akkitham (2019). Together with a strong tradition of political and social poetry, this work made Malayalam one of the most widely read literatures in India, supported by Kerala’s high literacy and its network of public libraries.

    10.8 Women Writers of the Southern Literatures¶

    Women’s voices are present in each of these literatures, though often at the margins of the canon. In Kannada, Akka Mahādēvi and other women vacana poets of the twelfth century (Chapter 9) were followed by Saṅciya Honnamma, a woman at the Mysore court in the late seventeenth century, whose Hadibadeya Dharma (“The Duties of a Devoted Wife”) teaches conventional wifely virtue but also includes passages questioning why women should be held inferior. In Telugu, Ātukūri Molla, a woman of the potter caste, wrote a concise and popular Rāmāyaṇa in the sixteenth century, claiming in her introduction that she had no formal learning and wrote by the grace of God; in the eighteenth century Tarigoṇḍa Veṅgamāmba, a widow who refused the shaving of her head and lived as an ascetic devotee, wrote devotional poems and yakṣagānas; and the women poets of the Tanjavur courts have been described above. In Malayalam, the women of the Maṇipravāḷam age are known mainly as subjects of courtesan poems, but in the twentieth century Lalithambika Antharjanam, Balamani Amma and Kamala Das (above) became central figures of the literature (Section 13.8). Feminist scholars such as Susie Tharu and K. Lalita, in their anthology Women Writing in India (1991–93), have recovered many of these writers for modern readers (Tharu and Lalita 1991).

    10.9 Other Languages of the South and West Coast¶

    Besides the four major Dravidian literary languages, the south-west of India has other literary traditions. Tulu, spoken around Mangalore, has a rich oral literature of pāḍdana epics sung by women during agricultural work and in the rituals of the bhūta spirit cults, and a small written literature from the seventeenth century. Konkani, an Indo-Aryan language of Goa and the coast, was the language of some of the earliest printed books in India: Thomas Stephens, an English Jesuit, wrote the Kristapurāṇa (1616), a long poem on Christian scripture in a Marathi–Konkani idiom modelled on the Marathi Purāṇas. Suppressed under Portuguese rule, Konkani literature revived in the twentieth century; the essayist Ravindra Kelekar received the Jnanpith Award in 2006.
