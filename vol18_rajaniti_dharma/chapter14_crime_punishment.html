    Chapter 14: Crime, Punishment and Penance¶

    Manusmṛti 7.18

    daṇḍaḥ śāsti prajāḥ sarvā daṇḍa evābhirakṣati
    daṇḍaḥ supteṣu jāgarti daṇḍaṃ dharmaṃ vidur budhāḥ
    “Punishment disciplines all the subjects, punishment alone protects them, punishment stays awake while they sleep; the wise know punishment to be dharma.”

    The Sanskrit word daṇḍa means a staff or rod, and by extension the coercive power of the king, the army, and punishment. Indian political theory gave it a central place: the science of government was called daṇḍanīti, “the policy of the rod”, and the texts are unanimous that without punishment the strong would devour the weak (Chapter 5). This chapter examines what the Dharmaśāstras and the Arthaśāstra say about the justification of punishment, the classification of offences, the forms and measure of punishment, the treatment of suspects and prisoners, and the relation between royal punishment and religious penance (prāyaścitta).

    It is in criminal law that the hierarchical character of the classical legal order is most visible. The punishments of the Dharmaśāstras depended on the varṇa of offender and victim and on the sex of the offender and victim; the Arthaśāstra prescribed torture in interrogation and cruel forms of execution. The same texts also insist on proportion and on weighing the circumstances of an offence. Both sides are set out here (Singh 2017; Kane 1930, vols. 3–4).

    14.1 The Justification of Punishment¶

    14.1.1 Manu¶

    Manu’s chapter on the king contains an extended praise of punishment (Manu 7.14–31). The creator produced punishment, his own son, as the protector of all beings and as dharma itself; through fear of it all beings, moving and unmoving, are kept to their duties. Properly used, after due consideration, it makes all people happy; used without consideration, it destroys everything. “If the king did not tirelessly inflict punishment on those who deserve it, the stronger would roast the weaker like fish on a spit” (7.20); crows would eat the sacrificial cake, dogs would lick the oblations, no one would have ownership, and everything would be turned upside down (7.21). Punishment is “a great power, difficult to wield by those of untrained mind”; it destroys the king who deviates from dharma, together with his kin (7.28).

    The texts thus justify punishment primarily by its effects — deterrence and the protection of the social order — while also presenting it as the embodiment of dharma. Manu adds a religious motive for the offender: men who have committed crimes and have been punished by kings go to heaven, cleansed, like those who have done good deeds (8.318). Punishment by the king thus also had an expiatory function.

    14.1.2 The Arthaśāstra¶

    The Arthaśāstra’s justification is more purely political. Daṇḍa secures the acquisition and preservation of wealth and the stability of the sciences (AŚ 1.4.3–4). Its measure is decisive: “a king severe with the rod terrifies people; a king mild with the rod is despised; a king who metes out just punishment is honoured”. Punishment properly applied after full consideration endows subjects with dharma, wealth and pleasure; used wrongly, out of passion, anger or contempt, it enrages even forest-dwellers and ascetics; not used at all, it gives rise to the law of the fish (AŚ 1.4.7–14).

    14.1.3 The Measure of Punishment¶

    Both traditions insist that punishment must be proportionate. Manu instructs the king to consider the motive, the time and place, the capacity of the offender and the nature of the offence before inflicting punishment (8.126). He describes a gradation of measures: first a verbal reprimand, then a stern rebuke, third a fine, and after that corporal punishment; and if even corporal punishment does not restrain an offender, all four together (8.129–130). Repeat offenders are to be punished more heavily, and a first offence committed without intent more lightly. In the Dharmaśāstras, however, “the capacity of the offender” and “the nature of the offence” were both understood in terms of varṇa, so that proportion and inequality were combined in the same calculation.

    14.2 Classes of Offence¶

    14.2.1 The Titles of Criminal Law¶

    Among the eighteen titles of law (Chapter 12), five concern what we would call criminal matters: verbal assault (vākpāruṣya), physical assault (daṇḍapāruṣya), theft (steya), violence (sāhasa) — a category that includes robbery, homicide, arson and other crimes committed openly by force — and sexual offences against women (strīsaṃgrahaṇa). Theft was distinguished from violence by its secrecy: what is done openly by force is sāhasa, what is done secretly is theft. The Arthaśāstra’s Book 4 adds a wide range of offences against public order: offences by artisans and merchants, by officials and judges, by counterfeiters and adulterators, by those who harbour criminals, and by those who conspire against the king.

    14.2.2 Grave Sins¶

    The legal categories were paralleled by the religious category of “grave sins” (mahāpātaka): killing a Brahmin, drinking liquor, theft (of a Brahmin’s gold), sexual intercourse with the wife of one’s teacher, and associating with those who commit these sins (Manu 11.55). The list reflects Brahmanical priorities: the killing of a Brahmin heads it, and the drinking of liquor is a grave sin for Brahmins in particular. Lesser sins (upapātaka) include killing a cow, usury, adultery, the sale of a wife or child, and teaching or learning the Veda for pay. These categories governed the penances described below, but they also affected punishment by the king, who was to brand those guilty of grave sins and banish them (Manu 9.235–239).

    14.2.3 Intention and Accident¶

    The texts distinguish intentional and unintentional acts. Penances for unintentional sins are lighter than for intentional ones, and some authorities hold that intentional sins cannot be expiated at all (Manu 11.45–46). In criminal law, accidental injury — for example by a driver of a cart whose animals run out of control — is treated differently from intentional assault, and the texts discuss at length the responsibility of drivers, owners of animals and persons who cause injury unknowingly (Manu 8.290–298). Modern writers have compared these distinctions with the common-law concept of mens rea, although the Indian texts never develop a general theory of criminal intent.

    14.3 Punishment by Varṇa¶

    14.3.1 The Dharmaśāstric Principle¶

    The most important feature of Dharmaśāstric criminal law is that the punishment for a given act depended on the varṇa of the offender and of the victim. The principle runs through every title of criminal law in the Manusmṛti and, with variations, the other smṛtis (Chapter 10):

    • Verbal abuse (Manu 8.267–272): A Kṣatriya who reviles a Brahmin is fined 100 paṇas, a Vaiśya 150 or 200, and a Śūdra is to receive corporal punishment; a Brahmin who reviles a Kṣatriya pays 50, a Vaiśya 25, a Śūdra 12. A Śūdra who insults twice-born men with gross abuse has his tongue cut out; one who mentions their names and castes contemptuously has a red-hot iron nail ten fingers long thrust into his mouth; one who presumes to instruct Brahmins in their dharma has hot oil poured into his mouth and ears.

    • Physical assault (8.279–283): A low-born man who strikes a superior loses the limb with which he struck; one who raises his hand or stick has his hand cut off; one who kicks has his foot cut off; one who sits on the same seat as a superior is branded on the hip and banished, or has his buttocks cut; one who spits on him loses his lips.

    • Sexual offences (8.359–385): Penalties vary with the varṇas of the man and woman and with whether the woman is “guarded”. A Śūdra who has intercourse with a guarded woman of a twice-born varṇa loses his limb and all his property, or his life (8.374); a Brahmin who does the same with a guarded woman pays a fine (8.378–385).

    • Capital punishment (8.379–381): A Brahmin is never to be executed. Shaving his head is the equivalent of execution for him; at most he is banished with his property intact, “for there is no greater injustice on earth than killing a Brahmin”.

    • Places of punishment (8.124–125): The ten “places” on which punishment may be inflicted — the genitals, belly, tongue, hands, feet, eyes, nose, ears, property and body — apply to the three lower varṇas; a Brahmin should depart unharmed.

    The one important exception is theft, where the texts reverse the gradation: Manu makes the guilt and fine of a thief eightfold for a Śūdra, sixteenfold for a Vaiśya, thirty-twofold for a Kṣatriya and sixty-four-fold or more for a Brahmin, because the higher-born man knows the gravity of what he does (8.337–338); the verse before them fines the king a thousandfold (8.336; Chapter 5). These rules express a principle that knowledge and status bring responsibility, and they show that the texts did not simply exempt the high-born. But they are exceptions within a system whose general rule protected Brahmins and degraded Śūdras.

    14.3.2 Interpretation¶

    Defenders of the tradition have sometimes argued that the varṇa-graded penalties expressed a rational differentiation of responsibility, or that they were rarely applied. The first argument is not supported by the texts, whose penalties for offences by Śūdras against Brahmins are the most severe in the whole law; the second cannot be tested, because we have so little evidence of actual criminal trials before the medieval period, but it does not change what the texts prescribe and what their authority later legitimated. B. R. Ambedkar singled out these provisions as proof that the śāstras could not be the basis of a just society (Ambedkar 1936); R. S. Sharma used them to reconstruct the legal disabilities of the Śūdras (Sharma 1958); and the Constitution’s guarantee of equality before the law (Article 14) is in direct contradiction with them.

    14.3.3 The Arthaśāstra¶

    The Arthaśāstra also grades penalties by varṇa in some places — for example, in its rules on verbal abuse and sexual offences — but it relies more consistently on fines and treats many offences without reference to varṇa. It exempts Brahmins from torture: for any crime, a Brahmin is not to be tortured, but is to be branded on the forehead with a mark indicating his crime — a dog for theft, a headless body for murder, and so on — proclaimed, and banished or sent to the mines (AŚ 4.8). Its frequent use of fines for offences that the smṛtis punish corporally reflects, in part, its interest in revenue.

    14.4 Forms of Punishment¶

    14.4.1 Fines¶

    Fines were the commonest punishment. The texts define three standard levels of the “violence fine” (sāhasa): in Manu, 250 paṇas for the lowest, 500 for the middle and 1,000 for the highest (8.138); in the Arthaśāstra, 48 to 96 paṇas for the lowest, 200 to 500 for the middle and 500 to 1,000 for the highest (AŚ 3.17). Many penalties are expressed by reference to these levels. Fines went to the king’s treasury; compensation to the victim was also required in cases of injury and damage to property.

    14.4.2 Corporal Punishment and Mutilation¶

    Corporal punishment ranged from flogging to branding and the cutting off of limbs — fingers, hands, feet, tongue, nose, ears or lips. The Arthaśāstra provides for the redemption of mutilation by the payment of fines on a fixed scale — so many paṇas for the tip of a finger, more for a hand or foot (AŚ 4.10) — which made mutilation a punishment chiefly for those who could not pay.

    14.4.3 Execution¶

    Death was prescribed for grave offences: murder, robbery with violence, certain thefts, sexual offences by men of low varṇa against women of high varṇa, treason and conspiracy against the king, and some offences against public works such as breaking a dam. The Arthaśāstra describes methods of execution that include impalement, burning and being torn apart by animals, and distinguishes execution with and without torture (AŚ 4.11). It prescribes death for anyone who kills an elephant in the royal forests (AŚ 2.2). The texts that exempt Brahmins from execution substitute shaving of the head, branding and banishment.

    14.4.4 Banishment and Loss of Caste¶

    Banishment from the kingdom, and expulsion from the caste by its own members, were severe punishments in a society in which a person’s livelihood and identity depended on membership of a group. Loss of caste (patitatva) entailed exclusion from commensality, marriage and inheritance, and the texts describe the ritual by which the kinsmen of a fallen man overturn a water pot to signify his social death (Manu 11.183–185) and the procedure for readmitting him after penance.

    14.4.5 Imprisonment¶

    Imprisonment was used chiefly for detaining suspects, debtors and those awaiting punishment, rather than as a sentence in its own right. The Arthaśāstra describes a prison (bandhanāgāra) with separate wards for men and women (AŚ 2.5), and punishes prison officials who torment prisoners, deny them food or water, or sexually abuse women in their custody (AŚ 4.9). It also provides that prisoners — the young, the old, the sick and the helpless among them — be released on the day of the king’s birth constellation and on full-moon days, and that prisoners be released on the occasion of a conquest or the installation of a crown prince (AŚ 2.36). Aśoka records that he released prisoners on the anniversaries of his consecration (Chapter 8). These provisions show concern for the conditions of detention; they do not amount to a theory of rehabilitation through imprisonment, which was a nineteenth-century European idea.

    14.5 The Arthaśāstra’s “Removal of Thorns”¶

    Book 4 of the Arthaśāstra, “The Removal of Thorns” (kaṇṭakaśodhana), is the fullest account of crime control in classical Indian literature. Its magistrates (pradeṣṭṛ) deal with artisans and merchants who cheat, with officials who embezzle or take bribes, and with criminals of all kinds. Their main tools are secret agents. Agents posing as holy men are to identify those who are living beyond their means, consorting with prostitutes, or seeking magical help; agents are also to provoke crimes — for instance by tempting a suspected official with a bribe or a seditious proposal — and then expose those who succumb (AŚ 4.4–5). Suspects are to be arrested on circumstantial grounds: unexplained wealth, a change of residence, signs of injury, possession of stolen goods.

    Suspects may be interrogated, and where the evidence is strong but the suspect will not confess, tortured. The text describes a graded series of tortures (karma), including beating, flogging, suspension and the water-tube, and lays down that they are not to be applied to pregnant women, to women within a month of childbirth, to minors, the aged, the sick, the intoxicated, the insane, those exhausted by hunger, thirst or travel, and some others (AŚ 4.8). It also warns that innocent persons under torture may confess to crimes they did not commit, giving examples, and requires that a confession be corroborated. The cautions are notable, but the procedure as a whole is the opposite of a presumption of innocence and of a prohibition of torture (Olivelle 2013; Singh 2017).

    14.6 Theft, Violence and Sudden Death¶

    14.6.1 Theft¶

    Manu treats theft at length (8.301–343). The king is to make special efforts to suppress thieves, for by protecting his subjects from them his fame and his kingdom grow; a king who takes taxes but does not suppress thieves takes on their sins. Punishments are graded by the value and kind of property stolen: small thefts of grain or goods are punished with fines several times the value, theft of larger amounts, of precious metals, of men and women of good family, or of important animals with mutilation or death. A thief caught with the stolen property, or with the instruments of burglary, is to be punished, and whoever gives food, shelter or help to thieves is to be punished like a thief. A thief who confesses and approaches the king with a club, asking to be punished, is either killed or released; in either case he is purified (8.314–316). Some takings are excused: a twice-born traveller who has run out of provisions and takes two stalks of sugarcane or two radishes from another’s field is not to be fined (8.341).

    14.6.2 Violence¶

    The category of sāhasa, “violence”, covers offences committed openly by force: robbery, homicide, assault on women, arson and the destruction of property. Nārada divides it into three degrees according to the object — fruits and similar goods, clothes and animals, and human life or wives — and Manu states that the man who commits violence is worse than a slanderer, a thief or one who strikes with a stick, and that a king must never pardon him (8.344–347). In self-defence, in protecting women and Brahmins, or in a war undertaken for dharma, a man who kills commits no offence; and one may kill without hesitation an assailant (ātatāyin) who comes with intent to murder, even if he is one’s teacher or a learned Brahmin (8.348–351).

    14.6.3 Insult and Defamation¶

    Verbal assault (vākpāruṣya) covered abuse, insult and defamation. Apart from the varṇa-graded penalties described above, Manu fines a man who calls another one-eyed, lame or the like — even if it is true — and imposes a larger fine on one who reviles another’s mother, father, wife, brother, son or teacher, or who refuses to make way for his teacher (8.274–275). Fines between persons of equal varṇa are lower than those across varṇas. Nārada distinguishes degrees of verbal assault according to the gravity of the words used — harsh speech, obscene speech and speech imputing grave sins — and treats false accusation of a grave crime as particularly serious. These rules reflect a society in which honour and reputation were valuable and fragile, and in which insult across lines of rank was treated as a threat to the social order.

    14.6.4 Harm to Animals and Plants¶

    The criminal law also protected animals and plants, in a manner that reflects both their economic value and religious sentiment. Manu prescribes fines for damaging trees according to their use — fruit trees, flowering trees, creepers and shrubs — and for injuring men and animals according to the pain caused, with compensation for the cost of treatment (8.285–287). Killing large domestic animals, and above all cows, attracted heavy penalties and penances; the killing of a cow is listed among the lesser sins (upapātaka) and required an elaborate penance (11.109–117). The Arthaśāstra fines those who kill protected animals or animals in sanctuaries, and those who injure cattle (Chapter 6). These provisions protected property, livelihood and religiously valued beings within a hierarchy of value that placed the cow above most humans of low status.

    14.6.5 Investigating Sudden Death¶

    The Arthaśāstra contains a chapter on the investigation of sudden deaths (AŚ 4.7). The magistrate is to examine the body of a person who has died suddenly for signs of strangulation, hanging, drowning, poisoning or beating — swollen hands and feet, protruding eyes, marks on the neck, discoloration and so on — and to inquire into the dead person’s quarrels, debts and relationships in order to discover whether the death was caused by another. The chapter is a striking example of the text’s empirical approach to crime, written as practical guidance for officials.

    14.6.6 Offences by Officials¶

    Both traditions treat crimes by officials severely. The Arthaśāstra lists punishments for judges who threaten, abuse or silence litigants, ask irrelevant questions, delay cases, or decide unjustly; for clerks who falsify records; and for prison officials who abuse prisoners (AŚ 4.9). Manu requires the king to confiscate the property of officials who take bribes from litigants and banish them (9.231). These rules show an awareness that the agents of justice could themselves be sources of injustice.

    14.7 Penance¶

    14.7.1 Prāyaścitta¶

    The Dharmaśāstras’ third division, after conduct and law, is prāyaścitta, penance or expiation. Wrongdoing, in this view, produces sin (pāpa), which brings suffering in this life and the next, including rebirth in lower forms; penance removes or reduces it. The penances range from fasts, recitations of Vedic texts and gifts to Brahmins to severe austerities. For the killing of a Brahmin, Manu prescribes that the killer live for twelve years in a hut in the forest, carrying the skull of the dead man as a banner and living on alms (11.73), or undergo other equally severe penances. For lesser sins there are graded fasts, of which the best known are the kṛcchra and the cāndrāyaṇa, in which the amount of food eaten is increased and decreased with the phases of the moon.

    14.7.2 Penance and the Social Order¶

    Penance was not only a private religious matter. It was imposed by assemblies of learned Brahmins (pariṣad), which decided what penance a sinner should perform, and it was the condition of readmission to the caste for those who had been outcast. In this sense prāyaścitta was a form of social discipline administered by the Brahmin community, alongside the king’s daṇḍa. Its penances, too, were graded by varṇa: the penance for killing a Kṣatriya was a quarter of that for killing a Brahmin, for killing a Vaiśya an eighth, and for killing a Śūdra a sixteenth (Manu 11.127), and the killing of a Śūdra was placed on a level with the killing of certain animals.

    14.7.3 Readmission and Reconversion¶

    Penance was also the means by which those who had lost caste could be readmitted. Some later smṛtis, such as the one attributed to Devala, prescribe penances for persons who had been forcibly carried off or converted by foreigners and wished to return to their community — a sign that such situations were known in the period of the texts’ composition. In the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries the Ārya Samāj and other Hindu organisations developed rites of “purification” (śuddhi) to receive converts to Hinduism and to readmit those who had converted to other religions, sometimes also to raise the status of low castes; these campaigns became entangled with the communal politics of the period. The history illustrates how a classical institution of caste discipline was adapted to new social and political purposes.

    14.7.4 Punishment and Penance Compared¶

    Scholars have discussed how far the domains of punishment and penance overlapped. The texts envisage both for many offences: the king punishes, and the offender also performs a penance for the sin. Some authorities held that a person who had been punished by the king was thereby purified (Manu 8.318). The relation between the two is better described as a coordination of royal and Brahmanical authority than as a “dual-track” system of punishment and rehabilitation in a modern sense (Kane 1930, vol. 4; Olivelle and Davis 2018). The system was centred on purity and status rather than on the victim.

    14.8 Punishment in Later Indian States¶

    What is known of punishment in practice in medieval and early modern India suggests both continuity and change. Inscriptions record fines, confiscation and banishment, and the endowment of temple lamps or other gifts as a form of compensation and expiation for offences such as accidental homicide (Chapter 12). Chroniclers describe kings inflicting mutilation and cruel executions on rebels and traitors, and travellers report harsh punishments for theft and robbery. Under the Delhi Sultanate and the Mughals, Islamic criminal law distinguished fixed penalties for certain offences, retaliation or compensation for homicide and injury at the choice of the victim’s heirs, and discretionary punishments by the judge or ruler; in practice much criminal justice was administered by governors and police officials exercising the ruler’s discretionary authority (Chapter 18). In the Maratha and other regional states, fines and confiscation were common, and caste and village councils punished breaches of caste rules by fines and outcasting.

    The varṇa-graded penalties of the Dharmaśāstras were thus never the whole of Indian penal practice, and for long periods they were not the law of the state at all. But the principle that status should affect liability, and the exemption of Brahmins from corporal and capital punishment, were widely respected by Hindu rulers, and the idea that caste councils could punish their members persisted into the colonial period and beyond.

    14.9 From Daṇḍanīti to the Penal Code¶

    Islamic criminal law, administered in the courts of the Delhi Sultanate and the Mughal empire, and the criminal law of the East India Company, which retained Islamic criminal law in modified form in Bengal until the nineteenth century, replaced the Dharmaśāstric penal law in most of India long before independence (Chapters 18 and 19). The Indian Penal Code, drafted by a Law Commission under Thomas Babington Macaulay in the 1830s and enacted in 1860, owed nothing to Dharmaśāstra and was based on English and continental models; it applied the same penalties to all, regardless of caste or religion, though the colonial state also enacted discriminatory laws such as the Criminal Tribes Act of 1871. Claims that the Penal Code’s structure “parallels” the Arthaśāstra, or that it adopted a three-tier classification derived from the sāhasa fines, have no basis. In 2023 the Penal Code, the Code of Criminal Procedure and the Evidence Act were replaced by new statutes (Chapter 20).

    Independent India retained the death penalty, but the courts have narrowed its use. In Bachan Singh v. State of Punjab (1980) the Supreme Court upheld its constitutionality while holding that it should be imposed only in the “rarest of rare” cases, after weighing aggravating and mitigating circumstances relating both to the crime and to the criminal. The classical texts, too, prescribed death for grave crimes while exempting Brahmins and urging the weighing of circumstances; the modern doctrine differs fundamentally in that it applies the same standard to every person, whatever his birth.

    14.11 Summary¶

    • Punishment (daṇḍa) was justified as the protector of order and as dharma itself, and was to be measured to the offence and the offender.

    • In the Dharmaśāstras, punishments depend on the varṇa of offender and victim, with Brahmins exempt from execution and corporal punishment; theft is the main exception. The Arthaśāstra relies more on fines but prescribes torture for suspects and cruel executions.

    • Penance (prāyaścitta), administered by Brahmin assemblies and graded by varṇa, worked alongside royal punishment; classical penal law was later displaced by Islamic and then colonial criminal law.

    Further Reading¶

    The principal texts are Manu chapters 7–9 and 11 (Olivelle 2005) and AŚ Books 3–4 (Olivelle 2013). Kane (1930), vols. 3–4, surveys punishment and penance. Singh (2017) is the best study of violence and punishment in early India; Sharma (1958) and Ambedkar (1936) discuss the varṇa provisions critically. Singha (1998) examines the transition to colonial criminal law.
