    Chapter 13: Debt, Contract, Property and Inheritance¶

    Most of the eighteen titles of law concern what modern lawyers would call private or civil law: debts and their recovery, deposits and pledges, sales, partnerships, gifts, wages, agreements, boundaries and inheritance. The Dharmaśāstras and the Arthaśāstra treat these subjects in considerable detail, and they are among the parts of the tradition that most impress modern readers with their technical precision. They also show the economic world in which the texts were written: a world of money-lenders and merchants, guilds and caravans, cultivators, herdsmen and labourers, slaves and bonded servants, and of joint families holding land across generations.

    This chapter surveys the main areas of this law. It follows the order of the titles only loosely, and it notes, where relevant, how the rules depended on varṇa and gender. Inheritance is treated here in outline; the schools of the Mitākṣarā and Dāyabhāga are described in Chapter 11, and women’s property in Chapter 15. Kane’s third volume remains the fullest survey of the titles discussed here (Kane 1930, vol. 3; Lingat 1973).

    13.1 Debt¶

    13.1.1 The First Title¶

    The non-payment of debts (ṛṇādāna) is the first of the eighteen titles in every list, and the texts discuss it at greatest length. Manu’s treatment (8.47–178) is the longest of any title, partly because he uses the suit for debt as the model case in which to set out the rules on witnesses; Yājñavalkya and Nārada also give it long sections. The word ṛṇa also carried a religious meaning — every man is born with debts to the seers, the gods and the ancestors (Chapter 2) — and the idea that debts must be paid, in this life or the next, gave the law of debt a moral weight. A debtor who died without paying, Nārada says, would be reborn as the creditor’s slave or animal.

    13.1.2 Interest¶

    The texts regulate interest (vṛddhi, “growth”) in detail. Manu permits a lender to take one-eightieth of the principal per month — one and a quarter per cent — a rate he attributes to Vasiṣṭha, or two per cent per month “remembering the dharma of good people”; but he also allows rates of two, three, four and five per cent per month according to the varṇa of the borrower, from Brahmin to Śūdra (Manu 8.140–142). Yājñavalkya gives the same scheme: one-eightieth per month when there is a pledge, and otherwise two, three, four or five per cent according to varṇa (Yājñ 2.37). The graded rates are sometimes explained as reflecting differences in risk; whatever their rationale, they meant that the poorest and lowest-ranked borrowers paid the most.

    Two important limits protected debtors. First, the total interest collected on a loan of money, when paid at one time, could not exceed the principal — so that the debt could at most double — while on grain, fruit, wool and draught animals the limit was five times the principal (Manu 8.151). This rule, known in later Indian law as dāmdupat, was applied by British courts in parts of India into the twentieth century. Second, Manu forbids several kinds of oppressive interest: interest for more than a year at a time, interest not agreed, compound interest (cakravṛddhi), and “corporal” interest exacted in labour (8.153). For loans on sea voyages and on long journeys, where risk was high, the rate was to be fixed by people experienced in such voyages (8.157) — a provision often compared with the Roman fenus nauticum, the high-interest maritime loan.

    13.1.3 Recovery¶

    Manu describes five ways in which a creditor may recover a debt: by persuasion (dharma), by suit at law (vyavahāra), by stratagem (chala), by customary pressure (ācarita) — such as sitting fasting at the debtor’s door — and by force (bala) (8.49). A creditor who recovers his money by these means is not to be blamed by the king (8.50). Self-help, in other words, was legitimate. A debtor who could not pay might be made to work off the debt: Manu says that a debtor of the same or lower varṇa may be made to pay by labour, while a debtor of higher varṇa should pay gradually (8.177). The texts thus recognise a form of debt bondage, graded by varṇa, which the Arthaśāstra also regulates (Chapter 6). Nārada adds that a debtor who denies a debt proved against him must pay it with a fine.

    13.1.4 Liability for a Father’s Debts¶

    The smṛtis require sons, and in some cases grandsons, to pay the debts of a father who has died, gone abroad or become incapable; Yājñavalkya excepts debts incurred for liquor, lust or gambling, unpaid fines and tolls, and idle promises (Yājñ 2.47–50). The colonial courts made this into the doctrine of the “pious obligation” of sons to pay their father’s debts not incurred for illegal or immoral purposes, which allowed creditors of a Mitākṣarā father to proceed against the ancestral property held jointly with his sons.

    13.1.5 Sureties and Pledges¶

    A creditor could secure a loan by a surety (pratibhū) or by a pledge (ādhi). Yājñavalkya distinguishes three kinds of suretyship: for the appearance of the debtor, for his honesty, and for payment; only the surety for payment, and in some cases his sons, could be made to pay the debt itself (Yājñ 2.53–54). Pledges were either merely held as security or used and enjoyed by the creditor. An enjoyed pledge, such as a field or a cow whose produce the creditor took, earned no further interest (Manu 8.143); a pledge merely held had to be preserved, and the creditor was liable if it was damaged through his fault. Yājñavalkya states that a pledge is forfeited to the creditor if it is not redeemed when the debt has doubled, except a pledge that is being enjoyed (Yājñ 2.58). These rules, the precursors of the forms of usufructuary and simple mortgage known to later Indian law, show the importance of land and cattle as security in an agrarian credit economy.

    13.1.6 Documents¶

    The later texts give particular attention to documents of debt. Yājñavalkya and Kātyāyana describe how a loan document should be written — with the names of the parties and their fathers, the date, the amount, the rate of interest and the signatures of the debtor and witnesses — and how payments should be endorsed on it, or a receipt given. A debt proved by a document could be recovered from the debtor’s heirs; disputes over documents were resolved by the methods described in Chapter 12.

    13.1.7 Debt in Colonial India¶

    The classical rules on debt had a long afterlife. Under British rule, the courts applied the rule of dāmdupat — that interest could not exceed the principal — to Hindus in the Bombay and Calcutta presidencies, while elsewhere English rules of contract prevailed. The enforcement of debts through civil courts, and the sale of land for debt, contributed to the transfer of land from cultivators to moneylenders in many regions. In 1875 peasants in the Deccan attacked moneylenders and destroyed their account books; the government responded with the Deccan Agriculturists’ Relief Act of 1879, which allowed courts to examine the history of transactions and reduce excessive claims, and later laws restricted the alienation of agricultural land. These measures were colonial responses to colonial problems, but they echo the classical concern to limit the creditor’s power over the debtor’s livelihood.

    13.2 Deposits, Sales and Gifts¶

    13.2.1 Deposits¶

    The title of deposits (nikṣepa) covers property entrusted to another for safe keeping. The texts distinguish an open deposit, counted or described in the presence of witnesses, from a sealed deposit (upanidhi), handed over in a sealed container. The depositary must return the deposit on demand. He is not liable if it is lost through the action of the king, through fate or through thieves, unless the loss occurred after the owner had demanded it back or through the depositary’s own fault; but if he uses the deposit without permission, he must pay it back with interest and is fined (Manu 8.179–196; Yājñ 2.65–67). The same rules apply, with variations, to property lent for use, property handed to an artisan to be worked, and property entrusted to a messenger.

    13.2.2 Sale by a Non-owner¶

    The third title concerns sale by one who is not the owner (asvāmivikraya). Manu declares such a sale invalid: the true owner may recover his property, and the seller who is not the owner is to be treated as a thief (8.197–198). A buyer who bought openly, in the market or before witnesses, is free from blame and may seek recompense from the seller; a buyer who bought secretly or at an unreasonably low price is under suspicion (8.201–202). The rule that one cannot give a better title than one has is familiar in many legal systems; the Dharmaśāstra version combines it with the practical recognition that open purchase in a market was evidence of good faith.

    13.2.3 Rescission¶

    Manu allows either party to a sale or purchase who repents of the transaction to cancel it within ten days; after ten days, the transaction stands (8.222–223). Nārada sets different periods for the inspection of different kinds of goods and provides that goods found defective may be returned. The Arthaśāstra fixes periods within which merchants, cultivators, herdsmen and artisans may withdraw from a sale, and regulates the conduct of merchants and artisans as part of its “removal of thorns” (Chapter 6).

    13.2.4 The Law of Gift¶

    Giving (dāna) was at the centre of Brahmanical religion and of kingship: the Vedic fee to the priest (Chapter 2), the king’s grants of land (Chapter 5), the marriage of a daughter conceived as a gift (Chapter 15). The title on gifts (dattasyānapakarma, “non-delivery” or “resumption” of gifts; in Nārada, dattāpradānika) is the part of the law that regulated this practice, and it is less a law of charity than a law of who may alienate what.

    The basic rule is stated by Yājñavalkya: a man may give what is his own, without detriment to his family, but not his wife or son, nor his whole property if he has descendants, nor what he has already promised to another (Yājñ 2.175); the acceptance of a gift, above all of land, should be made openly (2.176). Nārada’s title on gifts (Nārada 4) adds property that is not one’s own to give — a deposit, a pledge, property held jointly with others or borrowed — and distinguishes valid gifts from void ones: gifts made under fear, in anger or grief, in jest, by mistake, by a minor or by an insane or intoxicated person are invalid and may be recovered, as are gifts made in return for a service that was not performed. The commentators analysed a gift as the giver’s relinquishing of his own ownership and the creation of the recipient’s, completed by acceptance; once so completed it could not be resumed, which is what the name of the title expresses. The rules therefore protected two interests at once: the family’s claim on its property, against a donor’s piety or folly, and the recipient’s title, against a donor’s change of mind.

    Beside this law of alienation stood an immense religious literature on the merit of giving. It specified the worthy recipient (pātra), above all the learned Brahmin, the proper times and places, the right objects and the disposition of the giver, and it described “great gifts” (mahādāna), such as the weighing of a king against gold that was then distributed to Brahmins (tulāpuruṣa), which inscriptions show rulers actually performing. The medieval digests gave the subject whole books — the Dānakāṇḍa of Lakṣmīdhara’s Kṛtyakalpataru, the Dānasāgara of the Bengal king Ballālasena, the Dānakhaṇḍa of Hemādri’s Caturvargacintāmaṇi — and Kane’s survey of them remains the standard account (Kane 1930, vol. 2).

    The two literatures met in the gift of land. The charters by which kings granted villages to Brahmins and temples (Chapter 17) are legal instruments of gift: they name the donor’s title, define the object, record the acceptance and declare the gift perpetual, and their imprecatory verses add a religious sanction to the legal rule that a completed gift may not be taken back. What a royal gift transferred was in most cases the king’s revenue and rights over a village rather than the soil itself, a limit that Mīmāṃsā authors expressed in their argument that even a universal monarch cannot give away the earth (Section 13.5). The law of gift also shaped women’s property, much of which consisted of gifts made at and after marriage (Chapter 15), and it supplied the framework within which temples, monasteries and Brahmin settlements accumulated the endowments on which so much of medieval religious and economic life depended.

    13.3 Partnership, Guilds and Conventions¶

    13.3.1 Partnership¶

    The title of partnership (saṃbhūya-samutthāna, “rising up together”) covers joint undertakings by merchants, artisans, cultivators and also priests who perform a sacrifice together. The general rule is that profits and losses are shared in proportion to each partner’s contribution, unless otherwise agreed; a partner who causes loss through negligence or acts without the agreement of the others must make good the loss, while one who saves the common property from danger is entitled to a reward (Nārada; Yājñ 2.259–265). Manu discusses at length how priests who officiate jointly at a sacrifice should share the fee (8.206–211). The rules show the importance of collaborative enterprise in trade and crafts.

    13.3.2 Caravans and Sea Trade¶

    Long-distance trade raised its own legal problems. Merchants travelled in caravans (sārtha) under a leader (sārthavāha), and the texts discuss the sharing of profits and losses among the members, the responsibility of the leader, and the liability of merchants whose goods were lost to robbers, to the king’s officials or to accident. Nārada and Yājñavalkya hold that a partner who saves the common goods from danger — from fire, robbers or the king — is entitled to a tenth of what he saves, and that goods lost without anyone’s fault are a common loss. Sea voyages, which the Dharmaśāstras regarded with suspicion as polluting for Brahmins, were nonetheless undertaken by merchants of many communities, and the texts’ provision for special rates of interest on sea loans (Manu 8.157) shows that lenders financed them. Inscriptions and the accounts of foreign observers describe the ports and the merchant communities of the Indian Ocean in detail; that history belongs to Volume 19.

    13.3.3 Guilds and Corporate Bodies¶

    The title “breach of conventions” (saṃvid-vyatikrama) concerns the agreements (saṃvid, samaya) of villages, guilds (śreṇī), merchant associations (naigama), corporations (pūga) and other groups. The king is to enforce such agreements: a member who breaks the convention of his group, or who steals or misappropriates its common property, is to be banished and his property confiscated (Manu 8.219–221; Yājñ 2.186–192). Yājñavalkya and Bṛhaspati describe the officers who manage a group’s affairs, and require that they be honest, learned in the Veda and of good family; they forbid actions by the officers that harm the group and require that the group’s decisions be followed. These rules, together with the evidence of inscriptions, show that guilds and similar bodies made their own rules, held property, received endowments and settled their members’ disputes (Majumdar 1918).

    Inscriptions give life to these texts. At Nasik, in the early second century CE, Uṣavadāta deposited money with two weavers’ guilds as a permanent endowment, the interest of which was to clothe the monks of a cave monastery (Chapter 6). At Mandasor in western Malwa, an inscription of the fifth century CE records how a guild of silk-weavers who had migrated from Lāṭa (southern Gujarat) built and later restored a temple of the Sun. Medieval South Indian inscriptions record merchant associations, such as the “Five Hundred” (Ayyāvoḷe), operating across regions and overseas. Tamil inscriptions of merchant associations have been found outside India — at Takua Pa in southern Thailand (associated with the Maṇigrāmam, 9th century) and at Barus in Sumatra (of the Five Hundred, dated 1088) — showing that these bodies carried their organisation and their record-keeping across the Bay of Bengal. Modern writers sometimes describe the śreṇī as a “corporation” with “legal personality” and “perpetual succession”. The texts do treat the guild as a body that holds property and makes binding rules; the categories of company law are nonetheless not those of the śāstras, and the guild is better described in its own terms.

    13.4 Wages, Service and Slavery¶

    The titles on non-payment of wages (vetanādāna) and, in Nārada, on breach of a contract of service (abhyupetyāśuśrūṣā) regulate the relation between master and worker. A hired servant who abandons his work without cause forfeits his wages and is fined; a master who refuses to pay is compelled to pay and fined. Where no wage was agreed, the servant receives a customary share — a tenth of the produce for a cultivator, a tenth of the profit for a trader’s assistant, as the Arthaśāstra also provides (AŚ 3.13; Chapter 6). Herdsmen’s wages and liabilities are treated under the title of disputes between owner and herdsman (below).

    Nārada distinguishes five kinds of dependent worker: the student, the apprentice, the hired servant, the overseer and the slave. The first four do “pure” work; the slave does “impure” work, such as sweeping, cleaning latrines and removing refuse. The apprentice (antevāsin) lives with his master for an agreed period, receives instruction and maintenance, and may be compelled to complete his term. Nārada lists fifteen kinds of slave, among them those born in the house, bought, received as a gift, inherited, maintained in a famine, pledged, released from a heavy debt, captured in war, won in a wager, self-sold and enslaved for a fixed period; and he describes the ceremony of manumission, in which the master breaks a jar of water carried by the slave and sprinkles him with water (Nārada 5). A slave could be freed if he saved his master’s life; those who had sold themselves could not easily recover their freedom. These rules, like Manu’s seven kinds of slave and the Arthaśāstra’s chapter on slaves, show that slavery was an established legal institution in classical India (Chakravarti 1987).

    13.4.1 The End of Legal Slavery and the Persistence of Bondage¶

    Slavery remained a legal institution in India until the colonial period. The East India Company at first tolerated it; under pressure from the abolitionist movement in Britain, the Indian Slavery Act of 1843 provided that slavery would no longer be recognised by the courts, and the Indian Penal Code of 1860 made the buying, selling and holding of slaves criminal offences. These measures ended legal slavery but not unfree labour: agrarian servitude, often hereditary and tied to caste, and debt bondage persisted in many regions. Independent India’s Constitution prohibits forced labour (Article 23), and the Bonded Labour System (Abolition) Act of 1976 declared all bonded-labour agreements void; but bonded labour has continued to be documented in agriculture, brick-kilns and other industries. The classical texts’ regulation of slavery and debt bondage thus has a long and troubling sequel.

    13.5 Land, Boundaries and Cattle¶

    13.5.1 Who Owned the Land?¶

    Whether the king owned all the land of his kingdom was a question much debated in the colonial period, when the East India Company claimed the rights of the Mughal state and argued that cultivators held land only at the sovereign’s pleasure. Classical Indian thinkers had already discussed the question. In the Mīmāṃsā, a sacrifice called the viśvajit required the sacrificer to give away “everything”; Jaimini and his commentator Śabara argued that even a universal monarch could not give away the earth, because it is common to all, and that the king’s right was a right to a share of the produce in return for protection (Mīmāṃsāsūtra 6.7.3). The Dharmaśāstras assume private ownership of fields, which could be sold, mortgaged, given and inherited, while recognising the king’s right to taxes and his ownership of mines, treasure-trove and ownerless property. Medieval inscriptions show layered rights: those of the king to revenue, of grantees (often Brahmins or temples) to the king’s share, of village communities over common land, and of cultivators to their fields (Kane 1930; Sharma 1959).

    13.5.2 Ownership in Theory¶

    Medieval jurists also debated the nature of ownership (svatva) itself: was it a relation known only from the śāstras, so that property acquired by means the texts forbade was not truly owned, or a worldly relation known from ordinary usage? The Mitākṣarā argued that ownership is a worldly (laukika) matter, and that a man who acquires property by a forbidden means is guilty of a sin but is still its owner. The distinction between moral fault and legal effect, which the Dāyabhāga expressed in its principle that a fact cannot be altered by a hundred texts (Chapter 11), is one of the more sophisticated achievements of the tradition (Derrett 1968; Rocher 2012).

    13.5.3 Boundaries¶

    Boundary disputes (sīmāvivāda) were among the commonest lawsuits in an agrarian society. Manu recommends that villages mark their boundaries with trees, tanks, wells, shrines and mounds, and with hidden markers — stones, bones, cow’s hair, chaff, ashes, potsherds, dried cow-dung, bricks, charcoal, pebbles and sand — buried along the line so that the boundary can be proved later (8.245–251). Where there is doubt, the neighbours and the elders of the villages are to be examined; if they are lacking, people who know the forest — hunters, fowlers, herdsmen, fishermen, root-diggers, snake-catchers and gleaners — may be called as witnesses (8.258–260). If no evidence exists, the king himself fixes the boundary (8.265).

    13.5.4 Owners and Herdsmen¶

    The title on disputes between the owner of cattle and the herdsman (svāmipāla-vivāda) allocates responsibility for animals. By day the herdsman is responsible for the safety of the herd, by night the owner if the animals are in his house; a herdsman must make good animals lost through his negligence but not those killed by robbers he has reported or by unavoidable accidents. Around every village a strip of common land is to be left for pasture, and cattle that damage unfenced crops on it are not to be fined (Manu 8.229–244). These rules show a careful attention to the practical problems of a mixed farming and pastoral economy.

    13.6 Lost Property, Treasure and Escheat¶

    Several rules concern property without an evident owner. Lost property found by royal officials is to be kept in safe custody by the king for three years; the owner who proves his claim within that time recovers it, though the king takes a share — a sixth, a tenth or a twelfth, according to the circumstances — and after three years the king may take it (Manu 8.30–34). Treasure found buried in the ground belongs in principle to the king, who as the protector of the land is its “lord”, but a learned Brahmin who finds treasure may keep the whole of it, and others must give the king a share; the king who finds ancient treasure should give half to Brahmins (8.35–39). The property of a man who dies without heirs goes to the king, except the property of a Brahmin, which goes to other Brahmins (9.188–189). These rules show the king’s position as the residual owner of property in his realm, qualified, characteristically, by special privileges for Brahmins.

    13.7 Gambling and Betting¶

    The last of the eighteen titles concerns gambling with dice (dyūta) and betting on animal contests (samāhvaya). The texts take two different attitudes. Manu regards gambling as a form of open theft that destroys kingdoms: the king should suppress it, punish gamblers and those who keep gambling houses corporally, and banish them (9.220–228). Yājñavalkya and Nārada, like the Arthaśāstra, take a regulatory view: gambling should be conducted in licensed halls under a supervisor, who provides the dice, takes a percentage of the winnings for the king and enforces the payment of debts; gambling outside such halls is forbidden and cheating with false dice is punished (Yājñ 2.199–203; AŚ 3.20). The contrast illustrates the difference between the moralising and the fiscal approaches to the same social fact, and between Manu and the later, more practical smṛtis.

    13.8 Houses and Neighbours¶

    The Arthaśāstra’s Book 3 includes a chapter on disputes about dwellings and buildings (vāstuka), which shows the attention of its authors to urban life. It regulates the distance to be left between houses, the placing of drains, latrines, wells and fireplaces so as not to harm a neighbour, the obstruction of light and air, the use of common walls and the right of way; and it fixes fines for damage to a neighbour’s property, for blocking a path or a water channel, and for nuisances (AŚ 3.8). The Dharmaśāstras treat many of these matters under the title of boundaries. Such rules are among the most practical in the texts, and they remind us that the law of the śāstras was concerned with the ordinary frictions of village and town life as well as with grand questions of varṇa and kingship.

    13.9 Inheritance and Partition¶

    13.9.1 The Joint Family¶

    The Dharmaśāstras assume that property is held by families, and that sons inherit from their fathers. Manu states that after the death of the father and mother the brothers may divide the paternal property among themselves, or the eldest may take the whole and support the others as a father would (9.104–105). Yājñavalkya allows a father to partition his property among his sons during his lifetime, and gives the mother a share equal to a son’s when the sons partition after the father’s death (Yājñ 2.114–123). The British courts’ later doctrines of the Mitākṣarā coparcenary and of the Dāyabhāga’s absolute paternal ownership grew out of the commentators’ interpretations of these and related verses (Chapter 11).

    13.9.2 Shares and Exclusions¶

    The earlier texts give the eldest son an extra share (uddhāra) and allow unequal partition according to the varṇa of the mothers when a man has wives of different varṇas: the son of a Brahmin wife takes more than the son of a Kṣatriya wife, and so on, while the son of a Śūdra wife takes little or nothing (Manu 9.149–157). Later texts, and the commentators, prefer equal partition among sons. Unmarried sisters are to receive a quarter of a brother’s share for their marriage (Manu 9.118; Yājñ 2.124). Certain property is impartible: Manu lists clothes, vehicles, ornaments, cooked food, water, women (that is, slave women), the means of livelihood and pasture (9.219). Persons suffering from certain disabilities and those who have fallen from caste are excluded from inheritance but are to be maintained (Manu 9.201–203).

    13.9.3 Sons and Adoption¶

    Because the continuation of the family and of the ancestral offerings required a son, the texts recognise twelve kinds of son, including, besides the son born of a man’s lawful wife, the son begotten on his wife by another through niyoga, the adopted son (dattaka), the son made (kṛtrima), the son born secretly, the son of an unmarried girl, the son bought and the son of a remarried woman (Manu 9.158–180). Six of these are heirs; the others are kinsmen but not heirs. In later centuries only the natural and the adopted son remained important; the law of adoption is described in Chapter 15. A daughter could be “appointed” (putrikā) to bear a son for her father if he had no son.

    13.9.4 Order of Heirs¶

    Where a man died without male issue, the texts give an order of heirs. Yājñavalkya’s list — wife, daughters, parents, brothers and their sons, more distant kinsmen, pupil and fellow-student (2.135–136) — became the basis of the Mitākṣarā scheme; the Dāyabhāga ranked heirs by their capacity to benefit the deceased through ancestral offerings (Chapter 11). Women generally took a limited estate in property they inherited from men, with the right to its income but restricted power to alienate it; their own property (strīdhana) followed separate rules (Chapter 15). In the absence of any heir, the property went to the king, except the property of a Brahmin, which went to other learned Brahmins.

    13.10 The Arthaśāstra’s Private Law¶

    Book 3 of the Arthaśāstra is a treatise on the same titles of law, written from the perspective of the royal courts (Chapter 12). It covers marriage, the duties of spouses, inheritance, property in houses and fields, boundaries, debts, deposits, slaves and labourers, partnerships, sales, gifts, theft, assault and gambling. Its rules are often close to those of the smṛtis, sometimes more detailed, and in places notably different: it is more permissive on divorce and remarriage than the Dharmaśāstras (Chapter 15); it imposes fines more consistently than corporal punishments; and it frames its rules with an eye to public order and the king’s revenue. It also invalidates transactions made by dependants — sons dependent on their fathers, slaves, those under another’s authority — and by the intoxicated, the insane and the distressed, except in recognised cases (AŚ 3.1). McClish has argued that the presence of this dharmaśāstric material in the Arthaśāstra is in part the result of its later redaction (Chapter 3) (McClish 2019; Olivelle 2013).

    13.11 Assessment¶

    The civil law of the Dharmaśāstras and the Arthaśāstra is technically accomplished and closely engaged with economic life. Its rules on interest and its limits, on pledges and sureties, on deposits, sales and partnerships, on boundaries and on the joint family reflect the needs of a society of cultivators, traders and creditors, and many of them continued to be applied by Indian courts until well into the twentieth century. Comparisons with Roman law — the societas, depositum, fenus nauticum — or with Islamic law — the muḍāraba partnership, the prohibition of ribā — can illuminate common solutions to common problems. But this was not a law of free and equal contracting parties. Interest rates, debt bondage, the capacity to own property, the shares of sons and the inheritance of women all depended on varṇa and gender; slavery was regulated, not questioned. The technical achievements and the social hierarchy belong to the same system.

    13.13 Summary¶

    • The law of debt regulated interest — graded by the varṇa of the borrower — and limited it: the total interest on money could not exceed the principal, and compound and other oppressive forms of interest were forbidden. Creditors could recover debts by suit or by self-help; debtors of lower varṇa could be made to work off their debts.

    • Sureties, pledges, deposits, sales, partnerships and the conventions of guilds were each regulated in detail; the law of gift defined who might alienate what, and underlay the land grants of kings.

    • Hired labour, apprenticeship and slavery were legal institutions; land was held privately, subject to the king’s right to revenue.

    • Inheritance and partition were organised around the joint family and the male line, reflecting the importance of sons and of the ancestral rites.
