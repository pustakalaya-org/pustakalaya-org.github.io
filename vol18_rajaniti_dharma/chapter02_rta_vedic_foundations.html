    Chapter 2: Ṛta, Satya and Dharma: The Vedic Foundations¶

    The classical Indian traditions of law and statecraft did not invent their vocabulary from nothing. Their key terms — dharma, satya, daṇḍa, rājan, sabhā — already have a history in the Vedic corpus, and the authors of the Dharmasūtras and Dharmaśāstras claimed the Veda as the first source of their own authority. This chapter traces that background: the Ṛgvedic idea of ṛta, the place of truth and of the gods who guard it, the slow rise of the word dharma, and the assemblies and rituals of early kingship. It also marks the limits of what the evidence allows. The Vedic texts are liturgical poetry and ritual prose, not law books; the institutions they mention are known from scattered allusions, and much of what was once written about “Vedic democracy” or “Vedic courts” goes far beyond them.

    On dates: the core of the Ṛgveda is generally placed c. 1500–1200 BCE; the later Vedic texts (the other Saṃhitās, the Brāhmaṇas, Āraṇyakas and early Upaniṣads) c. 1200–500 BCE. The Dharmasūtras, in which dharma first becomes the subject of a dedicated literature, belong to the last three centuries BCE (Chapter 9).

    2.1 Ṛta: Cosmic and Ritual Order¶

    2.1.1 The Word and Its Range¶

    Ṛta is a past participle formed from the root ṛ, “to move, to fit, to join”; its sense is close to “what is fitted, what is in its right place and course”. It has an exact Avestan cognate, aša (older arta), and the pair shows that an idea of right order goes back to the common Indo-Iranian inheritance. In the Ṛgveda ṛta is one of the most important abstract notions. It names the regular course of nature — the dawns that never fail to arrive, the rivers that flow, the year that turns — and, at the same time, the correct course of the sacrifice, which the poets understood as keeping that larger order going. The gods are “born of ṛta” or “grow strong through ṛta”; the sacrificial fire is its charioteer; the dawns follow its “path” (ṛtasya panthāḥ).

    The moral sense grows out of the cosmic and ritual ones. To speak falsely, to break an agreement, or to fail the gods in ritual is to act against ṛta; its opposite is anṛta, “untruth, disorder”. Jan Gonda stressed that ṛta is less a fixed “law” than the condition of right and effective functioning in which each part of the world, including the ritual, performs as it should (Gonda 1963). This is a helpful caution. Ṛta is not a code of rules that a court could apply; it is a vision of a world held in order, within which human truthfulness and correct ritual have their place.

    Ṛgveda 10.190.1

    ṛtaṃ ca satyaṃ cābhīddhāt tapaso ’dhy ajāyata
    tato rātry ajāyata tataḥ samudro arṇavaḥ
    “Order and truth were born from heat kindled to its height; from that was born night, and from that the billowing sea.”

    In this late cosmogonic hymn ṛta and satya stand at the very beginning of the created world, before night and the ocean. The pairing is characteristic. Satya (“what is real, true”) is in many contexts the human, spoken face of ṛta: the true word that matches what is.

    2.1.2 Varuṇa and Mitra¶

    The guardians of ṛta among the gods are above all the Ādityas, and especially Varuṇa and Mitra, often invoked together as the dual deity Mitrāvaruṇā. Varuṇa watches human conduct with his spies (spaśaḥ) and binds wrongdoers in his fetters (pāśa); the poets of the hymns to him (notably Ṛgveda 1.24–25, 2.28 and 7.86–89) ask to be released from guilt (āgas) they may have committed knowingly or unknowingly. In Ṛgveda 7.86, attributed to the poet Vasiṣṭha, the speaker asks Varuṇa what his chief offence has been, that the god should wish to destroy a praiser who is his friend, and begs to be told so that he may come to the god free of guilt. The tone is that of a man before a superior whose anger he does not understand, and it is easy to see why modern readers have spoken of Varuṇa as a “judge”. The image should not be pressed too far: there is no procedure, no plaint and no verdict in these hymns, only guilt, confession and the hope of release.

    Mitra, whose name is also an ordinary noun meaning “contract, alliance” and then “friend” (it is cognate with Avestan Mithra), is the god of agreements between people. Paul Thieme and others showed how closely the Indo-Iranian Mitra is tied to the binding force of the pact. Georges Dumézil made the pair Mitra–Varuṇa the centre of his theory of the “sovereign function” in Indo-European ideology, with Mitra standing for the contractual, benevolent side of sovereignty and Varuṇa for its awesome, binding side (Dumézil 1988). Dumézil’s wider tripartite scheme is much debated, but the observation that early Indian ideas of rule join cosmic order, binding agreement and royal power remains useful. It is a modern scholarly construction, however, not a doctrine stated in the hymns.

    2.1.3 Anṛta and the Weight of Truth¶

    The Vedic poets treat untruth as a serious fault, and later law inherited this attitude in its treatment of testimony. In the Dharmaśāstras perjury is among the gravest offences a witness can commit, and Manu devotes a long passage (8.60–123) to the examination of witnesses, warning that the false witness falls into hell and destroys the merit of his ancestors (Chapter 12). Manu also prescribes how witnesses are to be put on oath according to their varṇa: a Brahmin by his truthfulness, a Kṣatriya by his mount and weapons, a Vaiśya by his cattle, grain and gold, and a Śūdra by all the grievous sins (8.113). The rule shows both continuities — truth as the core of legal process — and the way in which the later texts graded every procedure by social rank.

    It is tempting to read a straight line from Varuṇa’s fetters to the courtroom oath, but the connection is one of shared vocabulary and attitude, not institutional descent. The Vedic texts themselves contain no classification of kinds of falsehood with graded penalties; tables of such “Vedic” offences in popular books have no basis in the sources.

    2.2 The Early History of Dharma¶

    2.2.1 Dharman in the Ṛgveda¶

    The word dharma (Vedic dhárman) derives from the root dhṛ, “to hold, support, uphold”. A verse found in the Mahābhārata gives the classic later etymology: dharma is so called because it “upholds” (dhāraṇa) creatures. In the Ṛgveda, however, dhárman is a relatively uncommon word. Joel Brereton’s study of its occurrences concludes that it means something like “foundation” or “support”, the base on which a thing stands, and in some contexts the “ordinances” that establish and maintain order; it is closely associated with ṛta and with the Ādityas, but it is not yet the master concept it later became (Brereton 2004).

    2.2.2 From the Brāhmaṇas to the Upaniṣads¶

    In the middle and late Vedic texts the word appears more often, and frequently in connection with the king. Patrick Olivelle has shown that in this period dharma is associated especially with royal power and with Varuṇa, the divine king; the king is the “protector of dharma” (Olivelle 2004). A well-known passage in the Bṛhadāraṇyaka Upaniṣad (1.4.14) makes the point in a striking way. After the creation of the social orders, the creator “brought forth a form superior to himself, dharma”; dharma is “the ruling power of the ruling power” (kṣatrasya kṣatram), and “there is nothing higher than dharma”. Through dharma “a weak man can prevail over a strong one, as with the help of a king”. The same passage identifies dharma with truth: what is dharma is truth, and so people say of one who speaks truth that he speaks dharma. This is perhaps the earliest Indian statement of the idea that a principle of right stands above even the power of the ruler — though it is a theological statement, not a constitutional one.

    The Chāndogya Upaniṣad (2.23.1) speaks of three “branches” (skandha) of dharma: sacrifice, study and giving; austerity; and the life of the student living in his teacher’s house. Here dharma is already the sum of a person’s religious duties according to his way of life — the sense it has in the Dharmasūtras.

    2.2.3 Why Dharma Became Central¶

    Olivelle has argued that the sudden prominence of dharma after the Vedic period owes much to its adoption by the new religious movements and by the Mauryan state. The Buddha’s teaching is dhamma; Aśoka (r. c. 268–232 BCE) made dhaṃma the watchword of his inscriptions (Chapter 8). On this view the Brahmanical Dharmasūtras, which appear in the following centuries, are in part a response: they claim the term for the Vedic tradition and define it as the duties of the varṇas and āśramas, grounded in the Veda (Olivelle 2004; Olivelle and Davis 2018). Not every scholar accepts every step of this argument, but it is an important corrective to the assumption that dharma had always been the central concept of Brahmanical thought.

    2.3 Satya and the Social Order¶

    Truth appears again and again in later Vedic teaching as the foundation of right conduct. The Taittirīya Upaniṣad’s instruction to the departing student begins: “Speak the truth; practise dharma” (satyaṃ vada, dharmaṃ cara, 1.11.1). The Muṇḍaka Upaniṣad’s satyam eva jayate (3.1.6), “truth alone conquers”, became the national motto of the Republic of India. Both are moral and religious teachings; their later use in public life is a matter of reception, discussed in Chapter 20.

    The same Vedic sources that exalt truth also lay the foundations of social hierarchy. The Puruṣasūkta (Ṛgveda 10.90.12), a late hymn, describes the Brahmin as the mouth of the cosmic Person, the Rājanya as his arms, the Vaiśya as his thighs and the Śūdra as born from his feet. In the Brāhmaṇas, the Brahmin and the Kṣatriya are repeatedly described as the two powers (brahman and kṣatra) that together rule over the others, and the Vaiśya and Śūdra as their subordinates. The later law books take this ranking as given; it is the starting point of the varṇa provisions discussed honestly in Chapters 10 and 14 (Sharma 1958).

    2.3.1 Truth in Later Tradition¶

    The Vedic exaltation of truth had a long afterlife in Indian moral and political imagination. The legend of King Hariścandra, told in the Purāṇas, presents a king who gives up his kingdom, his wife and his son and becomes the servant of a cremation-ground keeper rather than break his word; it became one of the most popular stories in Indian literature and drama. M. K. Gandhi recorded in his autobiography that a play about Hariścandra, seen in his childhood, made a deep impression on him, and he made truth (satya) the centre of his political method, which he called satyāgraha, “holding fast to truth”. These modern uses, like the choice of the Muṇḍaka’s phrase as the national motto, are part of the reception of the Vedic idea, not evidence of continuity of institutions; but they show how the conviction that truth upholds the world could be given new political meanings.

    2.3.2 The Śūdra and the Woman in Vedic Texts¶

    The later Vedic texts already show the subordination that the law books would systematise. A passage of the Aitareya Brāhmaṇa (7.29), describing the characteristics of the varṇas, says that the Śūdra is “the servant of another, to be expelled at will, to be slain at will”. The Śūdra was excluded from the solemn Vedic sacrifices, and the Brāhmaṇas debate whether he may even be present at them. R. S. Sharma used such passages, together with the evidence of the Dharmasūtras, to trace the progressive degradation of the Śūdras from the late Vedic period onwards (Sharma 1958).

    Women’s position in Vedic ritual was more complex. The wife of the sacrificer was a necessary participant in the solemn rites, which a man could not perform without her, and some hymns of the Ṛgveda are attributed by tradition to women poets. Stephanie Jamison’s study of the sacrificer’s wife shows that her ritual role was real and significant, while also being defined by her relation to her husband (Jamison 1996). The idea of a Vedic “golden age” of women’s freedom, popularised by nationalist historians, has been criticised by feminist historians as an idealisation that served to present later subordination as a decline from an Aryan norm (Chakravarti 2003).

    2.3.3 The Doctrine of the Debts¶

    Another Vedic idea with long legal consequences is that of the debts (ṛṇa) with which a man is born. The Taittirīya Saṃhitā (6.3.10.5) says that a Brahmin is born with three debts: to the seers, paid by Vedic study as a student; to the gods, paid by sacrifice; and to the ancestors, paid by begetting offspring. The Śatapatha Brāhmaṇa (1.7.2.1–5) adds a debt to men, paid by hospitality. The doctrine expressed the householder’s obligations, and the Dharmaśāstras later used it to argue against renouncing the world before marrying and having a son (Olivelle 1993). The need for a son to pay the debt to the ancestors through the offerings of the ancestral rite (śrāddha) also underlies the law of adoption and inheritance (Chapters 13 and 15). The doctrine tells us about Brahmanical religious obligation; it is not, as is sometimes claimed, a theory of political duty.

    2.4 Kingship and Assemblies in the Vedic Texts¶

    2.4.1 The Rājan¶

    The Ṛgvedic rājan was the leader of a clan or tribe (jana, viś), above all a war leader and protector of the people’s cattle. His position was hereditary in some cases and perhaps elective in others; the evidence is too thin for certainty. By the later Vedic period, as settlements spread in the Gaṅgā–Yamunā region, kingship became more elaborate. The Brāhmaṇas describe great royal consecrations such as the rājasūya and the horse sacrifice (aśvamedha), in which the king’s power is ritually constituted and displayed. Romila Thapar has described this period as a transition “from lineage to state”: chiefdoms based on kinship gave way, gradually and unevenly, to kingdoms with more permanent authority, revenue and administration (Thapar 1984).

    The ritual texts already associate kingship with protection and with the support of the Brahmin. In the rājasūya, the king is presented to the people as their ruler and the priest declares that Soma is the king of the Brahmins; the “jewels” (ratnin) of the court — the general, the chief queen, the charioteer, the collector and others — receive offerings in their houses. These rites show a court with differentiated officers, but they are rituals; they do not describe a governmental system in detail (Heesterman 1985; Gonda 1966).

    2.4.2 Sabhā, Samiti and Vidatha¶

    Three kinds of gathering appear in the Vedic texts: the sabhā, the samiti and the vidatha. Their nature has been argued over for a century. The sabhā seems to have been both a building and a gathering of notables, a place of deliberation, of gaming with dice and of social prestige; a man “worthy of the assembly” (sabheya) is a respected figure. The samiti appears as a larger assembly of the people or the clan, which a king might attend. The vidatha is the most obscure; it may have been a gathering connected with ritual and the distribution of goods (Sharma 1959).

    Atharvaveda (Śaunaka) 7.12.1

    sabhā ca mā samitiś cāvatāṃ prajāpater duhitarau saṃvidāne
    “May the assembly (sabhā) and the gathering (samiti), the two daughters of Prajāpati, in accord, help me.”

    The Ṛgveda closes with a hymn (10.191) of concord addressed to an assembly: “Come together, speak together, let your minds be of one accord” (saṃ gacchadhvaṃ saṃ vadadhvaṃ saṃ vo manāṃsi jānatām, 10.191.2). These passages show that collective deliberation was valued. Nationalist historians of the early twentieth century, above all K. P. Jayaswal, read them as evidence for a “Hindu” constitutional tradition of popular assemblies, a parliament and a judiciary (Jayaswal 1924). That reading answered a colonial claim that India had known only despotism, and it was understandable in its time; but it goes far beyond the evidence. The texts say nothing about how membership was determined, how decisions were taken or what powers the assemblies had over a king, and there is no evidence that they exercised anything like judicial review. R. S. Sharma’s study remains the most careful survey (Sharma 1959). The sabhā of the later law books is something different again: the court of the king or of his judge, with assessors (sabhya) chosen for learning in the Veda (Chapter 12).

    2.4.3 Hymns for the King¶

    Some Vedic hymns were composed for use in royal rites. Ṛgveda 10.173, a hymn for the installation or confirmation of a king, prays that he may stand firm and unshaken “like a mountain”, that all the clans (viśaḥ) may want him, and that the kingdom may not slip from him; it calls on Varuṇa, Bṛhaspati, Indra and Agni to hold his kingship firm. The Atharvaveda contains several hymns for kings: for their consecration, for victory, for the return of an exiled king, and for success in the assembly. Nationalist historians read the phrases in which the people “choose” or “want” the king as evidence of election. The hymns certainly express the ideal that a king should have the support of his people, but they are prayers and spells, not records of elections, and they do not tell us how kings were actually chosen (Sharma 1959).

    2.4.4 Kings and Gods in the Brāhmaṇas¶

    The Brāhmaṇas also contain myths about the origin of kingship. In one well-known passage of the Aitareya Brāhmaṇa (1.14), the gods are defeated by the demons and conclude that this is because they have no king; “let us make a king”, they say, and they make Soma their king, after which they are victorious. The story presents kingship as a remedy for weakness in conflict, an idea that the later mātsyanyāya theory would develop in a different direction (Chapter 5). Other passages explain the king’s power as conferred by the gods in the rituals of consecration, or describe the king as the visible form of Indra, the warrior king of the gods.

    The royal rituals themselves are described at length. In the rājasūya, the king was anointed with waters from many sources; he made offerings in the houses of the “jewels” (ratnin) of his court; he played a ritual game of dice; and he took symbolic steps in the four directions to show his rule over the earth. In the aśvamedha, the horse sacrifice, a consecrated horse was allowed to wander for a year, guarded by armed men, and any ruler through whose land it passed without challenge was taken to acknowledge the sacrificer’s supremacy. The vājapeya included a chariot race that the sacrificer won. These rites, which later kings sometimes revived — several Gupta and other kings boasted of performing the horse sacrifice — show how closely kingship was bound to Brahmanical ritual. They also show the dependence of the king on the priest, a theme that J. C. Heesterman explored in his studies of the “inner conflict” between royal power and Brahmanical authority (Heesterman 1985).

    2.4.5 The Gambler and the Assembly¶

    The Vedic sabhā was not only a place of deliberation. The famous “gambler’s hymn” of the Ṛgveda (10.34), the lament of a man ruined by dice, describes him going to the sabhā to gamble, his body burning with hope, and losing everything, while his wife is abandoned and his family disowns him. The hymn reminds us that the assembly was a place of sociability and play as well as of counsel. The dice game reappears in the royal rituals and, most famously, in the Mahābhārata, where Yudhiṣṭhira gambles away his kingdom, his brothers, himself and Draupadī in the assembly hall of the Kauravas — an episode that raises, in dramatic form, questions about the legality of a wager and the rights of a wife that the later law books also discussed (Chapter 15).

    2.4.6 Kings in the Upaniṣads¶

    The early Upaniṣads show kings in another light: as patrons and participants in philosophical debate. Janaka of Videha presides over the great debate in the Bṛhadāraṇyaka Upaniṣad in which Yājñavalkya defeats the Brahmins of the Kuru-Pañcāla country and answers the questions of Gārgī Vācaknavī. In several dialogues a Kṣatriya king teaches a Brahmin — Ajātaśatru of Kāśī instructs the proud Brahmin Gārgya (BU 2.1), and Pravāhaṇa Jaivali teaches the doctrine of the “five fires” and rebirth, remarking that this knowledge had previously been the possession of Kṣatriyas (BU 6.2; CU 5.3). In the Chāndogya Upaniṣad (5.11), King Aśvapati Kaikeya receives a group of learned householders with the declaration that in his realm there is no thief, no miser, no drunkard, no man who does not keep the sacred fire, no ignorant man and no adulterer, let alone an adulteress. The boast is the earliest Indian statement of the ideal kingdom as a moral community in which the king’s virtue is reflected in the conduct of his subjects — an ideal that the epics and the śāstras would develop at length.

    2.4.7 From Vedic Kingship to the Śāstras¶

    Between the Vedic rituals and the śāstric theory of kingship lies a long and poorly documented history. The rise of territorial kingdoms and cities in the Gangetic plain from about the sixth century BCE, the formation of the Magadhan state, the Mauryan empire and the kingdoms that followed it created new forms of government that the Vedic texts do not describe. The śāstras inherited from the Vedic world the vocabulary of kingship, the association between king and Brahmin, and the ideal of the king as protector; they added a theory of the state, of law and of administration that belongs to the later period (Thapar 1984; Sharma 1959). Kumkum Roy’s study of the emergence of monarchy in north India between the eighth and fourth centuries BCE reads the later Vedic ritual texts and the early law books for this transition, showing how the Brahmanical sources represented, and helped to legitimate, the growth of royal power over the clan (Roy 1994).

    2.5 Ritual Forms of Proof¶

    Vedic literature contains the first Indian references to ordeals and solemn oaths, which later became formal modes of proof (divya). The Chāndogya Upaniṣad (6.16) describes a man accused of theft who is made to grasp a heated axe: if he has spoken the truth he is not burnt, and is released; if he lied, he is burnt and executed. The passage is used as an illustration of the power of truth, but it shows that the fire ordeal was a known procedure. Fire (Agni) was also invoked as a witness to agreements and above all to marriage, in which the couple circle the fire. In the Dharmaśāstras, oaths and ordeals are carefully regulated as evidence of last resort when human proof fails (Chapter 12). Nothing in the Vedic texts amounts to a law of evidence, but the conviction that a truthful person is protected by the gods underlies these later procedures.

    2.6 Gift, Fee and the Ritual Economy¶

    The Vedic sacrifice was also an economic institution. Its performance required the payment of a fee (dakṣiṇā) to the officiating priests, and the larger śrauta rites prescribed fees of hundreds or thousands of cattle, together with gold, horses and cloth. Several hymns of the Ṛgveda, known as dānastuti (“praises of the gift”), celebrate the generosity of named patrons who rewarded the poets with cattle, horses, chariots and, in some cases, slave women; these passages are among the earliest Indian evidence for the relationship between poets, priests and chiefs (Jamison and Brereton 2014). The patron who sponsored the sacrifice (yajamāna) gained prestige and divine favour; the priests gained wealth; and the exchange bound together the two groups whose alliance would later be expressed in the doctrine that the Brahmin and the Kṣatriya together uphold the world.

    The ideology of the gift (dāna) that grew out of this relationship became central to later Dharmaśāstra and to the history of Indian kingship. The texts teach that gifts to worthy Brahmins bring merit to the donor, specify the proper recipients, times and objects of gifts, and condemn those who withhold the fee. Medieval kings and nobles made grants of land and villages to Brahmins and temples, recorded on copper plates that end with verses cursing anyone who confiscates the grant; thousands of these grants survive and are a principal source for the history of land rights (Chapter 5; on the law of gift, Section 13.2.4). J. C. Heesterman and others have analysed the Dharmaśāstra theory of gift as part of a wider system of exchange in which the Brahmin’s acceptance of gifts was both a privilege and, in some texts, a source of ritual danger (Heesterman 1985).

    Gift and fee also defined the boundaries of the community. Only those entitled to sacrifice — the initiated men of the three higher classes — could be patrons, and only Brahmins could officiate and receive the fee. The Śūdra was excluded from both roles. The ritual economy thus encoded the hierarchy that the Puruṣasūkta expressed in myth.

    2.7 Comparative Perspective¶

    Many ancient societies grounded human order in a cosmic one. The Egyptian maat, the Greek dikē and themis, and the Avestan aša (the cognate of ṛta) all join the order of nature, the duties of kings and the truthfulness of persons. These parallels suggest that the Vedic case is one instance of a widespread pattern of archaic thought, not a unique achievement. What is distinctive in India is the later history: the development, from the Dharmasūtras onwards, of a specialised scholarly literature on dharma, written and transmitted by Brahmin experts, which claimed Vedic authority while dealing increasingly with matters — property, debt, procedure, punishment — that the Veda itself did not address (Chapter 9). The idea that this literature simply “unfolded” the Vedic vision of ṛta is a theological claim of the tradition itself; historians see instead a long and contested process in which Brahmin authors adapted older ideas to new social and political conditions.

    2.9 Summary¶

    • The Ṛgvedic concept of ṛta joined the order of nature, the correctness of ritual and the truthfulness of persons; its guardians were Varuṇa and Mitra, gods associated with binding obligation and agreement.

    • Dharma, a minor word in the Ṛgveda meaning “foundation” or “ordinance”, became associated with royal power in the later Vedic texts, and rose to prominence after the Vedic period, partly through its use by Buddhists and by Aśoka.

    • Vedic texts already contain the ideas of truth as the basis of social order, of the three debts, and of a ranked order of varṇas, which the later śāstras developed.

    • The Vedic sabhā and samiti were assemblies of uncertain composition and powers; claims that they were democratic or judicial institutions in a modern sense are not supported by the evidence.

    • Royal rituals and Brāhmaṇa myths present kingship as created to secure victory and order and as dependent on Brahmanical ritual; the Upaniṣads show kings as patrons and participants in philosophical debate.
