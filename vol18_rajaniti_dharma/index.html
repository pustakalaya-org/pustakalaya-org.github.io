    Preface¶

    This is Volume 18 of the series Bhāratīya Jñāna Paramparā: Rājanīti and Dharmaśāstra: Polity and Law. It surveys Indian traditions of political thought and law from the Vedic period to the Constitution of the Republic of India: the Arthaśāstra and the later literature on polity; the theory of kingship in the śāstras and the epics; Buddhist and Jain ideas of the righteous ruler and the law of the monastic order; the Dharmaśāstra literature, from the Dharmasūtras and the Manusmṛti to the medieval commentaries and digests; courts, procedure, contract, property, inheritance, crime and the family in classical law; custom, caste and village institutions; the charters, scribes and statecraft of the medieval kingdoms; Islamic law and the Indo-Persian state of the Sultanate and the Mughals; the making of “Anglo-Hindu” and “Anglo-Muhammadan” law under British rule; and the Constitution’s encounter with this inheritance.

    After an introduction, the chapters fall into four groups: the foundations of Indian political thought, the Arthaśāstra’s science of the state and the Buddhist and Jain alternatives to it (Chapters 2–8); the Dharmaśāstra literature (Chapters 9–11); the classical law of procedure, property, crime, the family and custom (Chapters 12–16); and the history of law and government from the medieval regional kingdoms and the Delhi Sultanate to the Republic (Chapters 17–20). Primary texts are quoted only where both the wording and the reference could be confirmed against a standard edition; elsewhere they are paraphrased, with the reference given. Dates follow current scholarship, with traditional dates labelled as such; in particular, the Arthaśāstra is treated as a composite text of c. 50 BCE–300 CE and the Manusmṛti as a work of about the second century CE, following Patrick Olivelle. The volume does not claim that the classical texts “anticipated” modern constitutions, welfare states or human rights; comparisons with other traditions are presented as comparisons.

    The texts discussed here are, among other things, charters of social hierarchy, and the volume does not hide this. It sets out their provisions on varṇa, untouchability and women with references to the primary sources, and it gives space to the critics — from the Buddha to B. R. Ambedkar and modern feminist and Dalit scholars — who exposed and opposed them. It also tries to give due weight to the real intellectual achievements of the traditions: the Arthaśāstra’s analysis of the state, the Dharmaśāstras’ law of procedure and property, and the many forms of local self-government recorded in inscriptions.

    References to standard editions, translations and studies — among others by P. V. Kane, Robert Lingat, J. D. M. Derrett, Ludo Rocher, Richard Lariviere, Patrick Olivelle, Donald Davis, R. P. Kangle, Thomas Trautmann, Mark McClish, R. S. Sharma, Romila Thapar, Upinder Singh, Uma Chakravarti, Muzaffar Alam and Marc Galanter — are given in the text and collected in the select bibliography, which is meant as a guide to further reading. A list of abbreviations follows the table of contents; a glossary, a chronology, brief notes on persons and a guide to the principal sources are provided at the end. No survey of this size can be exhaustive or free of error, and every quotation should, before scholarly use, be checked against a critical edition.

    Nikhil Kumar Rajput
    Ramanujan College, University of Delhi
    Guru Pūrṇimā, 2026
