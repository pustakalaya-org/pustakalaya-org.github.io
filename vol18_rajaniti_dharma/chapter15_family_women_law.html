    Chapter 15: Family, Saṃskāra and Women in Dharmaśāstra¶

    The Dharmaśāstras devote more space to the life of the household than to any other subject. Their rules on the rites of passage (saṃskāra), the stages of life (āśrama), marriage, the duties of husband and wife, adoption, maintenance and women’s property were the parts of the tradition that touched most people most directly, and they were the parts that British courts and, later, the Indian Parliament took over as “Hindu personal law”. They are also the parts in which the texts’ hierarchies of varṇa and gender are most consequential for ordinary lives.

    This chapter surveys the main institutions of family law in the śāstras: the saṃskāras and their exclusions, the āśramas, marriage and its forms, the position of wives and widows, women’s property and inheritance, and the controversial practices of child marriage and widow-burning. It then turns to women’s own voices and to the reform of family law in the nineteenth and twentieth centuries. It draws on the critical scholarship of Uma Chakravarti, who described the system as “Brahmanical patriarchy” — a structure in which the control of women’s sexuality was the means of maintaining the purity of caste — and on studies of particular texts and practices (Chakravarti 1993, 2003; Leslie 1989).

    15.1 The Stages of Life¶

    The Dharmaśāstras organise a man’s life in four stages (āśrama): the Vedic student (brahmacārin), the householder (gṛhastha), the forest-dweller (vānaprastha) and the renouncer (saṃnyāsin). Patrick Olivelle has shown that this scheme had a history. In the Dharmasūtras the āśramas were four alternative ways of life, among which an adult who had completed his studies could choose; only in the Manusmṛti and later texts did they become successive stages through which a twice-born man should pass in order (Olivelle 1993). Throughout, the texts give pre-eminence to the householder, on whom all the other orders depend “as all creatures depend on the air” (Manu 3.77). The scheme was addressed to twice-born men; women’s stages of life were defined by their relation to men — daughter, wife, widow — and Śūdras were excluded from Vedic studentship.

    15.2 The Saṃskāras¶

    15.2.1 Purpose and Number¶

    Saṃskāra means “making perfect” or “preparing”: the rites of passage were believed to remove impurities and to qualify a person for the next stage of ritual and social life. The domestic ritual manuals (Gṛhyasūtras) and the Dharmaśāstras describe them, and their number varies. Gautama lists forty saṃskāras, including many Vedic sacrifices (GDh 8.14–24); later authors settled on a list of sixteen, the form in which they are usually presented today (Pandey 1969; Kane 1930). Table 15.1 gives this list.

    Table 15.1. A common list of sixteen saṃskāras (after Pandey 1969)
    Saṃskāra Occasion
    garbhādhāna Rite for conception
    puṃsavana Rite in early pregnancy, intended to secure a male child
    sīmantonnayana Parting of the pregnant woman’s hair
    jātakarman Birth rites
    nāmakaraṇa Naming, usually on the tenth or twelfth day
    niṣkramaṇa First outing of the child
    annaprāśana First feeding with solid food
    cūḍākaraṇa First cutting of the hair
    karṇavedha Piercing of the ears
    vidyārambha Beginning of learning the alphabet
    upanayana Initiation into Vedic study (“investiture with the sacred thread”)
    vedārambha Beginning of Vedic study
    keśānta First shaving of the beard
    samāvartana Return home at the end of studentship
    vivāha Marriage
    antyeṣṭi Funeral rites

    The table conceals important differences in who could receive these rites. The rites of pregnancy and childhood were performed for the children of the twice-born; for girls, the texts prescribe them without Vedic mantras (Manu 2.66). The puṃsavana, despite modern euphemistic descriptions, was a rite intended to secure the birth of a son.

    15.2.2 Upanayana and Its Exclusions¶

    The most important saṃskāra before marriage was upanayana, the initiation of a boy into Vedic study, which made him “twice-born” (dvija). Manu prescribes it in the eighth year from conception for a Brahmin, the eleventh for a Kṣatriya and the twelfth for a Vaiśya, with outer limits of sixteen, twenty-two and twenty-four years respectively (Manu 2.36–38). The materials of the boy’s girdle differ by varṇa, and his sacred cord is to be of cotton for a Brahmin, hemp for a Kṣatriya and wool for a Vaiśya (2.42–44). Those who are not initiated in time become “fallen from the Sāvitrī” (vrātya) and are excluded from Vedic learning until they perform a penance (2.39–40).

    Upanayana was denied to Śūdras and, in the Dharmaśāstras, to women. Manu states that for women the marriage ceremony takes the place of initiation, serving the husband takes the place of residence with a teacher, and household work takes the place of the care of the sacred fires (2.67). A few later texts recall a former age in which girls too were initiated and studied the Veda, and the Upaniṣads mention women such as Gārgī and Maitreyī who debated with male teachers; but by the time of the Dharmaśāstras Vedic learning had become a male, twice-born preserve. The exclusion of Śūdras and women from Vedic learning, and therefore from the most prestigious forms of knowledge and ritual, is one of the foundations of the hierarchy the texts describe (Chakravarti 2003).

    15.2.3 The Student’s Life¶

    After initiation, the student (brahmacārin) lived in his teacher’s house, learning the Veda by heart. Manu describes his discipline: he should beg for food each day and offer it to his teacher, tend the sacred fire, bathe, avoid honey, meat, perfumes, garlands, women, gambling and idle talk, sleep on the ground, and obey and honour his teacher as a father (2.175–201). The period of study was ideally twelve years for each Veda, though shorter periods were common; it ended with a ritual bath (samāvartana) after which the young man, now a snātaka (“one who has bathed”), could marry. The initiation itself taught the boy the Sāvitrī verse (the “Gāyatrī”) and invested him with the girdle, staff and sacred cord. In practice, Vedic study of this kind was confined to Brahmin families of scholarly lineage; for most twice-born men, initiation became a short ritual followed by an ordinary education.

    15.2.4 Impurity¶

    Birth and death produced a temporary impurity (aśauca) in the family, during which relatives were excluded from rites and from contact with others. The texts grade its duration by closeness of kinship and by varṇa: Manu prescribes ten days for a Brahmin, twelve for a Kṣatriya, fifteen for a Vaiśya and a month for a Śūdra after the death of a close relative (5.83). Women were also regarded as impure during menstruation and after childbirth. These rules, which modern readers may find marginal to law, were central to the texts’ vision of social order: they reinforced the boundaries of kinship and caste, and they made purity, graded by varṇa, a matter of continual observance.

    15.2.5 Funeral and Ancestral Rites¶

    The last saṃskāra, the funeral (antyeṣṭi), was followed by a period of impurity for the relatives, graded by closeness of kinship and by varṇa, and by a series of ancestral offerings (śrāddha) in which rice-balls (piṇḍa) were offered to the deceased, his father and grandfather. About a year after death the deceased was united with his ancestors in the rite of sapiṇḍīkaraṇa. These rites had legal consequences: the relationship of sapiṇḍa, “sharing the rice-ball”, defined the circle of kin within which marriage was forbidden and, in the Dāyabhāga, the order of inheritance (Chapter 11). They also made the birth of a son, who could offer the piṇḍa, a religious necessity.

    15.3 Marriage¶

    15.3.1 A Sacrament and a Gift¶

    For the Dharmaśāstras, marriage (vivāha) was a saṃskāra and the foundation of the householder’s life. In its approved forms it was conceived as the gift of a girl (kanyādāna) by her father to the groom. The ceremony, described in the Gṛhyasūtras, included the taking of the bride’s hand (pāṇigrahaṇa), the circumambulation of the sacred fire, and the seven steps (saptapadī) taken together by bride and groom. Manu states that the marriage mantras are the definitive mark of a wife, and that the marriage is complete at the seventh step (8.227). The Hindu Marriage Act of 1955 still provides that where the saptapadī is part of the ceremony, the marriage becomes complete and binding when the seventh step is taken. The ideal of the relationship is expressed in a verse of Manu: “Let mutual fidelity continue until death; this, in brief, is the highest dharma of husband and wife” (9.101).

    15.3.2 The Marriage Rite¶

    The domestic ritual manuals describe the wedding in detail, and many of its elements survive in contemporary Hindu weddings. After the bride’s father has given her to the groom, the groom takes her right hand (pāṇigrahaṇa) with verses from the Ṛgvedic wedding hymn (10.85); the bride steps on a stone, to be firm like it; the couple offer parched grain into the fire; they circle the fire, keeping it to their right; and they take seven steps together, each step accompanied by a wish — for food, strength, wealth, happiness, offspring, the seasons and lifelong friendship. In the evening the groom shows the bride the pole star (dhruva), a symbol of constancy. The rites vary between the ritual schools and between regions; in much of southern India, for example, the tying of a pendant (tāli) around the bride’s neck became the essential act.

    15.3.3 The Eight Forms¶

    The texts list eight forms of marriage (Manu 3.21–34):

    • Brāhma: the father gives his daughter, adorned, to a learned man of good conduct whom he has invited;

    • Daiva: the gift of a daughter to the officiating priest at a sacrifice;

    • Ārṣa: the gift of a daughter after receiving a cow and a bull from the groom “in accordance with dharma”;

    • Prājāpatya: the gift of a daughter with the injunction “May you both practise dharma together”;

    • Āsura: the groom gives wealth to the bride’s kin and to the bride and takes her — marriage by bride-price;

    • Gāndharva: the union of a girl and a man by mutual desire;

    • Rākṣasa: the forcible abduction of a girl from her home, after killing or wounding her kin;

    • Paiśāca: the seduction or rape of a girl who is asleep, intoxicated or out of her senses — “the most evil” form.

    The first four were approved, especially for Brahmins; the Rākṣasa form was allowed to Kṣatriyas; the Āsura and Paiśāca were condemned, though the texts differ in detail (3.23–26). The list is often cited as evidence that the tradition recognised love marriage (the Gāndharva form). That is true, and the Gāndharva marriage of Duṣyanta and Śakuntalā is celebrated in literature; but the texts rank it low, and their inclusion of abduction and rape among the “forms of marriage” shows that the purpose of the classification was to regularise the status of the woman and her children after the event, not to recognise women’s choice. Consent of the bride plays no part in the approved forms, which are all gifts by her father.

    15.3.4 Rules of Eligibility¶

    A man should marry a girl of his own varṇa who is not a sapiṇḍa of his mother nor of his own gotra (patrilineal clan) on his father’s side (Manu 3.4–5). Twice-born men may also take wives from lower varṇas in descending order (anuloma), but a Brahmin who takes a Śūdra wife is condemned (3.12–19); a woman’s marriage to a man of lower varṇa (pratiloma) is the gravest violation of the order of varṇas and produces the most degraded offspring (Chapter 10). Polygyny was permitted and polyandry was not (Section 15.13). Northern authorities condemned the marriage of cross-cousins, which was customary and accepted in the south (Chapter 9).

    15.3.5 The Age of Marriage¶

    The Dharmaśāstras increasingly recommended that girls be married before puberty. Gautama advises giving a daughter in marriage before she reaches puberty; Manu suggests that a man of thirty should marry a girl of twelve, and a man of twenty-four a girl of eight (9.94), and later authorities treated it as a sin for a father to allow his daughter to menstruate unmarried. These norms, combined with the prohibition of widow remarriage, produced the problem of child widowhood that became a central concern of nineteenth-century reformers.

    15.3.6 Divorce¶

    The Dharmaśāstras, which regarded marriage as an indissoluble sacrament, did not recognise divorce in the approved forms of marriage. A husband could, however, take another wife (“supersede” his first wife) on grounds discussed below (Section 15.13). Nārada permits a woman to take another husband in five calamities (Chapter 11), and the Arthaśāstra allows the dissolution of marriages contracted in the unapproved forms by mutual consent, and permits a wife to leave a husband who has been long absent (AŚ 3.3–4). Many castes, especially among cultivating, artisan and lower-status communities, recognised divorce and remarriage by custom; these customs were often recognised by the colonial courts (Chapter 16).

    15.4 Wives and Widows¶

    15.4.1 The Duties of Wives¶

    The “dharma of women” (strīdharma) in the śāstras is above all the duty of a wife to her husband. Manu’s statement of it is unambiguous: a woman should do nothing independently, even in her own house; she should always be cheerful, skilful in household work and frugal; she should serve her husband as a god even if he is of bad character (5.147–156; 9.2–3). The Strīdharmapaddhati of Tryambakayajvan, an eighteenth-century treatise from the Thanjavur court studied by Julia Leslie, shows how these norms were elaborated into a detailed daily discipline for wives (Leslie 1989).

    The texts also impose obligations on husbands: to maintain their wives, to honour them, not to abandon a wife without cause, and to give a superseded wife compensation. A wife who was abandoned could seek maintenance. These obligations belong, however, to a framework of guardianship rather than equality.

    15.4.2 Widowhood¶

    The Dharmaśāstras of Manu and his successors forbid the remarriage of widows (Manu 5.157–162, 9.64–68; Chapter 10). A widow was to live a life of austerity, often with shaved head, plain clothes and restricted diet, and to devote herself to her husband’s memory. Among higher castes the result was a large population of widows, including child widows, dependent on their husbands’ families and subject to severe social restrictions. Among many other communities, widow remarriage — often with the deceased husband’s younger brother — was customary.

    The exception provided by Nārada and Parāśara, which allow a woman to remarry if her husband is missing, dead, has become an ascetic, is impotent or has fallen from caste (Nārada 12.97; Parāśara 4.30), became the textual basis for the reformers’ campaign. Īśvaracandra Vidyāsāgar (1820–1891) argued from Parāśara, which claims authority for the Kali age, that widow remarriage was permitted by the śāstras; his campaign led to the Hindu Widows’ Remarriage Act of 1856. The Act removed the legal bar on remarriage, but it also provided that a remarrying widow forfeited her rights in her deceased husband’s property — a provision that, among communities that had previously allowed remarriage, could worsen a widow’s position.

    15.4.3 Satī¶

    The burning of a widow on her husband’s funeral pyre (satī, “the virtuous woman”, a name applied to the woman and, in English, to the practice) is the most notorious practice associated with Hindu law. The early Dharmaśāstras do not prescribe it; Manu prescribes chaste widowhood. Later texts praise it: the Viṣṇusmṛti offers the widow the choice of chastity or “ascending the pyre after him” (25.14), and several Purāṇas and later smṛtis promise great rewards to the widow who dies with her husband. Some commentators rejected it: Medhātithi regarded it as a form of suicide forbidden by the Veda, and the dramatist Bāṇa condemned it in the seventh century. Historical evidence for satī comes from inscriptions and memorial stones from about the sixth century CE, from the accounts of foreign observers, and from the records of the East India Company; it was practised especially among some ruling and warrior groups and, in the late eighteenth and early nineteenth centuries, among several castes in Bengal (Mani 1998).

    Rammohun Roy campaigned against satī from 1818, arguing from the śāstras that it was not obligatory; the Bengal Sati Regulation of 1829, issued under the governor-general William Bentinck, declared it illegal. Lata Mani’s study of the debate showed that both the reformers and their orthodox opponents argued mainly about what the scriptures prescribed, and that the women who died were largely absent from the debate as subjects (Mani 1998). The practice did not disappear entirely: the death of Roop Kanwar at Deorala, Rajasthan, in 1987 led to the Commission of Sati (Prevention) Act of 1987, which prohibits the glorification of satī as well as its commission.

    15.5 Women in the Arthaśāstra¶

    The Arthaśāstra’s treatment of marriage and women differs in several respects from the Dharmaśāstras, and the differences are instructive. Its chapters on marriage, the duties of spouses and women’s property (AŚ 3.2–4) accept the eight forms of marriage and the authority of fathers and husbands; but they also regulate the relationship in practical terms that give women more room than Manu allows.

    The text defines a wife’s property as consisting of maintenance (vṛtti) and ornaments, fixes a maximum amount of maintenance that may be settled on her, and allows her to use it for herself, her son and her daughter-in-law if her husband is absent and has made no provision. A widow who wishes to live a religious life may keep it; if she remarries, she may keep what her father-in-law and husband gave her only if she marries with her father-in-law’s consent, and loses it otherwise. The text thus assumes that widows may remarry, while protecting the interests of the husband’s family.

    The Arthaśāstra also allows the dissolution of marriage. Marriages in the four unapproved forms may be dissolved by mutual consent of husband and wife, though marriages in the approved forms may not (AŚ 3.3). It lays down periods for which the wife of an absent husband should wait — longer if he is a Brahmin who has gone for study, shorter for a short journey, and differing according to whether she has children and whether he has left provision for her — after which she may take another husband, preferably a kinsman of her husband, with the permission of the judges (AŚ 3.4). It regulates a husband’s right to punish his wife and imposes fines on wives who are disobedient or who leave the house without permission, and on husbands who are cruel or who neglect their wives.

    These rules are not egalitarian: they assume the authority of husbands and the control of women’s sexuality, and they treat women’s property as limited and contingent. But they show that the Brahmanical opposition to widow remarriage and divorce was not universal even among learned authors, and they are closer to the practices of many communities recorded in later centuries than are the rules of Manu (Olivelle 2013).

    15.6 Women’s Property and Inheritance¶

    15.6.1 Strīdhana¶

    The Dharmaśāstras recognise a category of women’s own property (strīdhana). Manu lists six kinds: what was given before the nuptial fire, at the bridal procession and as a token of affection, and what she received from her brother, her mother and her father (9.194). Yājñavalkya adds what was given by her husband, gifts on supersession, the bride-price and later gifts from her husband’s family, and ends with the word “and the like” (Yājñ 2.143–144). On this last word the Mitākṣarā built an expansive interpretation: strīdhana, Vijñāneśvara argued, includes all property a woman acquires by inheritance, purchase, partition, seizure or finding. The British courts did not follow him: the Privy Council held that property a woman inherited from a male was not her strīdhana but a “limited estate”, which passed on her death to the heirs of the last male owner.

    A woman’s control of her strīdhana had limits. Yājñavalkya allows a husband to take his wife’s strīdhana in a famine, for a religious duty, in illness or when he is imprisoned, without being obliged to return it (Yājñ 2.147). Kātyāyana distinguishes gifts from her own relatives, over which she has full power of disposal, from gifts made by her husband, over some of which her power was restricted. On her death strīdhana passed to her daughters and children; if she died childless, to her husband or her parents according to the form of her marriage (Manu 9.195–197).

    15.6.2 Inheritance¶

    The classical law gave women limited rights of inheritance. Daughters inherited from their fathers only in the absence of sons, or through the device of the appointed daughter (putrikā); unmarried sisters were to receive a share for their marriage. Widows inherited the separate property of sonless husbands under Yājñavalkya’s list of heirs, but under the Mitākṣarā not their husbands’ interest in joint family property, and in either case usually as a limited estate (Chapters 11 and 13). Mothers took a share equal to a son’s when sons partitioned after their father’s death, and widows and unmarried daughters had a right of maintenance charged on the family property.

    In the colonial period, the Hindu Women’s Right to Property Act of 1937 gave widows a right to their husbands’ share in joint family property, but only as a limited estate. The Hindu Succession Act of 1956 made women absolute owners of property they possessed (section 14), made daughters, widows and mothers heirs of the first class to a man’s separate property, but preserved the male coparcenary; the Amendment of 2005 made daughters coparceners by birth (Chapter 20).

    15.6.3 Women as Witnesses and Litigants¶

    Manu excludes women as witnesses except for other women, on the ground that “women’s understanding is unsteady” (8.68, 8.77); some later texts admit women as witnesses in cases of violence and in matters concerning women (Chapter 12). Women could be parties to lawsuits, particularly about their strīdhana and maintenance, though they were generally represented by male relatives. Inscriptions show many women — queens, wives of merchants and officials, temple women — making gifts of land and money and endowing temples, which implies that they controlled substantial property in practice (Davis 2010).

    15.7 Women in the Buddhist and Jain Orders¶

    Buddhism and Jainism offered women an alternative to the household: the life of a nun. Buddhist tradition holds that the Buddha agreed, after initial reluctance and at the request of his foster-mother Mahāpajāpatī Gotamī and the intercession of Ānanda, to admit women to the order, subject to the eight “weighty rules” that subordinated nuns to monks (Chapter 8). The Therīgāthā, the verses of the early nuns, include poems in which women celebrate their release from domestic drudgery, from husbands and from grief. The order of fully ordained nuns died out in the Theravāda countries in the medieval period, though it survived in East Asia.

    The Jain community has had nuns throughout its history, and nuns have often outnumbered monks. The two main Jain traditions differed over women’s spiritual capacity: the Digambaras held that women cannot attain liberation in a female body, because they cannot practise the complete nudity required of the highest ascetics, while the Śvetāmbaras held that they can, and revered the nineteenth Tīrthaṅkara, Mallī, as a woman (Jaini 1979; Dundas 2002). Monastic life thus gave some women independence from the household and access to learning, within institutions that also maintained hierarchies of gender.

    15.8 Devadāsīs¶

    The devadāsīs (“servants of the god”) were women dedicated to temple service, especially in southern India, where they danced and sang in temple rituals and at royal courts. Inscriptions from the Cōḷa period onwards record their appointment, their endowments and their gifts; they were often well-to-do, could own and dispose of property, and were not bound to a husband. They also stood outside the norms of marriage, and in many places their status was linked to sexual relations with patrons. In the late nineteenth and twentieth centuries, reformers — among them Muthulakshmi Reddy, the first woman legislator in the Madras presidency — campaigned against temple dedication as a form of prostitution, and laws such as the Bombay Devadasis Protection Act (1934) and the Madras Devadasis (Prevention of Dedication) Act (1947) prohibited it. Historians have since debated the consequences of these laws: they ended a practice that exposed girls to exploitation, but they also stripped a community of women of its livelihood, property and artistic role, while the dance associated with them was reinvented as “Bharatanatyam” by upper-caste performers (Soneji 2012).

    15.9 Adoption and Maintenance¶

    The need for a son to continue the lineage and perform the ancestral rites made adoption an important institution. Of the twelve kinds of son listed in the early texts (Chapter 13), only the adopted son (dattaka), “given” by his natural parents, remained widely recognised in later centuries. The law of adoption was elaborated in two late treatises, the Dattakamīmāṃsā of Nanda Paṇḍita (17th century) and the Dattakacandrikā, which the British courts treated as authoritative. They laid down, among other rules, that the adopted boy should ideally be of the same caste and close kin, that he should be one who could have been born to the adopter (so that, for instance, a sister’s son was excluded in some schools), and that a woman could adopt only with her husband’s authority. The question whether a widow could adopt a son to her deceased husband, and with whose permission, produced much litigation in the colonial courts. The Hindu Adoptions and Maintenance Act of 1956 replaced these rules, permitting the adoption of girls as well as boys and allowing women to adopt in their own right.

    The texts recognise duties of maintenance. A man must maintain his aged parents, his wife and his minor children; a verse attributed to Manu in the commentaries (it is quoted in the Mitākṣarā on Yājñ 2.175, but is not in the Manusmṛti itself) declares that aged parents, a virtuous wife and an infant son must be supported even at the cost of doing a hundred wrongs. Widows and unmarried daughters, and members excluded from inheritance by disability, were entitled to maintenance from the family property. These obligations were enforced by the colonial courts as rights of maintenance and were codified in the Hindu Adoptions and Maintenance Act; the general law also provides, since the nineteenth century, a summary remedy for maintenance of wives, children and parents, which became the subject of the Shah Bano case (Chapter 20).

    15.10 Women’s Voices¶

    The Dharmaśāstras were written by men. Women’s own perspectives survive in other sources. The Therīgāthā, verses attributed to early Buddhist nuns, include poems of release from domestic servitude (Chapter 8). Women poet-saints of the bhakti movements — Āṇṭāḷ, Akkā Mahādevī, Lal Ded, Mīrābāī and Bahiṇābāī, among others — defied the expectations of wifely obedience in the name of devotion (Volume 8 of this series).

    In the nineteenth century, women began to write directly about law and custom. Savitribai Phule (1831–1897) opened, with her husband Jotirao Phule, one of the first schools for girls in Pune in 1848, and wrote poetry against caste and the subordination of women. Tarabai Shinde’s Marathi tract Strī-Puruṣ Tulanā (“A Comparison of Women and Men”, 1882) attacked the double standards by which women were blamed for vices tolerated in men. Pandita Ramabai (1858–1922), a Sanskrit scholar from a Brahmin family, criticised the treatment of high-caste widows in The High-Caste Hindu Woman (1887), founded a home for widows, and became a Christian. Rukhmabai, married as a child, refused in the 1880s to join the husband to whom she had been married, and the litigation that followed — in which she was ordered to join him or go to prison — became a cause célèbre; she later qualified as a physician. Her case and the death of the child bride Phulmoni contributed to the Age of Consent Act of 1891, which raised the age of consent for girls from ten to twelve. The Child Marriage Restraint Act of 1929 (the Sarda Act) set minimum ages of marriage (Kumar 1993; Chakravarti 2003).

    15.11 Women as Patrons, Authors and Reformers¶

    The exclusion of women from Vedic study did not mean that women had no part in public, religious and literary life. Inscriptions show women of royal and merchant families acting as donors and patrons. The Nāṇeghāṭ inscription of the Sātavāhana queen Nāyanikā records the great Vedic sacrifices performed by her husband and the fees paid for them, and the Nāsik inscription of Gautamī Balaśrī, mother of King Gautamīputra Sātakarṇi, records her gift of a cave to the Buddhist community and celebrates her son’s achievements. At the Buddhist sites of Sāñcī and Bhārhut, many of the donative inscriptions on railings and gateways name women, lay and monastic, as donors. Such records show women controlling wealth and making religious gifts in their own names, in ways the normative texts do not always lead one to expect (Singh 2008).

    Women also wrote. Sanskrit anthologies preserve verses attributed to women poets, and the bhakti poets mentioned above composed in the regional languages. The nineteenth-century writers discussed in the previous section — Savitribai Phule, Tarabai Shinde and Pandita Ramabai, who had been given the title paṇḍitā in Calcutta for her Sanskrit learning — drew on, criticised and sometimes turned against itself the textual tradition discussed in this chapter (Chakravarti 2003).

    15.12 From Strīdhana to Dowry¶

    The gifts that the Dharmaśāstras classed as a woman’s own property — what she received at her wedding from her parents and kin — were the historical basis of the institution later called dowry. In the classical texts, such gifts belonged to the bride; the bride-price (śulka) paid by the groom’s family was condemned in the Āsura form of marriage. Over the centuries, and especially among higher castes in northern India, gifts at marriage increasingly took the form of payments demanded by the groom’s family and controlled by it, and the practice spread to many communities in the nineteenth and twentieth centuries. Dowry became a major cause of the indebtedness of brides’ families and of violence against married women.

    Independent India responded with legislation: the Dowry Prohibition Act of 1961 forbade the giving and taking of dowry; later amendments, and the addition to the Penal Code in 1983 and 1986 of offences of cruelty by a husband or his relatives and of “dowry death”, sought to protect married women. The persistence of the practice, despite the law, shows how deeply marriage payments are embedded in social expectations. It also shows that the history of women’s property in India runs from the strīdhana of the śāstras through the changing customs of communities to the problems of the present (Agnes 1999; Kumar 1993).

    15.13 Polygyny, Supersession and Monogamy¶

    The Dharmaśāstras permit a man to have more than one wife, while requiring a woman to have only one husband. Manu allows a man of a higher class to take wives of lower classes in addition to a wife of his own class, and ranks the wives by class, the wife of equal class having precedence in religious rites (Manu 3.12–13; 9.85–87). The texts also allow a husband to take another wife — to “supersede” (adhivedana) his first wife — in specified circumstances. Manu permits a barren wife to be superseded in the eighth year, one whose children have died in the tenth, one who bears only daughters in the eleventh, and one who speaks unpleasantly immediately (Manu 9.81). Yājñavalkya lists grounds such as drinking, chronic illness, barrenness, hostility and extravagance, and requires that the superseded wife be maintained, failing which the husband incurs a heavy sin (Yājñ 1.73–74); she was also owed a compensatory gift (ādhivedanika), which became part of her strīdhana. The Arthaśāstra likewise requires a husband who takes another wife after waiting the prescribed period to give the first a compensatory payment in addition to her property (AŚ 3.2). These provisions protected the superseded wife’s maintenance, but they confirm that the purpose of marriage in the texts was above all the birth of sons, and that the burden of failing to produce them fell on the woman.

    In practice, polygyny was mainly a practice of kings, nobles and the wealthy; inscriptions record kings with several chief queens, and ordinary households were, for economic reasons, largely monogamous. Polyandry, though forbidden by the texts, is not unknown to the tradition: in the Mahābhārata, Draupadī is the common wife of the five Pāṇḍava brothers, and the epic itself treats this as so irregular that it offers several stories to justify it. Polyandrous marriage has also been documented by ethnographers among some communities of the Himalayas and the Nilgiri hills.

    Modern law moved towards monogamy. Several provinces prohibited bigamy among Hindus in the 1940s, and the Hindu Marriage Act of 1955 made a second marriage during the lifetime of a spouse void and punishable for all Hindus, Buddhists, Jains and Sikhs. Muslim personal law continues to permit a man to have up to four wives, subject to the Qurʾānic condition of equal treatment, a difference that has figured prominently in debates over a uniform civil code (Chapter 20). The Special Marriage Act of 1954, available to all, is monogamous.

    15.14 Reform and Codification¶

    The colonial state’s interventions in family law, from the prohibition of satī (1829) to the Sarda Act (1929), were piecemeal and often contested by orthodox opinion, and each was justified by its Indian supporters partly by appeal to the śāstras themselves (Chapters 11 and 19). The comprehensive codification proposed in Ambedkar’s Hindu Code Bill, and enacted in four statutes in 1955–56 after the Bill’s defeat, is the subject of Chapter 20 (Som 1994; Newbigin 2013; Agnes 1999).

    15.15 Assessment¶

    The family law of the Dharmaśāstras was a sophisticated and internally coherent system for the household of a twice-born man. It provided for the maintenance of dependants, gave women a protected category of property, regulated marriage and inheritance in detail, and embedded the whole in a ritual life that gave meaning to birth, marriage and death. It also excluded Śūdras and women from Vedic learning, made women perpetual dependants of men, condemned widows to austerity, recommended child marriage, and tied women’s sexuality to the purity of caste. Modern apologetics that emphasise the honour paid to women, the Gāndharva marriage or strīdhana, without these facts, misrepresent the system; so do polemics that ignore the variety of custom and the agency women exercised in practice. The modern history of Hindu family law has been, in large part, the history of dismantling the system’s inequalities while retaining some of its forms.

    15.16 Summary¶

    • The āśramas and saṃskāras organised the life of the twice-born man; Śūdras and women were excluded from Vedic initiation, and marriage took its place for women.

    • Marriage in the approved forms was the gift of a girl by her father, within varṇa and outside the gotra; the texts recommended child marriage, permitted polygyny, forbade widow remarriage except in Nārada and Parāśara, and did not recognise divorce in the approved forms.

    • Women had a protected category of property (strīdhana) but limited rights of inheritance, generally as a limited estate.

    • Women’s own voices and the legislation of 1829–1956 mark the long process by which this system was challenged and reformed.
