    Persons¶

    Brief notes on authors, rulers, jurists, reformers and scholars mentioned in this volume. Dates are approximate where marked; for legendary or traditional figures, the notes describe the tradition. Chapter references are to the chapters in which each person is chiefly discussed.

    Person Note
    Authors and figures of the classical tradition
    Manu Mythical first man and law-giver, to whom the Mānava Dharmaśāstra is attributed; the text itself is a work of c. 2nd c. CE (ch. 10).
    Kauṭilya (Cāṇakya, Viṣṇugupta) Traditionally the minister of Candragupta Maurya and author of the Arthaśāstra; the text as we have it is composite, c. 50 BCE–300 CE (ch. 3).
    Yājñavalkya Upaniṣadic teacher, to whom a smṛti of c. 4th–5th c. CE is attributed (ch. 11).
    Nārada Divine sage to whom the first purely legal smṛti (c. 5th–6th c.) is attributed (ch. 11).
    Bṛhaspati, Kātyāyana Names attached to lost law books of c. 6th–7th c., known from quotations (ch. 11, 12).
    Kāmandaka Author of the Nītisāra, a verse digest of polity, c. 500–700 CE (ch. 3).
    Medhātithi Author of the oldest complete surviving commentary on Manu, 9th–10th c., probably in Kashmir (ch. 9, 10).
    Viśvarūpa Author of the Bālakrīḍā, the earliest surviving commentary on Yājñavalkya, c. 9th c. (ch. 11).
    Vijñāneśvara Jurist at the court of Vikramāditya VI at Kalyāṇa; author of the Mitākṣarā, c. 1100–1120 (ch. 11).
    Jīmūtavāhana Bengali jurist, author of the Dāyabhāga, 12th c. (ch. 11).
    Devaṇṇabhaṭṭa Author of the Smṛticandrikā, a digest respected in the south, c. 1200 (ch. 11).
    Mādhava Scholar and minister at Vijayanagara, author of the commentary on Parāśara known as the Parāśaramādhavīya, 14th c. (ch. 11).
    Vācaspati Miśra Jurist of Mithilā, author of the Vivādacintāmaṇi and other digests, 15th c.; not to be confused with the earlier philosopher of the same name (ch. 11).
    Mitra Miśra Author of the Vīramitrodaya, composed for the Bundela king Vīrasiṃha of Orchha, 17th c. (ch. 11).
    Nīlakaṇṭha Bhaṭṭa Author of the Bhagavantabhāskara, of which the Vyavahāramayūkha is a part, 17th c. (ch. 11).
    Lakṣmīdhara Minister of Govindacandra of Kanauj; compiler of the Kṛtyakalpataru, 12th c. (ch. 9).
    Caṇḍeśvara Minister in Mithilā; author of digests on law and on royal policy, 14th c. (ch. 3, 9).
    Kullūka Bhaṭṭa Author of the Manvarthamuktāvalī, the most widely used commentary on Manu, perhaps 13th–15th c. (ch. 10).
    Somadeva Sūri Digambara Jain monk, author of the Nītivākyāmṛta and Yaśastilaka, 10th c. (ch. 8).
    Jinasena Digambara Jain author of the Ādipurāṇa, 9th c. (ch. 8).
    Hemacandra Jain scholar at the court of Kumārapāla of Gujarat, 12th c. (ch. 8).
    Kalhaṇa Kashmiri poet, author of the Rājataraṅgiṇī (1148–49) (ch. 5, 17).
    Kṣemendra Kashmiri poet and satirist, 11th c.; mocked the scribes of Kashmir; a formulary, the Lokaprakāśa, is attributed to him (ch. 17).
    Muhaṇot Naiṇsī (1610–1670) Minister of Jodhpur; compiler of a chronicle of the Rajputs and a survey of the districts of Marwar (ch. 17).
    Megasthenes Envoy of Seleucus to the court of Candragupta Maurya, c. 300 BCE; his Indika survives only in quotations by later Greek and Latin writers (ch. 3, 6, 7).
    Kālidāsa Sanskrit poet and dramatist, c. 4th–5th c. CE; author of the Raghuvaṃśa and Abhijñānaśākuntala (ch. 7).
    Bāṇa Court poet of Harṣa, 7th c.; author of the Harṣacarita and Kādambarī, in which a character condemns Kauṭilya’s teaching (ch. 3).
    Basavaṇṇa Twelfth-century minister and poet-saint in the Kannada country, leader of the Vīraśaiva movement, whose vacanas rejected caste privilege (ch. 1).
    Guru Nānak (1469–1539) First Guru of the Sikhs, who rejected the religious authority of caste (ch. 1).
    The Buddha Founder of Buddhism; c. 480–400 BCE on the short chronology now widely accepted (traditional Theravāda dates 624–544 BCE) (ch. 8).
    Mahāvīra Twenty-fourth Jina of the Jains, an older contemporary of the Buddha (traditional Śvetāmbara dates 599–527 BCE) (ch. 8).
    Nāgārjuna Buddhist philosopher, c. 2nd c. CE, to whom the Ratnāvalī, a letter of counsel to a king, is attributed (ch. 8).
    Bhavabhūti Sanskrit dramatist, early 8th c.; his Uttararāmacarita makes Rāma recoil from killing Śambūka (ch. 5).
    Raghunandana Bengali jurist, 16th c.; author of the Smṛtitattva (ch. 9, 11).
    Kamalākara Bhaṭṭa Scholar of the Bhaṭṭa family of Banaras; author of the Nirṇayasindhu (1612) (ch. 9, 18).
    Gāgā Bhaṭṭa Banaras scholar who consecrated Śivājī as king in 1674 (ch. 18).
    Tryambakayajvan Author of the Strīdharmapaddhati at the Thanjavur court, 18th c. (ch. 10, 15).
    Jagannātha Tarkapañcānana Bengali paṇḍit who compiled the Vivādabhaṅgārṇava at the instance of William Jones, translated by Colebrooke (ch. 19).
    Rulers
    Candragupta Maurya Founder of the Mauryan empire, r. c. 321–297 BCE (ch. 3).
    Aśoka Mauryan emperor, r. c. 268–232 BCE; author of the Rock and Pillar Edicts (ch. 8).
    Khāravela King of Kaliṅga, c. 1st c. BCE; patron of Jain ascetics (ch. 5, 8).
    Samudragupta Gupta emperor, 4th c. CE; Allahabad pillar inscription (ch. 5, 7).
    Harṣa King of Kanauj, r. c. 606–647 (ch. 5, 7).
    Kumārapāla Caulukya king of Gujarat, r. 1143–1172; patron of Hemacandra (ch. 8).
    Akbar Mughal emperor, r. 1556–1605 (ch. 18).
    Aurangzeb Mughal emperor, r. 1658–1707; commissioned the Fatāwā-i ʿĀlamgīrī (ch. 18).
    Śivājī Founder of the Maratha state, crowned 1674 (ch. 18).
    Ranjit Singh Sikh ruler at Lahore, r. 1801–1839 (ch. 18).
    Prabhāvatīguptā Daughter of Candragupta II, Vākāṭaka queen and regent for her sons, c. 400 CE (ch. 5, 7).
    Diddā Queen of Kashmir, regent and then ruler in her own right until her death in 1003 (ch. 5, 17).
    Rājarāja I Cōḷa king, r. 985–1014; builder of the Rājarājeśvara temple at Thanjavur (ch. 5).
    Mārttāṇḍa Varma King of Travancore, r. 1729–1758; dedicated his kingdom to the deity Padmanābha in 1750 (ch. 5).
    Raḍiyya (Raziya) Sultan of Delhi, r. 1236–1240 (ch. 5).
    Rudramā Devī Kākatīya ruler of the eastern Deccan, later 13th c. (ch. 5).
    Kṛṣṇadevarāya Vijayanagara emperor, r. 1509–1529; author of the Telugu Āmuktamālyada (ch. 3, 5, 17).
    Ahilyabai Holkar Ruler of the Maratha state of Indore, r. 1767–1795 (ch. 5).
    Someśvara III Cālukya king of Kalyāṇa, r. 1126–1138; the Mānasollāsa (1131) is attributed to him (ch. 3, 17).
    Bukka I Vijayanagara king, r. c. 1356–1377; settled a dispute between Jains and Śrīvaiṣṇavas in 1368 (ch. 17).
    Muḥammad b. Tughluq Sultan of Delhi, r. 1325–1351 (ch. 18).
    Fīrūz Shāh Tughluq Sultan of Delhi, r. 1351–1388; author of the Futūḥāt-i Fīrūzshāhī (ch. 18).
    Jahāngīr Mughal emperor, r. 1605–1627; his memoirs record his ordinances of justice (ch. 18).
    Nūr Jahān (d. 1645) Wife of Jahāngīr from 1611; issued orders under her own seal and had coins struck in her name (ch. 18).
    Shahu of Kolhapur Ruler of Kolhapur, r. 1894–1922; reserved posts for the backward classes in 1902 (ch. 19).
    Islamic and Persian writers
    Ziyāʾ al-Dīn Baranī Historian and political writer of the Delhi Sultanate (c. 1285–1357); Fatāwā-yi Jahāndārī (ch. 18).
    Abu’l Faẓl Minister and historian of Akbar; Akbarnāma and Āʾīn-i Akbarī (ch. 18).
    Shaykh Niẓām Burhānpūrī Head of the board that compiled the Fatāwā-i ʿĀlamgīrī (ch. 18).
    Naṣīr al-Dīn Ṭūsī (1201–1274) Persian philosopher and scientist; author of the Akhlāq-i Nāṣirī (ch. 18).
    Todar Mal (d. 1589) Revenue minister of Akbar; architect of the zabt system (ch. 18).
    Ibn Baṭṭūṭa (1304–c. 1368) Moroccan traveller and Mālikī jurist; qāḍī at Delhi under Muḥammad b. Tughluq (ch. 18).
    Shāh Walī Allāh (1703–1762) Scholar of Delhi; author of the Ḥujjat Allāh al-bāligha (ch. 18).
    Ashraf ʿAlī Thānawī (1863–1943) Deobandi scholar whose treatise on a wife’s right to divorce informed the Act of 1939 (ch. 19).
    Syed Ameer Ali (1849–1928) Jurist and judge of the Calcutta High Court; writer on Muslim law (ch. 19).
    Colonial administrators and scholars
    Warren Hastings (1732–1818) Governor and Governor-General of Bengal; Judicial Plan of 1772 (ch. 19).
    Nathaniel Brassey Halhed (1751–1830) Translator of A Code of Gentoo Laws (1776) (ch. 19).
    William Jones (1746–1794) Judge and Orientalist; translator of Manu (1794) (ch. 10, 19).
    Henry Thomas Colebrooke (1765–1837) Translator of the Digest (1797–98) and Two Treatises (1810) (ch. 11, 19).
    Thomas Babington Macaulay (1800–1859) Law Member; chief author of the draft Indian Penal Code (ch. 14, 19).
    R. Shamasastry (1868–1944) Librarian at Mysore; discoverer, editor and translator of the Arthaśāstra (ch. 3).
    Mountstuart Elphinstone (1779–1859) Commissioner of the Deccan after 1818 and governor of Bombay (ch. 16).
    Georg Bühler (1837–1898) Indologist; translator of Manu and other smṛtis in the Sacred Books of the East (ch. 1, 10).
    Julius Jolly (1849–1932) Indologist; translator of Nārada and Bṛhaspati; historian of Hindu law (ch. 1, 11).
    Reformers and critics
    Rammohun Roy (1772–1833) Bengali reformer; campaigned against satī (ch. 15).
    Īśvaracandra Vidyāsāgar (1820–1891) Sanskrit scholar and reformer; campaign for widow remarriage (ch. 15).
    Jotirao Phule (1827–1890) Anti-caste reformer; Gulamgiri (1873) (ch. 10, 15).
    Savitribai Phule (1831–1897) Teacher and poet; co-founder of schools for girls in Pune (ch. 15).
    Tarabai Shinde (c. 1850–1910) Author of Strī-Puruṣ Tulanā (1882) (ch. 15).
    Pandita Ramabai (1858–1922) Scholar and reformer; The High-Caste Hindu Woman (1887) (ch. 15).
    Rukhmabai (1864–1955) Litigant in a celebrated restitution-of-conjugal-rights case; later a physician (ch. 15).
    Bal Gangadhar Tilak (1856–1920) Nationalist leader, opponent of the Age of Consent Bill of 1891 (ch. 11).
    Hansa Mehta (1897–1995) Member of the Constituent Assembly and of the UN Commission on Human Rights (ch. 20).
    Dakshayani Velayudhan (1912–1978) The only Dalit woman in the Constituent Assembly (ch. 20).
    M. K. Gandhi (1869–1948) Leader of the national movement; Hind Swaraj (1909) (ch. 16, 20).
    E. V. Ramasamy (Periyar) (1879–1973) Leader of the Self-Respect Movement (ch. 10, 20).
    Muthulakshmi Reddy (1886–1968) Physician and legislator; campaigned against temple dedication (ch. 15).
    Kashinath Trimbak Telang (1850–1893) Lawyer, scholar and judge of the Bombay High Court (ch. 19).
    Mahadev Govind Ranade (1842–1901) Judge of the Bombay High Court and social reformer (ch. 19).
    Hari Singh Gour (1870–1949) Lawyer; author of The Hindu Code (1919) (ch. 19).
    B. N. Rau (1887–1953) Jurist; chairman of the Hindu Law Committee and constitutional adviser to the Constituent Assembly (ch. 20).
    Jawaharlal Nehru (1889–1964) First Prime Minister of India (ch. 20).
    B. R. Ambedkar (1891–1956) Jurist, scholar and Dalit leader; chairman of the Drafting Committee of the Constitution; burned the Manusmṛti at Mahad (1927) (ch. 8, 10, 16, 20).
    Modern scholars
    K. P. Jayaswal (1881–1937) Historian; Hindu Polity (1924) (ch. 1, 2).
    P. V. Kane (1880–1972) Sanskritist and lawyer; History of Dharmaśāstra (1930–62) (ch. 9).
    R. P. Kangle Editor and translator of the Arthaśāstra (1960–65) (ch. 3).
    Robert Lingat French scholar of Indian law; The Classical Law of India (ch. 9).
    J. D. M. Derrett Scholar of Hindu law and its colonial administration (ch. 9, 19).
    R. S. Sharma Historian of ancient Indian political ideas and of the Śūdras (ch. 2, 4, 10).
    Patrick Olivelle Editor and translator of the Dharmasūtras, Manu, Yājñavalkya and the Arthaśāstra (ch. 3, 9, 10).
    Uma Chakravarti Historian of early Buddhism and of caste and gender (ch. 8, 10, 15).
    Ludo Rocher (1926–2016) Scholar of Dharmaśāstra and of its colonial reception (ch. 9, 19).
    Richard W. Lariviere (b. 1950) Editor and translator of the Nāradasmṛti (ch. 11, 12).
    Thomas R. Trautmann (b. 1940) Historian of the Arthaśāstra, of Dravidian kinship and of colonial knowledge of India (ch. 3, 6).
    Marc Galanter (b. 1931) Scholar of modern Indian law, legal pluralism and compensatory discrimination (ch. 16, 20).
    Donald R. Davis, Jr. Historian of Dharmaśāstra as a legal tradition, including medieval Kerala (ch. 9, 12).
    U. N. Ghoshal (1886–1969) Historian of Indian political ideas (ch. 1, 5).
    Romila Thapar (b. 1931) Historian of early India; Aśoka and the Decline of the Mauryas (1961) (ch. 3, 8).
    Bernard S. Cohn (1928–2003) Anthropologist and historian of colonial knowledge and law (ch. 16, 19).
    Muzaffar Alam (b. 1947) Historian of Islamic political thought and the Mughal state (ch. 18).
    Steven Collins (1951–2018) Scholar of Pali Buddhism; translator of the Aggañña Sutta (ch. 8).
    Upinder Singh (b. 1959) Historian of early India; Political Violence in Ancient India (2017) (ch. 3, 5, 14).
    Mark McClish Historian of the Arthaśāstra and its relation to Dharmaśāstra (ch. 3, 9).
