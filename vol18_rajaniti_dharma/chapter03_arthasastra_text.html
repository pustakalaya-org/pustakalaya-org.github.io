    Chapter 3: The Arthaśāstra: Text, Date and Structure¶

    The Arthaśāstra ascribed to Kauṭilya is the only complete surviving treatise of the ancient Indian “science of politics”. It is a long and technical work in Sanskrit prose interspersed with verse, addressed to a king and his ministers, which sets out how a kingdom is to be organised, administered, enriched, defended and enlarged. It deals with the training of the ruler, the appointment and testing of ministers, the departments of state and their superintendents, revenue, law courts and the suppression of crime, foreign policy, war and covert operations. No other early Indian text describes the machinery of a state in such detail, and since its rediscovery in the first years of the twentieth century it has transformed the study of ancient Indian history and political thought.

    The text has also been surrounded by a great deal of confident misinformation: that it was written by Cāṇakya, minister of Candragupta Maurya, in the late fourth century BCE; that it describes the actual administration of the Mauryan empire; and that it is a forerunner of modern economics, welfare policy or strategic theory. This chapter sets out what can be said about the text with reasonable confidence, before the following chapters examine its theory of the state (Chapter 4), its political economy (Chapter 6) and its theory of inter-state relations (Chapter 7). Its law is treated in Chapters 12–14.

    3.1 What Is “Arthaśāstra”?¶

    The word arthaśāstra names both a discipline and the particular treatise that survives. The text itself offers two definitions. Its opening sentence presents it as a compendium: “This single treatise on artha has been composed mostly by bringing together the treatises on artha composed by former teachers for the acquisition and protection of the earth” (AŚ 1.1.1). The last book defines the discipline: “The livelihood (vṛtti) of men is artha; the earth inhabited by men is artha. The science that is the means of acquiring and protecting that earth is the science of artha” (AŚ 15.1.1–2). Artha here is not merely “wealth”; it is material well-being and, for a king, the territory and population from which wealth and power derive (Kangle 1960; Olivelle 2013).

    Closely related terms are daṇḍanīti, “the policy of the rod”, the science of governing through coercive authority, and nītiśāstra, the broader genre of works on policy, including later verse manuals such as Kāmandaka’s Nītisāra. Rājanīti, “royal policy”, the word used in the title of this volume, is a general term for the same field. Within Brahmanical classifications of knowledge, artha is one of the three (later four) aims of human life (puruṣārtha), alongside dharma (right conduct), kāma (pleasure) and, later, mokṣa (liberation). The Arthaśāstra itself declares that, of the first three, “artha alone is principal, says Kauṭilya, for dharma and kāma are rooted in artha” (artha eva pradhāna iti kauṭilyaḥ, arthamūlau hi dharmakāmāv iti, AŚ 1.7.6–7) — while also advising the king to pursue the three in balance.

    3.1.1 The Four Sciences¶

    In its first substantive section (AŚ 1.2–4) the text discusses which bodies of knowledge (vidyā) a ruler needs. It reports the views of earlier schools: the Mānavas recognise three sciences (the Vedas, economics and government, treating philosophy as part of the Vedas); the Bārhaspatyas only two (economics and government, the Vedas being merely a cover for worldly men); the Auśanasas only one, government (daṇḍanīti), on which all undertakings depend. Kauṭilya holds that there are four: ānvīkṣikī (critical inquiry, identified as Sāṃkhya, Yoga and Lokāyata), trayī (the three Vedas), vārttā (economics: agriculture, cattle-herding and trade) and daṇḍanīti (government). Of ānvīkṣikī he says, in a verse that has been much quoted, that it “has always been regarded as the lamp of all sciences, the means of all actions and the support of all dharmas” (AŚ 1.2.12). The inclusion of Lokāyata — the materialist school — among the philosophies worth a prince’s study is striking, though it should not be overstated: the purpose of ānvīkṣikī here is to train the king’s judgement, not to free politics from religion. The Vedas define the duties of the varṇas and āśramas, and the king is to see that these are kept (AŚ 1.3).

    3.2 Author and Date¶

    3.2.1 The Traditional Account¶

    Indian tradition from at least the middle of the first millennium CE identifies the author of the Arthaśāstra as Kauṭilya (or Kauṭalya — the manuscripts vary), also called Cāṇakya and Viṣṇugupta, the Brahmin who helped Candragupta Maurya (r. c. 321–297 BCE) overthrow the Nanda dynasty of Magadha and became his minister. The story of Cāṇakya’s vow to destroy the Nandas and his cunning in establishing the Maurya is told in later literature, notably Viśākhadatta’s drama Mudrārākṣasa (perhaps fourth to eighth century CE), the Buddhist Pali commentarial tradition and Jain narratives. The text itself ends with a verse attributing it to the one “who in anger swiftly rescued the science, the weapon and the earth that had passed to the Nanda king” (yena śāstraṃ ca śastraṃ ca nandarājagatā ca bhūḥ / amarṣeṇoddhṛtāny āśu tena śāstram idaṃ kṛtam, AŚ 15.1.73), and a closing verse names Viṣṇugupta as the author of both the aphorisms and their commentary.

    This traditional identification is the reason why the text is so often dated to c. 300 BCE and described as a manual of Mauryan government. It is a tradition worth knowing, and it shaped how Indians read the text for centuries. But the historical evidence does not support it as a statement about the text we have.

    3.2.2 The Evidence of the Text¶

    Several lines of evidence point to a later date and to a composite work.

    • Vocabulary and institutions. The text refers to coins, administrative titles and trade goods (for instance Chinese silk, cīnapaṭṭa, and coral from the Mediterranean) that fit better in the centuries around the turn of the era than in the fourth century BCE. It never mentions the Mauryas, Pāṭaliputra or Candragupta in the body of the work.

    • Its picture of the state. The Arthaśāstra describes a kingdom of modest size surrounded by rival kingdoms of comparable power (the maṇḍala of Chapter 7), not a subcontinental empire. Its “conqueror” (vijigīṣu) is the king of a middling state.

    • Its relation to Megasthenes. The Greek envoy Megasthenes, who visited Candragupta’s court c. 300 BCE, described Pāṭaliputra and Mauryan officials in his Indika, which survives only in fragments quoted by later Greek and Latin writers. His account and the Arthaśāstra differ in many respects; there are some parallels, but they do not show that the two describe the same state, and Megasthenes does not “corroborate” Kauṭilya (Thapar 1961).

    • Its internal layering. Thomas Trautmann’s statistical study of the frequency of common words (1971) showed that different books of the text differ systematically in style, and argued that it is the work of several authors, later combined. His conclusion was that the text cannot be the work of a single hand (Trautmann 1971). This undermines the single-author view, and with it the identification of the whole work with a Mauryan minister.

    3.2.3 Current Scholarship¶

    The most detailed recent account is that of Patrick Olivelle, building on Trautmann and on the work of his student Mark McClish. Olivelle distinguishes three stages (Olivelle 2013; McClish 2019):

    1. Sources. Earlier treatises on government, now lost, on which the author drew. Some may go back to the Mauryan period or earlier, and the text’s frequent citations of “the teachers” (ācāryāḥ) and of named authorities preserve traces of them.

    2. The Kauṭilya recension, c. 50–125 CE: the composition of a single treatise from these sources by an author called Kauṭilya.

    3. The Śāstric redaction, c. 175–300 CE: a thorough revision that divided the text into books and chapters, added the table of contents and the verses that close many chapters, and, McClish argues, brought the text into closer conformity with dharmaśāstric ideas about the king’s duty to uphold varṇa and dharma.

    The text as we have it is therefore best dated c. 50 BCE–300 CE and treated as composite. The series uses this date consistently. The traditional attribution to Cāṇakya, minister of Candragupta, is recorded as tradition; it may preserve a memory that the Mauryan period saw important writing on statecraft, but it cannot be used to date the surviving text or to treat it as a description of Mauryan government (Olivelle 2013; Trautmann 2012).

    Scholarly Note: Kangle’s View

    R. P. Kangle, whose critical edition (1960–65) remains fundamental, defended a Mauryan date and the unity of authorship in the third volume of his work. His arguments — that the text’s political world is pre-imperial and that later references treat Kauṭilya as Candragupta’s minister — are still cited. Most specialists today follow Trautmann and Olivelle, but Kangle’s edition and translation remain indispensable (Kangle 1960).

    3.3 Rediscovery and Editions¶

    Although the Arthaśāstra was cited and summarised by later authors, it fell out of circulation in most of India, and by the nineteenth century European scholars knew it only from quotations. In 1905 R. Shamasastry (1868–1944), librarian of the Government Oriental Library at Mysore, identified the text in a palm-leaf manuscript that had been given to the library by a paṇḍit from the Tanjore region. He published an English translation in instalments, in the Indian Antiquary and elsewhere, from 1905, the first edition of the Sanskrit text in 1909 and a complete English translation in 1915 (Shamasastry 1909, 1915). The discovery provoked an intense debate about the text’s date and authenticity, in which scholars such as J. Jolly, A. B. Keith and M. Winternitz argued for a later date and others for a Mauryan one.

    Further manuscripts were found, and J. Jolly and R. Schmidt published an edition with a commentary in Lahore (1923–24). J. J. Meyer’s German translation appeared in 1926. The critical edition of R. P. Kangle (text 1960, translation 1963, study 1965), based on all the manuscripts and on a fragmentary old commentary, became the standard (Kangle 1960). L. N. Rangarajan’s readable English version (1992) rearranges the text by topic (Rangarajan 1992). Patrick Olivelle’s annotated translation (2013) is now the standard scholarly rendering, and a volume of selections by McClish and Olivelle (2012) is the best introduction for students (Olivelle 2013; McClish and Olivelle 2012).

    3.4 Structure¶

    The Arthaśāstra is a carefully organised work. It is divided into fifteen books (adhikaraṇa), 150 chapters (adhyāya) and 180 topics (prakaraṇa), which do not always coincide with the chapters; a table of contents at the start lists the topics. Its prose is terse and technical, using the style of the śāstras, and many chapters end with one or more summarising verses. Table 3.1 lists the books.

    Table 3.1. The fifteen books of the Arthaśāstra (after Kangle and Olivelle)
    Book Title Chs. Main contents
    1 Vinayādhikārika 21 Training of the king; the sciences; ministers and their testing; spies; the council; envoys; protection of the king and princes; the king’s daily routine
    2 Adhyakṣapracāra 36 Settlement of the countryside; forts and the capital; the treasury and accounts; the superintendents of departments (mines, mint, trade, forests, armoury, weights and measures, customs, weaving, agriculture, liquor, slaughterhouses, prostitutes, shipping, cattle, horses, elephants, passports, pasture); the city superintendent
    3 Dharmasthīya 20 Courts of justice; contracts; marriage and the duties of spouses; inheritance; property and boundaries; debts, deposits, slaves and labourers; partnerships; sale; gifts; theft; verbal and physical assault; gambling
    4 Kaṇṭakaśodhana 13 “Removal of thorns”: supervision of artisans and merchants; remedies in calamities; detection of criminals by secret agents; arrest on suspicion; interrogation, including torture; punishments, including mutilation and execution
    5 Yogavṛtta 6 Secret means: covert punishment of seditious officials; replenishing the treasury in emergencies; salaries of state servants; conduct of courtiers; continuity of kingship
    6 Maṇḍalayoni 2 The constituent elements of the state; the circle of kings
    7 Ṣāḍguṇya 18 The six measures of foreign policy and their application
    8 Vyasanādhikārika 5 Calamities affecting the constituent elements
    9 Abhiyāsyatkarma 7 Preparations for a military expedition
    10 Sāṃgrāmika 6 War: camps, marches, battle arrays, kinds of fighting
    11 Saṃghavṛtta 1 Policy towards confederacies (saṃgha)
    12 Ābalīyasa 5 The weaker king facing a stronger one
    13 Durgalambhopāya 5 Means of taking a fort; pacifying conquered territory
    14 Aupaniṣadika 4 Secret practices: poisons, potions and occult recipes for use against enemies
    15 Tantrayukti 1 The methodical devices used in composing the treatise

    The order of the books follows a logic of widening circles: the person of the king; the administration of his own territory; justice and order within it; then relations with other states, war and conquest. Books 1–5 thus concern internal government and Books 6–14 external affairs. Book 15 explains the thirty-two “devices of composition” (tantrayukti), such as the statement of topic, the use of illustrations, exceptions and analogies, by which a śāstra is constructed and interpreted. Similar lists occur in medical texts, notably the Carakasaṃhitā and Suśrutasaṃhitā, and in later scholarly literature; the Arthaśāstra’s list belongs to a shared Sanskrit tradition of technical writing rather than being a unique methodological invention (Olivelle 2013).

    3.4.1 The Dialectical Method¶

    A characteristic feature of the text is its habit of citing other opinions before giving its own. On many questions it first reports “the teachers” (ācāryāḥ) or a named authority — Bhāradvāja, Viśālākṣa, Parāśara, Piśuna, Kauṇapadanta, Vātavyādhi, Bāhudantīputra — and then states the view of Kauṭilya (iti kauṭilyaḥ), usually in disagreement. On the selection of ministers (AŚ 1.8), for example, it reports a sequence of opinions: that a king should appoint his former fellow students, whose honesty he knows; that he should rather appoint those who share his secrets; that he should appoint those who have proven loyalty in difficulty; and so on, before giving its own view that ministers should be chosen for their capacity to do the work. These named teachers are otherwise almost unknown, and some may be literary devices; but the citations confirm that the text draws on an earlier literature, as its first sentence says.

    3.4.2 Schools and Teachers¶

    Besides individual teachers, the Arthaśāstra cites the opinions of schools named after legendary founders: the Mānavas (followers of Manu), the Bārhaspatyas (of Bṛhaspati, the preceptor of the gods), the Auśanasas (of Uśanas or Śukra, the preceptor of the demons), the Pārāśaras and the Āmbhīyas. Their views are reported on such matters as the number of sciences a king needs, the size of the council of ministers and the relative gravity of calamities. No work of these schools survives, and the names may be partly honorific; but their citation shows that the authors of the Arthaśāstra saw themselves as working within an established discipline with competing doctrines. The treatise’s opening salutation is to Śukra and Bṛhaspati, the two mythical founders of the science (Kangle 1960; Olivelle 2013).

    3.4.3 Religion as an Instrument¶

    A feature of the Arthaśāstra that has troubled many readers is its use of religion for political purposes. The king is to honour Brahmins and ascetics and to support temples, but he is also to use them. Secret agents disguised as holy men are to win the confidence of the people and of the enemy; in an emergency, the king may raise money by having agents set up a shrine or an image overnight and organising festivals and fairs to collect offerings, or by staging the appearance of a serpent or other portent and charging people to see it (AŚ 5.2). In war, the king should have his agents proclaim his omniscience and his association with the gods to encourage his own troops and frighten the enemy’s, and astrologers should declare that victory is certain (AŚ 10.3; 13.1). Such passages show that the authors regarded religious belief as a social force that a ruler could manipulate. They also explain why later moralists condemned the text, and why modern readers have compared it with Machiavelli’s discussion of the political uses of religion.

    3.5 The Contents in Outline¶

    3.5.1 The Training of the King¶

    Book 1 makes the king’s self-discipline the foundation of government. Training (vinaya) depends on the sciences studied under teachers, on association with the elders, and above all on the conquest of the senses (indriyajaya): a king who is controlled by lust, anger, greed, pride, arrogance or excitement — the “six enemies” — perishes, and the text lists kings of legend who were destroyed by each (AŚ 1.6). A widely quoted chain of maxims — “the root of happiness is dharma, the root of dharma is artha, the root of artha is the kingdom, the root of the kingdom is the conquest of the senses” — is often attributed to the Arthaśāstra, but it is not in the treatise; it opens a later collection of aphorisms ascribed to Cāṇakya (the Cāṇakyasūtra). The idea, however, is faithful to Book 1.

    The king’s day and night are each divided into eight periods of about one and a half hours each, measured by the water-clock (nālikā), with fixed duties for each (AŚ 1.19). In outline: in the day, the king hears reports on defence and on income and expenditure; attends to the affairs of the town and country people; bathes, eats and studies; receives revenue and assigns tasks to heads of departments; consults his ministers by letter and receives secret reports; takes recreation or deliberates; inspects the elephants, horses, chariots and troops; and plans military operations with the commander. At night, he receives secret agents; bathes, eats and studies; in the third period retires to bed to the sound of music, and sleeps through the fourth and fifth; in the sixth, woken by music, reflects on the teachings of the science and on the work to be done; consults his counsellors and sends out spies; and receives the blessings of priests and teachers and sees his physician, chief cook and astrologer. The schedule may be adjusted to the king’s capacity. The same chapter contains the famous verse (AŚ 1.19.34) quoted in Chapter 5: the king’s happiness lies in the happiness of his subjects.

    3.5.2 Administration and Political Economy¶

    Book 2, the longest, describes the settlement of new land, the building of forts and the capital, the treasury and the system of accounts, and the duties of some thirty superintendents (adhyakṣa) in charge of departments ranging from mines and the mint to weaving, agriculture, liquor and shipping. It is the source of most of what is written about “Kauṭilyan administration” and is treated in Chapter 6. Two points should be stressed here. First, Book 2 is normative: it describes how its author thought a kingdom ought to be run, not how any known kingdom was run. Second, its picture is of a state that seeks to control and profit from almost every economic activity — through crown lands, state workshops, monopolies, licences, tolls and fines — and that watches its own officials with suspicion, listing forty ways in which they embezzle (AŚ 2.8; Chapter 6).

    3.5.3 Law and Coercion¶

    Books 3 and 4 concern justice. Book 3, “On Judges”, deals with the courts and with private law — contracts, marriage, inheritance, debts, sale, labour and assault — and sets out the four bases of a lawsuit, in which royal edict overrides custom, transaction and dharma (AŚ 3.1.39; Chapter 12). Book 4, “The Removal of Thorns”, describes the suppression of crime by magistrates (pradeṣṭṛ) and secret agents, including the use of torture in interrogation (AŚ 4.8) and a range of punishments including mutilation and death. These books are discussed in Chapters 12, 13 and 14.

    3.5.4 Secret Agents and Covert Action¶

    The Arthaśāstra is famous, and for some readers notorious, for its reliance on secret agents (gūḍhapuruṣa), stationary and roving, who test the loyalty of ministers with secret temptations (upadhā; Chapter 4) and watch officials, the population and foreign courts (AŚ 1.10–12; the network is described in Chapter 7). Book 5 prescribes “silent punishment” (upāṃśudaṇḍa) — secret killing — for seditious high officials, and ways of raising money in an emergency that include deceiving the pious with staged miracles. Book 1.13 recommends that spies spread among the people a story about the origin of kingship, so that they accept taxes and punishment (Chapter 5). Books 11–14 recommend sowing dissension among confederacies, assassination of enemy leaders, and the use of poisons and magical preparations. Any honest account of the text must give this side its due weight: the Arthaśāstra’s political vision is one of a ruler who maintains order and welfare through pervasive surveillance, manipulation and, when necessary, violence (Singh 2017; Boesche 2002).

    3.5.5 Foreign Policy and War¶

    Books 6–14 present the theory of the circle of states (maṇḍala), the six measures of foreign policy (ṣāḍguṇya), the calamities that may weaken a state, and the conduct of war and sieges; they are treated in Chapters 4 and 7.

    3.5.6 Confederacies¶

    Book 11 is short but historically valuable, a witness to the non-monarchical polities of early India seen through the eyes of a monarchist who wishes to divide and absorb them. It distinguishes two kinds of confederacy (saṃgha): those whose members live by the title of “king” (rājaśabdopajīvin) — among them the Licchavikas, Vṛjikas, Mallakas, Madrakas, Kukuras, Kurus and Pāñcālas — and those whose members live by agriculture and trade as well as arms, such as the Kāmbojas and Surāṣṭras (AŚ 11.1). Several of these names are those of the clan-based polities known from Buddhist texts and from inscriptions and coins (Chapter 8). The text advises the king to win over a confederacy if it is united, and otherwise to break it up by sowing quarrels among its leaders, by bribery, by provoking rivalry over women and honours, and by assassination. Confederacies are dangerous, it says, because they are hard to defeat when united.

    3.6 The Arthaśāstra and the Mauryan State¶

    Because tradition linked the Arthaśāstra to Candragupta Maurya, generations of historians used it as a source for the Mauryan empire. Two other bodies of evidence make it possible to test that use: the fragments of Megasthenes and the inscriptions of Aśoka.

    Megasthenes, as quoted by later Greek writers, described Indian society as divided into seven “classes” — philosophers, farmers, herdsmen and hunters, artisans and traders, soldiers, overseers (inspectors) and councillors — and spoke of officials who supervised markets, roads and irrigation, of city magistrates organised in boards, and of a large army maintained by the king. Some of this is reminiscent of the Arthaśāstra’s superintendents and spies, but much is not: the Arthaśāstra knows nothing of boards of city magistrates, and Megasthenes’ “seven classes” correspond to no Indian classification. Megasthenes was also capable of serious misunderstanding — he reported, for example, that Indians did not use writing in legal matters (Thapar 1961).

    Aśoka’s inscriptions name officials called mahāmātra, rajjūka, prādeśika and yukta, as well as the “great officers of dhamma” that Aśoka created; they describe tours of inspection, judicial officers, and measures for the welfare of people and animals (Chapter 8). The vocabulary only partly overlaps with that of the Arthaśāstra, whose characteristic officers — the collector (samāhartṛ), the treasurer (sannidhātṛ) and the superintendents — are not named in the edicts. Romila Thapar, who used the Arthaśāstra extensively in her study of Aśoka, already cautioned that it could not be taken as a description of the Mauryan state, and later scholarship on the date of the text has reinforced the caution (Thapar 1961; Olivelle 2013).

    The relationship is therefore best described as follows. The Arthaśāstra was compiled in the centuries after the Mauryas from earlier materials, some of which may go back to the Mauryan period; it describes an idealised kingdom of moderate size; and it can be used, with care, as evidence for the political ideas and administrative practices known to its authors, but not as a handbook of Mauryan government.

    3.7 Style, Audience and Ideology¶

    The Arthaśāstra is a technical treatise for specialists. It assumes a reader who knows the conventions of śāstric prose, and it addresses the king or his chief minister directly. Its tone is coolly practical: it treats moral and religious institutions largely in terms of their usefulness to the state, and it repeatedly considers several possible courses of action and their likely results. This has earned it comparisons with Machiavelli (see the box below), which capture its tone better than its purposes. The text is also, as McClish has emphasised, deeply concerned with the king’s role as guardian of the social order defined by varṇa and āśrama; it requires the king to ensure that each person follows his own dharma (svadharma) and declares that a people so governed “prospers and does not perish” (AŚ 1.3–4). Its vision of the good kingdom is a hierarchical one, in which Śūdras serve, women are guarded, and slaves and bonded labourers are regulated rather than freed (Chapters 6 and 13) (McClish 2019).

    Comparison: Kauṭilya and Machiavelli

    Modern readers often set the Arthaśāstra beside Machiavelli’s The Prince (written 1513). Both are works of political realism that advise rulers on acquiring and keeping power and both accept deception and violence when the state requires them. The differences are as important: the Arthaśāstra is a comprehensive administrative manual, not a short political essay; it is embedded in a religious and social order that it does not question; and it was transmitted as a technical śāstra, not as an intervention in a political crisis. Max Weber’s well-known remark that, compared with the Arthaśāstra, Machiavelli’s Prince is “harmless” reflects the shock of early twentieth-century European readers at the text’s frankness about espionage and covert killing (Weber 1919; Boesche 2002).

    3.8 Reception¶

    The Arthaśāstra became the classic of its genre. Later writers cite it as the authority on government, and some criticise it. Kāmandaka’s Nītisāra presents itself as a verse digest of Viṣṇugupta’s teaching (Section 3.9). Daṇḍin’s prose romance Daśakumāracarita (c. 7th century) has a character mock the “science of polity” composed by Viṣṇugupta for the Maurya, and Bāṇa’s Kādambarī (7th century) has the minister Śukanāsa condemn the teaching of Kauṭilya as “merciless”. These passages show both the text’s prestige and the moral unease it provoked among literary authors (Olivelle 2013).

    The ideas of the Arthaśāstra also passed into other genres. The Mahābhārata’s Śānti Parvan (Book 12), the Manusmṛti (Book 7) and the Yājñavalkyasmṛti contain long sections on royal policy that draw on the same body of ideas (Chapter 5). Animal fables such as the Pañcatantra, framed as instruction for princes, teach the same maxims of policy through stories.

    3.8.1 The Later Nīti Tradition¶

    Works on polity continued to be written for more than a thousand years. Among the most important are:

    • Kāmandaka’s Nītisāra (c. 500–700 CE), a verse epitome of Kauṭilyan doctrine, described in the next section.

    • The Tirukkuṟaḷ (c. 5th century CE), whose second book, Poruṭpāl (“on wealth”, the Tamil counterpart of artha), contains 700 couplets on kingship, ministers, the army, allies and the economy. It represents a Tamil tradition of political ethics that shares ideas with Sanskrit nīti but has its own voice (Volume 8 of this series).

    • Somadeva Sūri’s Nītivākyāmṛta (10th century), a Jain work in aphorisms that adapts the Arthaśāstra’s categories to a Jain ethical framework (Chapter 8).

    • The Mānasollāsa (1131) of the Cālukya king Someśvara III, an encyclopaedia of kingship covering government as well as the royal pleasures.

    • Caṇḍeśvara’s Rājanītiratnākara (14th century, Mithilā), a digest of royal policy compiled from earlier dharmaśāstra and nīti sources.

    • The Śukranīti, attributed to the mythical preceptor Śukra or Uśanas. Despite claims of great antiquity, modern scholarship has shown it to be a late work; its mentions of firearms and other features have led several scholars to date it to the nineteenth century (Gopal 1962). It cannot be used as evidence for ancient Indian polity.

    3.8.2 Polity in Epic, Fable and Maxim¶

    Political wisdom also circulated in more popular forms. The Mahābhārata contains, besides the Śānti Parvan, a long discourse of the wise counsellor Vidura to the blind king Dhṛtarāṣṭra in the Udyoga Parvan (the “Vidura-nīti”), a collection of maxims on the qualities of the wise and the foolish, the duties of kings, the dangers of greed and the value of good counsel. The Pañcatantra (perhaps c. 300–500 CE), framed as a series of stories told by a Brahmin to teach three dull princes the science of polity, conveys the maxims of nīti through animal fables: its five books deal with the breaking of alliances, the winning of friends, war and peace (illustrated by the war of the crows and the owls), the loss of what has been gained, and hasty action. It was translated into Middle Persian in the sixth century and from there passed into Arabic (as Kalīla wa Dimna) and many other languages, becoming one of the most widely travelled works of Indian literature. The Hitopadeśa of Nārāyaṇa, a later reworking from eastern India, continued the tradition.

    Collections of gnomic verse also carried political maxims: the Nītiśataka (“Hundred Verses on Conduct”) attributed to Bhartṛhari, and various collections of sayings attributed to Cāṇakya, which circulated widely in Sanskrit and in translation. These works are less systematic than the Arthaśāstra, but they were far more widely known, and they shaped the political common sense of literate Indians for centuries. Volume 8 of this series discusses them as literature.

    3.8.3 Medieval and Early Modern Works¶

    Works on polity continued to be produced in the medieval and early modern periods, often as parts of larger encyclopaedic projects such as the Mānasollāsa and the digests of Dharmaśāstra, whose sections on rājadharma — like Caṇḍeśvara’s Rājanītiratnākara — gathered smṛti and nīti material on the king, his ministers, his chaplain, the treasury and the army. In sixteenth-century Vijayanagara, the emperor Kṛṣṇadevarāya included in his Telugu poem Āmuktamālyada a section of advice on government — on choosing officials, managing forts and ports, encouraging trade and treating subjects justly (Chapter 17). In western India the Maratha state produced a Sanskrit administrative lexicon and treatises on government (Chapter 18). These works show that the tradition of polity remained a living field of writing, adapting older categories to new circumstances.

    From the thirteenth century, Persian works on statecraft and ethics — the “mirrors for princes” and akhlāq literature — became a second major tradition of political thought in India (Chapter 18). Since its rediscovery, the Arthaśāstra has found a new readership among historians, political scientists and students of strategy (Chapter 20). It has been translated into many Indian languages, and the figure of Cāṇakya — far more than the text — has become a popular hero of plays, novels, films and television, often portrayed as the master strategist who unified India against foreign invaders. These modern images draw on the medieval legend of Cāṇakya, on the drama Mudrārākṣasa and on nationalist historiography rather than on the treatise itself, whose pages never mention the Mauryas: only its closing verses allude to the overthrow of the Nanda king and name Viṣṇugupta as the author.

    3.9 Kāmandaka’s Nītisāra¶

    The most important treatise on polity after the Arthaśāstra is the Nītisāra (“Essence of Policy”) of Kāmandaka, also called Kāmandaki. Its date cannot be fixed. It is later than the Arthaśāstra, which it summarises, and earlier than the medieval works on rājadharma that quote it; the range c. 500–700 CE used in this series is a common estimate, and some scholars place it a century or two earlier. Nothing is known of the author beyond the name. The work is in verse, mostly in the śloka metre, and is divided into about twenty cantos (sarga).

    3.9.1 A Digest of Viṣṇugupta¶

    Kāmandaka is open about his debt. His opening verses praise Viṣṇugupta, who destroyed the Nandas and “drew out the nectar of the science of polity from the great ocean of the science of artha”, and present the work as a condensation of that teaching for the instruction of kings. The claim is accurate as far as it goes. The Nītisāra takes over the Arthaśāstra’s categories almost entire: the king’s training and the conquest of the senses, the seven constituents of the kingdom and their calamities (Chapter 4), the circle of kings, the six measures of policy and the four means (Chapter 7), envoys and spies, and the conduct of expeditions, camps and battle.

    What it leaves out is as telling as what it keeps. The departments and superintendents of Book 2, with their crown farms, mines, workshops, tolls and accounts (Chapter 6), are almost entirely absent; so are the private law and the courts of Books 3 and 4, and the poisons and occult recipes of Book 14. The Arthaśāstra was a manual for those who ran a kingdom; the Nītisāra is a book of counsel for the man at its head. Its verse form, easier to memorise than the Arthaśāstra’s terse prose, suited that purpose, and its tone is that of a teacher addressing a prince.

    3.9.2 What Kāmandaka Changes¶

    The shift of audience brings a shift of emphasis. The opening cantos dwell at length on the king’s self-discipline, on the vices that ruin kings and on the company he should keep, before the work turns to ministers, allies and enemies. The analysis of the maṇḍala is restated and extended: Kāmandaka discusses the circle of kings at length and reports alternative schemes in which it has different numbers of members (Section 7.6). On violence the work is as unsentimental as its model — it discusses stratagem, covert killing and the destruction of enemies without embarrassment — but it frames these within a calculus of restraint. Upinder Singh has argued that Kāmandaka treats force as the last of the means, to be used when conciliation, gifts and dissension have failed, and that his discussion of war is shaped throughout by a concern with its costs and with the king’s own discipline, so that the text offers a more reflective account of political violence than is usually allowed (Singh 2010, 2017). The framework of dharma is also more explicit than in the Arthaśāstra: the king’s task is to keep the varṇas and āśramas in their places and to pursue dharma, artha and kāma in balance, a view close to that of the Dharmaśāstras (Chapter 5).

    3.9.3 Transmission¶

    Unlike the Arthaśāstra, the Nītisāra never dropped out of circulation. Later authors on polity, among them the Jain Somadeva (Chapter 8) and the compilers of the medieval digests (Chapter 17), drew on it, and it was edited and printed in Calcutta, in the Bibliotheca Indica series, in the nineteenth century, while the Arthaśāstra itself was still known only from quotations (Section 3.3). For much of the second millennium, therefore, Kauṭilyan doctrine was known to most readers in Kāmandaka’s version. Modern scholarship has paid it far less attention than the Arthaśāstra, and its later reception, in India and beyond, remains little studied.

    3.10 Reading the Arthaśāstra Today¶

    How should a modern reader approach a text like the Arthaśāstra? Three cautions emerge from the scholarship surveyed in this chapter.

    The first is to respect its genre. The Arthaśāstra is a śāstra: a systematic body of rules and analyses, composed by specialists for specialists, which aims at completeness and consistency. Its many lists, definitions and cases are not records of observation but products of a method of classification. When the text gives the salaries of officials, the rates of tolls or the number of villages in a district, it is offering a model, not reporting statistics.

    The second is to take its history into account. The text we have is the product of several centuries of compilation and revision. Its sources may preserve material from the Mauryan period or earlier, and its final redaction belongs to the third century CE or thereabouts. Its political world — a kingdom of moderate size among rival kingdoms — is that of the post-Mauryan centuries. Using it as evidence for any particular period requires care.

    The third is to read it whole. Selective reading has produced two opposite images of the Arthaśāstra: a cynical manual of power politics, and a benevolent charter of welfare government. Both rest on real passages; neither does justice to the text. The Arthaśāstra combines a genuine concern for the prosperity and order of the kingdom, and for the protection of subjects from officials and criminals, with an acceptance of espionage, torture, covert killing and deception as instruments of rule, and with a social vision in which varṇa hierarchy, slavery and the subordination of women are taken for granted. Its intellectual distinction lies in the clarity and comprehensiveness with which it analyses the problems of governing; its moral world is that of an early historic monarchy, not of a modern democracy (Olivelle 2013; Trautmann 2012; Singh 2017).

    3.12 Summary¶

    • The Arthaśāstra is the only complete surviving treatise of the Indian science of politics. It defines its subject as the means of acquiring and protecting the earth, and it treats economics, administration, law, foreign policy and war as parts of a single science.

    • Its attribution to Kauṭilya (Cāṇakya, Viṣṇugupta), minister of Candragupta Maurya, is traditional. Trautmann’s statistical study showed that it is the work of several hands; Olivelle dates the Kauṭilya recension to c. 50–125 CE and a later redaction to c. 175–300 CE. The text is best dated c. 50 BCE–300 CE.

    • It was rediscovered by R. Shamasastry in 1905 and published in 1909 (text) and 1915 (translation); Kangle’s critical edition (1960–65) and Olivelle’s translation (2013) are now standard.

    • Its fifteen books move from the training of the king through administration and law to foreign policy, war and secret practices; Book 15 explains its method of composition.

    • It was praised and criticised by later Sanskrit authors and condensed into verse by Kāmandaka, whose Nītisāra kept its theory of the king, the constituents and foreign policy but dropped its administration and law; it was followed by a long tradition of works on polity in Sanskrit, Tamil and other languages; the Śukranīti, often cited as ancient, is a late work.

    Further Reading¶

    The standard translation is Olivelle (2013), with an important introduction on date and composition; McClish and Olivelle (2012) is a good selection for students. Kangle (1960) remains the basic critical edition and study, and Rangarajan (1992) a readable topical version. On authorship and date see Trautmann (1971), McClish (2019) and the short book by Trautmann (2012). Boesche (2002) and Singh (2017) discuss the text’s political thought, the latter with particular attention to violence; Singh (2010) is the best short study of Kāmandaka. Scharfe (1989) places it in the wider history of the Indian state.
