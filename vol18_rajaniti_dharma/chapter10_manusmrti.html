    Chapter 10: The Manusmṛti¶

    No Indian legal text has been more admired or more hated than the Mānava Dharmaśāstra, the “treatise on dharma of Manu”, better known as the Manusmṛti or, in English, the “Laws of Manu”. For the commentators of the first and second millennia it was the most authoritative of the smṛtis. For the British administrators who sought a “code of the Hindus” it was the obvious first text to translate, and William Jones’s version of 1794 made it one of the best-known Sanskrit works in Europe. For the anti-caste movements of the nineteenth and twentieth centuries it was the charter of Brahmanical oppression, and on 25 December 1927 B. R. Ambedkar and his followers burned it publicly at Mahad. It remains a symbol over which Indians argue.

    This chapter tries to read the text as a historian should: to say what it is, when and how it was composed, what it contains and how it was received, and to set out plainly its provisions on varṇa, untouchability and women, which are central to its social vision and cannot be explained away. It draws throughout on Patrick Olivelle’s critical edition and translation (Olivelle 2005).

    10.1 The Text¶

    10.1.1 Frame and Authority¶

    The Manusmṛti presents itself as the teaching of Manu, the first human being and ancestor of humanity, son of the self-existent creator. In its opening verses the great seers approach Manu and ask him to teach them the dharmas of all the varṇas and of the intermediate classes. Manu describes the creation of the world and states that he learned the treatise from the creator; he then asks his pupil Bhṛgu to recite it, and the rest of the text is spoken by Bhṛgu (1.1–60). The frame gives the text the highest possible authority: it is not the opinion of a scholar but the primeval law of humanity. It also makes the text a Brahmin’s account of dharma, addressed to Brahmins: the seers ask, Bhṛgu speaks, and the work repeatedly declares that Brahmins are the lords of creation.

    10.1.2 Date¶

    The text cannot be dated precisely, but its relative position is clear. It is later than the Dharmasūtras and earlier than the Yājñavalkyasmṛti, and it knows the Greeks (yavana), Śakas and Pahlavas as peoples of the north-west, whom it classes, with other peoples on the margins of its world, as Kṣatriyas who have sunk to the status of Śūdras through the neglect of rites (10.43–44); Aloka Parasher has traced such Brahmanical attitudes to outsiders up to c. 600 CE (Parasher 1991). Olivelle places its composition in the second century CE, and the series follows this date; older estimates ranging from the second century BCE to the second century CE are still found in the literature (Olivelle 2005). It is thus a work of the post-Mauryan period, composed in a world of foreign dynasties and powerful Buddhist and Jain communities, when Brahmin authors had reason to restate their vision of a social order headed by Brahmins and protected by a king loyal to them.

    10.1.3 Unity of Composition¶

    Earlier scholars often treated the text as a stratified compilation, built up by successive authors over several centuries, and used this hypothesis to explain its apparent contradictions — for example, between verses that honour women and verses that subordinate them. Some have hoped that the harshest provisions on caste and gender might prove to be late interpolations. Olivelle’s critical edition, based on a comprehensive study of the manuscripts and commentaries, points the other way. He argues that the Manusmṛti was composed by a single, highly skilled author, or by a strong chairman working with a team, according to a carefully designed plan, and that its structure is deliberate; he identifies relatively few interpolations. The tensions within the text, on this view, are tensions within its author’s vision, not evidence of different hands (Olivelle 2005). The claim, sometimes repeated, that Olivelle showed the restrictive provisions on women or on Śūdras to be later additions is not correct.

    10.1.4 Structure¶

    The critical edition has about 2,685 verses in twelve chapters. Olivelle shows that the text is organised in four parts: the creation of the world (chapter 1); the sources of dharma (2.1–24); the dharma of the four varṇas, including the life of the student, the householder, the forest-dweller and the renouncer, the king and the law (2.25–11.266); and the consequences of action and the path to liberation (chapter 12). Table 10.1 summarises the contents chapter by chapter.

    10.2 The Householder’s World¶

    Most of the Manusmṛti is not about law in the narrow sense but about the life of the twice-born man, above all the Brahmin householder. Chapter 2 describes the student’s life: his initiation, his duties to his teacher, the rules of begging, celibacy and Vedic recitation. Chapters 3 to 5 describe the householder: his marriage (Chapter 15), the five “great sacrifices” he must perform daily — to the Veda by study, to the ancestors by offerings of water and food, to the gods by oblations in the fire, to beings by scattering food, and to men by hospitality to guests (3.70) — the ancestral rites, and the rules of food, impurity and purification. The householder is praised as the support of all the other orders: as all creatures live by depending on air, so all the orders live by depending on the householder (3.77).

    Manu’s account of how a Brahmin should earn his living is revealing. He lists modes of livelihood in descending order of purity: gleaning grain from fields (called ṛta, “truth”); accepting what is given unasked (amṛta, “immortal”); begging (mṛta, “dead”); agriculture (pramṛta, “killing”, because ploughing destroys living beings); and trade (satyānṛta, “truth-and-falsehood”); service of others is “the dog’s way of life” and is to be avoided (4.4–6). In distress a Brahmin may live by the occupations of the Kṣatriya and, failing that, of the Vaiśya, though he should avoid agriculture because it injures living beings (10.81–84). The scheme expresses a Brahmanical ideal of detachment from worldly production; it also shows how the texts ranked occupations, placing agriculture and service low and treating trade with suspicion.

    Chapter 6 describes the forest-dweller and the renouncer, who abandon the household and live by alms. Chapter 11 sets out gifts and penances, and chapter 12 the consequences of action: rebirth according to one’s deeds and the three qualities (guṇa) of goodness, passion and darkness, and the path of knowledge that leads to liberation. In this frame, the law of chapters 7 to 9 is one part of a comprehensive account of the right life, which ends not in the court but in release from rebirth.

    10.3 The Social Order: Varṇa¶

    10.3.1 The Creation of the Varṇas¶

    The Manusmṛti grounds the social hierarchy in creation. “For the growth of the worlds, he brought forth from his mouth, arms, thighs and feet the Brahmin, the Kṣatriya, the Vaiśya and the Śūdra” (1.31), an image derived from the Ṛgvedic Puruṣasūkta (Chapter 2). To each the creator assigned activities: to Brahmins, teaching and study, sacrificing for themselves and others, giving and receiving gifts; to Kṣatriyas, protecting the people, giving, sacrificing and study; to Vaiśyas, cattle, giving, sacrificing, study, trade, money-lending and agriculture; “but the Lord assigned only one activity to the Śūdra: service to these very varṇas without resentment” (1.88–91). Because he was born from the mouth, the Brahmin is the lord of this whole creation (1.93). The text declares that there are only four varṇas: the Brahmin, Kṣatriya and Vaiśya are “twice-born”, the Śūdra “once-born”, “and there is no fifth” (10.4).

    10.3.2 The Twice-born and the Śūdra¶

    The line between the twice-born and the Śūdra runs through the whole text. Only the twice-born undergo Vedic initiation and study the Veda; a Brahmin must not teach the Veda to a Śūdra, nor give him advice, nor teach him dharma or penance (4.80–81). The Śūdra’s dharma is service, above all service of Brahmins, “which alone is declared to be his highest duty” (10.123). He should not accumulate wealth even if he is able to, “for a Śūdra who has acquired wealth harasses Brahmins” (10.129). A Brahmin may confidently take the property of his Śūdra slave, for the slave has no property of his own (8.417), and a Śūdra, whether bought or not, may be made to do servile work, “for he was created by the Self-existent one for the service of the Brahmin” (8.413). Even if freed by his master, a Śūdra is not freed from servitude, “for it is innate in him” (8.414).

    10.3.3 The Mixed Classes and the Caṇḍāla¶

    Chapter 10 explains the many occupational communities (jāti) of the author’s world as the offspring of unions between members of different varṇas. Unions in which the husband is of higher varṇa than the wife (anuloma, “with the grain”) are tolerated; those in which the wife is of higher varṇa (pratiloma, “against the grain”) produce degraded classes, the lowest of all being the Caṇḍāla, the child of a Śūdra man and a Brahmin woman (10.12). The text’s treatment of the Caṇḍālas and similar groups is a classic statement of what would later be called untouchability:

    The dwelling of Caṇḍālas and Śvapacas shall be outside the village; they must use discarded vessels, and their wealth shall be dogs and donkeys. Their garments shall be the clothes of the dead; they shall eat from broken dishes; their ornaments shall be of iron; and they must wander constantly. A man who follows the dharma should not seek contact with them. … At night they must not walk about in villages and towns. (Manu 10.51–54, paraphrased after Olivelle)

    They are to carry out executions and to take the clothes, beds and ornaments of those executed (10.56).

    The chapter assigns occupations to the mixed classes. The Sūta, it says, should look after horses and chariots, the Ambaṣṭha practise medicine, the Vaidehaka serve in the women’s quarters and the Māgadha engage in trade; the Niṣāda should fish; the Āyogava work in wood; the Medas, Andhras, Cuñcus and Madgus hunt wild animals; the Kṣattṛs, Ugras and Pukkasas catch and kill animals living in holes; the Dhigvaṇas work leather; and the Veṇas play musical instruments (10.46–49). Several of these names were names of real peoples or occupational groups, and some, like the Andhras, of regions. The text thus attempts to fit the occupations of a complex society, and peoples on its margins, into a scheme of descent from the four varṇas, ranking them by the degree of their supposed mixture and by the impurity of their work. Many of the occupations it degrades — leather-working, hunting, fishing, the handling of the dead — were those of groups later classed as untouchable.

    The chapter also sets out what the varṇas may do in times of distress (āpad). A Brahmin who cannot live by his own occupations may adopt those of the Kṣatriya and then the Vaiśya; a Kṣatriya may live as a Vaiśya; a Vaiśya unable to live by his own work may do a Śūdra’s work, but must abandon it when he can; a Śūdra unable to find service may live by crafts (10.81–100). The rules acknowledge economic reality while insisting on the hierarchy: a lower varṇa may never take up the occupation of a higher one, and a man who lives by a higher varṇa’s work is to be punished by the king with confiscation of property and banishment (10.95–96). This taxonomy of mixed classes has no historical value as an account of the origins of real communities; it is a Brahmanical theory designed to fit an existing world of hereditary occupational groups into the four-varṇa scheme, and to rank them. Its importance lies in its later authority. Uma Chakravarti and R. S. Sharma have shown how such texts helped to fix the low status of labouring and “impure” groups, and Ambedkar devoted much of his scholarship to the history of untouchability that they record (Sharma 1958; Chakravarti 2003; Ambedkar 1946).

    10.3.4 Varṇa and the Law¶

    The varṇa hierarchy shapes Manu’s law at every point. The king is to compel Vaiśyas and Śūdras to do their work (8.418). Witnesses are to be of the same varṇa as the parties and are sworn in different ways according to varṇa (8.68, 8.113; Chapter 12). Interest rates rise as the debtor’s varṇa falls (8.142; Chapter 13). Above all, punishments vary with the varṇa of offender and victim, a principle set out in detail in Chapter 14. A Śūdra who reviles or strikes a twice-born man suffers corporal punishment or mutilation, while a Brahmin who reviles a Śūdra pays a small fine (8.267–283); a Śūdra’s sexual offence against a guarded twice-born woman may cost him his life, a Brahmin’s only a fine (8.374–385); a Brahmin is never to be executed, “for there is no greater injustice on earth than killing a Brahmin” (8.379–381); and even the penance for homicide shrinks as the victim’s varṇa falls (11.127). One principle runs the other way — the heavier guilt of a high-born thief (8.337–338), discussed with the king’s thousandfold fine in Chapter 14 — but it is confined to theft and cannot offset the systematic privileging of Brahmins and degradation of Śūdras in the rest of the law.

    10.3.5 Varṇa, Jāti and “Caste”¶

    Modern scholars emphasise that varṇa, the fourfold scheme of the texts, and jāti, the many endogamous hereditary groups of social life, are different things, and that the English word “caste” blurs them. That is true, and the history of the caste system cannot be read off the Manusmṛti. But the distinction should not be used to minimise the text’s significance. Manu’s chapter 10 is precisely an attempt to map the jātis onto the varṇas and to rank them, and his rules on purity, commensality, marriage and occupation became, through the commentaries and digests and later through colonial courts, a powerful resource for those who defended hierarchy (Dirks 2001; Chakravarti 2003).

    10.4 Women in the Manusmṛti¶

    The Manusmṛti’s provisions on women are the second great focus of modern debate. They must be read together, not selectively.

    10.4.1 Honour and Protection¶

    The text contains a well-known passage on honouring women: “Where women are revered, there the gods rejoice; but where they are not revered, all rites are fruitless. Where the women of the family grieve, that family quickly perishes; but where they do not grieve, it always prospers” (3.56–57). Fathers, brothers, husbands and brothers-in-law who desire prosperity should honour women and give them ornaments, clothes and food (3.55, 3.59). A mother, the text says elsewhere, surpasses a thousand fathers in veneration (2.145). The verses belong to the critical text, and the concern they voice for the welfare of women within the household is not to be dismissed.

    10.4.2 Dependence and Control¶

    The same text insists that women must never be independent. “Her father guards her in childhood, her husband guards her in youth, and her sons guard her in old age; a woman is not fit for independence” (pitā rakṣati kaumāre bhartā rakṣati yauvane / rakṣanti sthavire putrā na strī svātantryam arhati, 9.3; compare 5.147–148). Men must guard their women day and night, because women are by nature fickle and lustful: the text attributes to them, at its creation, a love of bed and seat and ornament, impure desires, anger, crookedness, malice and bad conduct (9.14–17). Women are excluded from the Vedic rites performed with mantras, and the text declares that marriage is for women the equivalent of Vedic initiation, serving the husband the equivalent of living with a teacher, and housework the equivalent of tending the sacred fires (2.66–67). A virtuous wife should constantly worship her husband as a god, even if he is devoid of virtue, given to lust or without good qualities (5.154).

    10.4.3 Widowhood and Remarriage¶

    The Manusmṛti opposes the remarriage of widows. After her husband’s death a woman should emaciate her body, living on flowers, roots and fruits, and should never mention the name of another man; a virtuous wife who remains chaste after her husband’s death goes to heaven even without a son, while one who breaks faith with him is reborn in the womb of a jackal (5.157–164). “Nowhere is a second husband prescribed for good women” (5.162). The text describes the older practice of levirate (niyoga), in which a widow or the wife of an impotent man might be appointed to bear a son by a kinsman of the husband (9.59–63), but then condemns it as fit only for animals, a practice introduced in the reign of the wicked king Vena, and states that the marriage formulae nowhere mention niyoga or the remarriage of widows (9.64–68). The text’s waiting periods for a wife whose husband has gone abroad — eight years if he has gone for a religious purpose, six for learning or fame, three for pleasure (9.76) — have sometimes been read as permitting her to remarry afterwards; the text does not say so, and the commentators understood the rule otherwise. Only a virgin widow, whose marriage was not consummated, may undergo the marriage rite again with her husband’s brother (9.69–70).

    10.4.4 Property¶

    Manu recognises a category of women’s property (strīdhana): what was given before the nuptial fire, at the bridal procession, as a token of love, and by her brother, mother and father (9.194), which passes on her death to her children (Chapter 15). Yet he also states that “a wife, a son and a slave — these three are declared to have no property; whatever they earn belongs to the man to whom they belong” (8.416). Commentators reconciled the two by distinguishing kinds of property and degrees of control. The text gives daughters a share of inheritance only through an “appointed daughter” (putrikā) when there is no son, and recommends that brothers give their unmarried sisters a portion of their own shares (9.118).

    10.4.5 Interpreting the Tension¶

    The coexistence of these provisions is not a puzzle to be solved by assigning them to different authors. Uma Chakravarti’s analysis of “Brahmanical patriarchy” explains it as a coherent system: the honour paid to women within the household and the control exercised over their sexuality both served to preserve the purity of lineage and thus of caste, which required that women’s sexuality — especially that of upper-caste women — be closely guarded (Chakravarti 1993, 2003). Julia Leslie’s study of a later text, the eighteenth-century Strīdharmapaddhati, shows how such norms were transmitted and elaborated (Leslie 1989). These analyses do not deny that the text expresses concern for women’s welfare; they show that this concern operated within a framework of subordination.

    10.5 The King and the Law¶

    Chapters 7 to 9 are the Manusmṛti’s treatise on government and law, and they are discussed in detail in Chapters 5 and 12–14. Their main features may be noted here.

    • The king is created by the Lord from particles of the gods for the protection of all (7.3–8). His instrument is punishment (daṇḍa), which “rules all subjects, protects them, and keeps watch while they sleep” (7.18).

    • Taxation is moderate: one-sixth, one-eighth or one-twelfth of grain, one-fiftieth of cattle and gold; artisans and Śūdras who live by labour owe one day’s work a month; a Brahmin learned in the Veda is never to be taxed (7.130–138).

    • The court is held daily by the king or a Brahmin judge with three assessors (8.1–11). Manu explains that if justice is defeated by injustice, a quarter of the sin falls on the wrongdoer, a quarter on the witness, a quarter on the court and a quarter on the king (8.18).

    • The eighteen titles of law (8.4–7) are listed for the first time in this text and became the standard classification of litigation (Chapter 12).

    • Fines are graded into three levels: 250 paṇas (the lowest “violence fine”), 500 (the middle) and 1,000 (the highest) (8.138).

    • The king is fined a thousand times what an ordinary man would pay for the same offence (8.336; Chapter 5).

    Manu’s account of the king (chapter 7) follows the pattern of the Arthaśāstra in compressed form. The king should keep seven or eight hereditary ministers, learned and tested, and a Brahmin of special learning as his chief counsellor; he should appoint an envoy who is learned, honest and skilled in reading gestures (7.54–68). He should live in a fort — a desert fort, an earth fort, a water fort, a forest fort, a fort of men or a mountain fort, the last being the best (7.70–71). He should organise the countryside in units of one, ten, twenty, a hundred and a thousand villages, each under a lord responsible to the next (7.115–120). He should deliberate in secret, on a mountain top or in a lonely place, excluding from his council idiots, the dumb, the blind and the deaf, animals, the very old, women, barbarians, the sick and the maimed (7.147–150). He should know the circle of kings around him — the conqueror, his enemy, the middle king and the neutral — and the six measures of policy (7.155–180), and wage war according to the rules of righteous battle (7.87–98; Chapter 7). His day is divided between the court, counsel, exercise, meals, the inspection of troops and the reports of spies (7.145–226). The chapter shows how thoroughly the Dharmaśāstra absorbed the science of polity, while framing it within the king’s duty to uphold dharma.

    The Manusmṛti’s legal sections are technically accomplished. They include rules on the examination and credibility of witnesses, on the oath and ordeals, on interest and pledges, on deposits, partnerships and sales, on boundaries and on inheritance, and they show a concern for orderly procedure and for proportion in punishment. They are achievements of legal thought within a system that is explicitly unequal, and their technical virtues cannot be detached from it.

    10.6 Action and Rebirth¶

    The last chapter of the Manusmṛti places the whole of its law within a theory of action (karman) and its consequences. Every action, mental, verbal or bodily, bears fruit, good or bad; from bad actions of the mind a man is reborn in a low station, from bad actions of speech as a bird or beast, from bad actions of the body as an immobile being (12.3–9). The text analyses human character in terms of the three qualities (guṇa) of goodness, passion and darkness, which determine the kind of rebirth one attains (12.24–52), and it lists the rebirths that await particular sinners — the killer of a Brahmin, the drinker of liquor, the thief of gold, the violator of the teacher’s bed — in the wombs of animals and of despised humans (12.54–72). It ends with the knowledge of the self, which leads to the highest state.

    This framework explains a feature of the text that modern readers often find puzzling: its readiness to assign to the next life consequences that a modern law would seek in this one, and its treatment of social position as the result of past deeds. A Śūdra’s birth, on this view, is the consequence of his past actions, and his faithful performance of service is the means to a better birth. Ambedkar singled out this doctrine as one of the ideological foundations of caste, because it made social inequality appear as cosmic justice (Ambedkar 1936). It also made law, penance and rebirth parts of a single moral order, in which the king’s punishment, the Brahmins’ penances and the workings of karma all served to maintain dharma.

    10.7 Universal Virtues¶

    The Manusmṛti also contains statements of virtues common to all. In the chapter on the renouncer it lists ten marks of dharma for twice-born men in all the āśramas:

    Manusmṛti 6.92

    dhṛtiḥ kṣamā damo ’steyaṃ śaucam indriyanigrahaḥ
    dhīr vidyā satyam akrodho daśakaṃ dharmalakṣaṇam
    “Resolve, forbearance, self-control, not stealing, purification, mastery of the senses, wisdom, learning, truthfulness and lack of anger: these are the ten marks of dharma.”

    In chapter 10 it states the dharma of all four varṇas in brief: “Abstention from injury, truthfulness, not stealing, purification and mastery of the senses” (10.63). These lists are often quoted as evidence of a universal ethic in the text, with good reason; but in the text they stand alongside, not above, the differentiated duties of varṇa and gender.

    10.8 Commentaries¶

    The Manusmṛti attracted more commentaries than any other smṛti. An earlier commentary by Bhāruci survives only in part; the oldest to survive complete is the Manubhāṣya of Medhātithi (9th–10th century, probably Kashmir), an independent and learned work that applies Mīmāṃsā reasoning and frequently notes alternative views and readings; Ganganatha Jha translated it in full (Jha 1920). Later commentators include Govindarāja (11th–12th century), Sarvajñanārāyaṇa, Rāghavānanda, Nandana and Kullūka Bhaṭṭa, whose Manvarthamuktāvalī (perhaps 13th–15th century) became the most widely used. Olivelle has pointed out that the text known to most modern readers is the vulgate transmitted with Kullūka’s commentary, because it was this version that Jones translated and that Indian printers reproduced (Olivelle 2005).

    10.9 Reception¶

    10.9.1 In India before the Modern Period¶

    The Manusmṛti’s authority in the classical tradition was very great. Later smṛtis cite it as the foremost authority; the Bṛhaspatismṛti is quoted as saying that a smṛti contrary to Manu is not approved. Its verses are quoted throughout the commentaries and digests, and inscriptions sometimes praise kings for ruling according to Manu. But its authority was the authority of a learned text within a scholastic tradition; there is no evidence that any pre-colonial state enforced it as a code, and many of its rules were modified or set aside by commentators and by custom (Chapter 9).

    10.9.2 Southeast Asia¶

    Law books in Burma (the dhammathat), and in the Mon and Thai traditions derived from them, traced their authority to a sage called Manu, and Old Javanese legal literature also invoked Manu. The content of these works was adapted to local custom and to Buddhist ethics, and they should not be read as translations of the Sanskrit text (Chapter 8 and Section 9.11).

    10.9.3 Colonial Law and European Reception¶

    Under the East India Company, the Manusmṛti was the first Dharmaśāstra text to be translated into English, by Sir William Jones (Institutes of Hindu Law: or, the Ordinances of Menu, Calcutta, 1794) (Jones 1794). Jones believed it to be among the oldest law codes in the world and to have been the law of the Hindus; his translation, together with the later rendering by Georg Bühler in the Sacred Books of the East (1886), gave it a place in European thought far beyond that of any other Indian legal text (Bühler 1886). British judges cited it as a source of “Hindu law”, and its prestige was thereby increased in India itself (Chapter 19). In Europe, it was read as evidence for the early history of law (Henry Maine) and, in a different spirit, by Friedrich Nietzsche, who in his late works praised the “Law of Manu” as a model of an aristocratic order superior to Christian morality — a reading based on a French popular version and coloured by his own polemics.

    10.9.4 Manu in the Colonial Courts¶

    Although Jones’s translation made the Manusmṛti the best-known Hindu law book in English, the colonial courts relied less on Manu than on the medieval commentaries and digests, especially the Mitākṣarā and the Dāyabhāga, which dealt with inheritance and family property in detail (Chapter 11). Manu was cited as a general authority, often in the translations of Jones and Bühler, on questions of marriage, adoption and caste. Its prestige among British administrators and Orientalists, and the assumption that it represented “the” ancient law of the Hindus, nonetheless gave it a symbolic status out of proportion to its practical role in the courts, and that status explains why it became the principal target of anti-caste critique.

    10.9.5 Critique: Phule, Ambedkar and After¶

    The most important modern responses to the Manusmṛti came from Indians who had suffered under the order it defended. Jotirao Phule (1827–1890), in Gulamgiri (“Slavery”, 1873), attacked the Brahmanical texts as instruments by which Brahmins had enslaved the Śūdras and Atiśūdras. E. V. Ramasamy (Periyar) made the critique of Brahmanical scriptures central to the Self-Respect Movement in the Tamil region.

    B. R. Ambedkar (1891–1956) engaged with the text both as an activist and as a scholar. In March 1927 he led thousands of Dalits to draw water from the Chavdar tank at Mahad, in the Konkan, asserting a right of access that the municipality had recognised on paper but caste Hindus denied in practice. When the tank was ritually “purified” by caste Hindus afterwards, Ambedkar convened a second conference at Mahad in December 1927, at which, on 25 December, a copy of the Manusmṛti was publicly burned. The act repudiated the text’s authority as a charter of untouchability, and 25 December is still observed by many Dalits as Manusmṛti Dahan Din. In Annihilation of Caste (1936), Ambedkar argued that caste could not be reformed while the authority of the śāstras that sanctioned it was accepted, and in Who Were the Shudras? (1946) he offered a historical account of how the Śūdras came to be the lowest varṇa (Ambedkar 1936, 1946; Zelliot 1992). As chairman of the Drafting Committee of the Constitution and as Law Minister, he later led the effort to replace Dharmaśāstra-based personal law with modern legislation (Chapter 20).

    10.9.6 Feminist and Historical Scholarship¶

    From the 1980s, feminist historians, above all Uma Chakravarti, examined the Manusmṛti as a source for the history of patriarchy and its links with caste; Chakravarti’s essay on “Brahmanical patriarchy” (1993) and her book Gendering Caste (2003) are the most influential statements (Chakravarti 1993, 2003). Historians such as R. S. Sharma used it as a source for the social history of the Śūdras (Sharma 1958). Olivelle’s edition has provided all sides with a reliable text and with an argument about its date and composition that makes it harder to evade its content by attributing unwelcome passages to interpolators (Olivelle 2005).

    10.9.7 In Contemporary India¶

    The Manusmṛti remains contested. Some Hindu traditionalists continue to regard it as a sacred text, often quoting its verses on honouring women and on universal virtues; Dalit and feminist activists regard it as a symbol of oppression. A statue of Manu installed in the premises of the Rajasthan High Court at Jaipur in 1989 has been the object of protests and litigation ever since. The Constitution’s guarantees of equality before the law (Article 14), its prohibition of discrimination on grounds of caste and sex (Article 15) and its abolition of untouchability (Article 17) are, in substance, the negation of the Manusmṛti’s legal order (Chapter 20).

    10.10 Assessment¶

    The Manusmṛti is a work of great intellectual ambition: a carefully constructed account of the whole of human life — creation, society, family, kingship, law, penance and liberation — from a Brahmanical point of view. Its legal sections are among the most important sources for the history of Indian law, and its literary skill and influence are beyond doubt. It is also, unmistakably, a charter of hierarchy, which assigns rights and penalties by birth and sex, places Brahmins above all others, condemns Śūdras to service and outcastes to exclusion, and subordinates women to men. A responsible reading must hold both facts together. It should neither sanitise the text by selective quotation or by imaginary interpolation, nor reduce the whole of Indian tradition to it: the Manusmṛti was one Brahmanical text among many, and Indian traditions — Buddhist, Jain, bhakti, Islamic, Sikh and modern — also produced powerful critiques of the order it describes.

    10.12 Summary¶

    • The Manusmṛti is a carefully structured Brahmanical treatise of about the second century CE, presented as the teaching of the first man, Manu. Olivelle has argued for its unity of composition; attempts to explain away its harsh provisions as late interpolations are not supported by his edition.

    • It covers the whole of dharma: creation, the sources of dharma, the student and householder, women, the renouncer, the king, law, mixed classes, penance and rebirth.

    • Its social order is explicitly hierarchical: Brahmins are lords of creation, Śūdras exist to serve, and groups such as the Caṇḍālas are excluded; legal penalties are graded by varṇa, with a reverse gradation for theft.

    • It honours women within the household while declaring them unfit for independence, opposes widow remarriage and gives wives no property of their own apart from strīdhana — a combination analysed by Uma Chakravarti as “Brahmanical patriarchy”.

    • It was the most authoritative smṛti for the commentators, the first Dharmaśāstra translated into English, and a model for law books in Southeast Asia. Jotirao Phule, B. R. Ambedkar — who burned it at Mahad in 1927 — and later Dalit and feminist scholars (Section 20.2) made it the central target of their critique of caste and patriarchy.

    Further Reading¶

    Olivelle (2005) is the critical edition and translation, with an essential introduction; Doniger and Smith (1991) and Bühler (1886) are earlier translations, and Jha (1920) translates Medhātithi’s commentary. On the text’s social vision and its critics see Ambedkar (1936), Ambedkar (1946), Sharma (1958), Chakravarti (1993), Chakravarti (2003) and Zelliot (1992).
