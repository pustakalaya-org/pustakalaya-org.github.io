    Chapter 12: Gandhi and Ambedkar: Non-violence, Caste and Justice¶

    “On the 26th of January 1950, we are going to enter into a life of contradictions. In politics we will have equality and in social and economic life we will have inequality.”
    — B. R. Ambedkar, speech to the Constituent Assembly, 25 November 1949

    12.1 Two Founders¶

    Mohandas Karamchand Gandhi (1869–1948) and Bhimrao Ramji Ambedkar (1891–1956) are the two most influential Indian political thinkers of the twentieth century. Both drew on Indian traditions to answer the questions of their age; both were also profoundly shaped by Western thought, Gandhi through Tolstoy, Ruskin and the New Testament, Ambedkar through John Dewey and liberal constitutionalism. They disagreed fundamentally about caste, about the village, about the authority of Hindu scripture and about the means of achieving justice, and their disagreement remains one of the central debates of Indian public life. This chapter presents each in turn and then compares them. To treat Ambedkar merely as a footnote to Gandhi, as many older accounts did, is to repeat the exclusion he spent his life fighting.

    12.2 Gandhi¶

    12.2.1 Life¶

    Gandhi was born in Porbandar, in Kathiawar, into a family of Vaiṣṇava Baniyas in a region where Jain influence was strong. He studied law in London (1888–91), where he read the Bhagavadgītā for the first time, in Edwin Arnold’s English version, with Theosophist friends, and encountered vegetarian and Christian circles. In South Africa (1893–1914), where he went as a lawyer, his experience of racial discrimination turned him into a political organiser. There he founded the Natal Indian Congress (1894), the Phoenix Settlement (1904) and Tolstoy Farm (1910), and developed the method he called satyāgraha in campaigns against discriminatory laws from 1906 onwards. Returning to India in 1915, he led local campaigns in Champaran (1917) and Kheda (1918), the nationwide Non-Cooperation Movement (1920–22), the Salt March (1930) and the Quit India Movement (1942). He opposed the partition of India, spent the months around independence trying to stop communal violence in Bengal and Delhi, and was assassinated on 30 January 1948 (see below) (Brown 1989; Guha 2018).

    12.2.2 Sources¶

    Gandhi wrote in his autobiography that the Gītā became his “dictionary of daily reference”. He read it, against most traditional commentators, as an allegory: the battle of Kurukṣetra was the struggle within the human heart, and the teaching of action without attachment to its fruits (anāsakti) was its essence. The Jain jeweller and poet Raychandbhai (Śrīmad Rājacandra), with whom he corresponded from South Africa, was, he wrote, one of the three modern people who had most influenced him. The other two were Tolstoy, whose The Kingdom of God Is Within You (1894) confirmed him in the conviction that evil must not be resisted by force, and John Ruskin, whose Unto This Last (1860), read on a train journey in 1904, convinced him that the life of manual labour was the life worth living and that the good of the individual is contained in the good of all. He paraphrased Ruskin in Gujarati under the title Sarvodaya, “the welfare of all”. He also admired Thoreau’s essay on civil disobedience and the Sermon on the Mount (Gandhi 1927).

    12.2.3 Hind Swaraj¶

    In November 1909, on a voyage from London to South Africa, Gandhi wrote in Gujarati, in about ten days, the short dialogue Hind Swaraj (“Indian Home Rule”). Its argument is that the enemy is not the British but “modern civilisation” — the civilisation of railways, lawyers, doctors and machinery, which multiplies wants and makes people slaves of their bodies — and that true self-rule (svarāj) is the rule of oneself, achieved by “soul-force” rather than “brute force”. Gandhi later moderated some of its positions, but never repudiated it. Anthony Parel’s edition is the standard English text (Gandhi 1997). The book’s critique of industrial civilisation has been admired by environmentalists and critics of modernity; it has also been criticised as romanticising a village life that was, for many Indians, a life of poverty and caste oppression — a criticism Ambedkar made with particular force.

    12.2.4 Satyāgraha¶

    Satyāgraha, “holding firmly to truth”, was Gandhi’s name for a method of resistance that was neither violent nor merely “passive”. Its principles can be summarised as follows. No one possesses the whole truth, so no one may impose his view on another by violence; the resister must therefore accept suffering rather than inflict it. The aim is not to defeat or humiliate the opponent but to convert him, by appealing to his conscience and by making the injustice visible. The means must be as pure as the end, since “the means may be likened to a seed, the end to a tree”. And the satyāgrahī must be disciplined — truthful, fearless, non-possessive and, in Gandhi’s view, celibate. Fasting was the extreme form of self-suffering, used to move opponents and, more often, followers (Parekh 1989).

    Gandhi’s ashrams were governed by a set of vows that expressed this discipline: truth, non-violence, chastity, control of the palate, non-stealing, non-possession, bread labour, fearlessness, equal respect for all religions, use of local goods (svadeśī) and the removal of untouchability.

    12.2.5 The constructive programme¶

    For Gandhi, resistance to injustice had to be accompanied by the reconstruction of society. His Constructive Programme (1941) listed its items: communal unity, removal of untouchability, prohibition of alcohol, hand-spun cloth (khādī) and village industries, village sanitation, “basic education” through productive work (nayī tālīm), adult education, the uplift of women, education in health, provincial languages and a national language, economic equality, and work among peasants, labourers, tribal people (ādivāsī), lepers and students (Gandhi 1941). His ideal political order was a federation of self-governing village republics. In 1946 he described it as “not a pyramid with the apex sustained by the bottom” but “an oceanic circle whose centre will be the individual”, ever ready to sacrifice for the village, the village for the circle of villages, and so on outwards. On property he proposed “trusteeship”: the rich should regard their wealth as held in trust for the community. A saying widely attributed to him, that the earth provides enough for everyone’s need but not for everyone’s greed, has become a slogan of the environmental movement.

    12.2.6 Religion and the communities¶

    Gandhi described himself as a Sanātanī Hindu, but his Hinduism was idiosyncratic: he rejected the authority of any text that conflicted with reason and morality, and he held that all the great religions were true but imperfect. His prayer meetings included readings from the Gītā, the Qurʾān, the Bible and other scriptures. He made Hindu–Muslim unity central to his politics, supporting the Khilafat agitation of Indian Muslims in 1919–22 and spending the last months of his life trying to quell communal violence, with fasts in Calcutta in 1947 and Delhi in January 1948. His inclusive Hinduism was rejected both by Muslim separatists, who saw it as a Hindu claim to speak for all, and by Hindu nationalists, who saw it as a betrayal.

    12.2.7 Conversion¶

    Gandhi’s pluralism had a firm limit: he opposed religious conversion. Since all religions were, in his view, true but imperfect, there was no reason to change one’s religion, and he regarded the work of Christian missionaries among the poor and the untouchables as an exploitation of their misery. He told missionaries that they should serve without seeking converts, and he resisted Ambedkar’s threat to lead the untouchables out of Hinduism. Indian Christians and Muslims pointed out that this position, however sincerely held, left them in an uncomfortable place: their faiths were accepted as true, but their freedom to share them was not. The Constitution guarantees the right to “profess, practise and propagate” religion (Article 25), and the question of conversion has remained politically contested, with several states passing laws to regulate it.

    12.2.8 Gandhi on caste¶

    Gandhi’s views on caste changed over his life. In the early 1920s he defended varṇāśrama as a natural division of hereditary occupations, compatible with equality of status, while condemning untouchability as a sin. He called untouchables harijan, “people of God”, a name many Dalits came to reject as patronising. He campaigned for temple entry and founded the Harijan Sevak Sangh (1932) and the weekly Harijan (1933). In his last years he went further, approving of inter-caste marriages and saying he would attend only weddings between “Harijans” and caste Hindus. But he never accepted that untouchability required the destruction of Hinduism’s scriptural authority, and he opposed the political separation of the Depressed Classes from the Hindu community. On these points he clashed with Ambedkar.

    12.2.9 Temple entry¶

    The campaign for the entry of untouchables into Hindu temples, and for their use of the roads around them, shows both the reach and the limits of Gandhian methods. At Vaikom in the princely state of Travancore, in 1924–25, a satyagraha was organised to open the roads near the temple to the lower castes, who were forbidden to use them. Gandhi lent his support, visited Vaikom and negotiated a compromise by which some roads were opened; the Tamil leader E. V. Ramasamy (Periyar), then still in the Congress, took part and was imprisoned. A later satyagraha at Guruvayur (1931–32) was led by K. Kelappan. In 1936 the Maharaja of Travancore issued a proclamation opening the state’s temples to all Hindus, and other states and provinces followed. Supporters of the movement saw it as proof that caste Hindus could be persuaded to change; Ambedkar, who had led the Kalaram campaign at Nashik, concluded by the mid-1930s that temple entry was a secondary goal and that the untouchables needed political power, education and economic independence more than access to the temples of a religion that degraded them.

    12.2.10 Women in the movement¶

    Gandhi’s campaigns drew women into public life on an unprecedented scale. Women picketed liquor shops and foreign-cloth shops, joined the Salt Satyagraha of 1930 — the poet Sarojini Naidu led the raid on the Dharasana salt works after Gandhi’s arrest — and went to jail in thousands. Gandhi regarded women as especially suited to non-violent resistance, because of their capacity for suffering and self-sacrifice. His views were nevertheless conservative in other respects: he idealised the self-sacrificing wife of the epics, opposed artificial contraception and placed great emphasis on celibacy. Feminist historians have noted both the opportunities his movement opened and the limits of the roles it offered.

    12.2.11 Gandhian economics¶

    Gandhi’s economic ideas, scattered through his journalism, were systematised by his associate J. C. Kumarappa (1892–1960), an economist trained at Syracuse and Columbia, who argued in Economy of Permanence (1945) for a decentralised economy of self-sufficient villages, based on handicrafts and local resources, in harmony with the cycles of nature. Gandhi’s doctrine of “trusteeship” held that the owners of wealth should regard themselves as trustees holding it for the benefit of society, and should be persuaded rather than compelled to do so. Independent India, under Nehru, chose state-led industrialisation instead, though it gave a place to khadi and village industries through a statutory commission. The Gandhian critique of industrial civilisation found a later echo in the West: the economist E. F. Schumacher, who had worked as an adviser in Burma and visited India, advocated “intermediate technology” and a “Buddhist economics” of sufficiency in Small Is Beautiful (1973), citing Gandhi among his inspirations (Schumacher 1973). Ambedkar, as noted below, took the opposite view, seeing industrialisation as the way to free Dalits from the village economy. The disagreement remains alive in Indian debates about development and the environment (Chapter 21).

    12.2.12 Partition and the last years¶

    The last years of Gandhi’s life were overshadowed by the communal violence that accompanied the end of British rule. After the riots in Calcutta in August 1946 and the attacks on Hindus in Noakhali in eastern Bengal later that year, Gandhi spent four months walking from village to village in Noakhali, attempting to restore confidence between Hindus and Muslims. He opposed partition to the end but did not resist the decision of the Congress leaders to accept it. On 15 August 1947, while Delhi celebrated independence, he was in Calcutta, where his presence and his fast in early September were credited with ending the killing in the city. In January 1948 he fasted again in Delhi for communal peace and the safety of Muslims in the capital; during the fast the government of India agreed to pay Pakistan its withheld share of the cash balances of undivided India, a decision for which his opponents blamed him. On 30 January 1948 he was shot dead at a prayer meeting in Delhi by Nathuram Godse (Chapter 9), who held him responsible for the concessions to Muslims and to Pakistan. Gandhi’s death was the culmination of a life spent insisting that Hindus and Muslims, and all the communities of India, belonged to a single nation, a conviction that his assassin rejected.

    12.3 Ambedkar¶

    12.3.1 Life¶

    Bhimrao Ramji Ambedkar was born in 1891 at Mhow, in central India, into a family of the Mahar caste, regarded as untouchable in Maharashtra; his father was a subedar in the army. As a schoolboy he experienced the humiliations of untouchability — being made to sit apart, denied water from the common tap. With scholarships from the Maharaja of Baroda he studied at Columbia University in New York, where he was taught by the philosopher John Dewey and the economist E. R. A. Seligman, and at the London School of Economics; he became a barrister at Gray’s Inn. Back in India he founded journals and organisations, led the Mahad satyagraha of 1927 for the right of untouchables to draw water from a public tank, publicly burned the Manusmṛti in the same year, and led a campaign for entry to the Kalaram temple at Nashik (1930). He represented the Depressed Classes at the Round Table Conferences in London (1930–32), founded the Independent Labour Party (1936) and later the Scheduled Castes Federation, and served as Labour Member of the Viceroy’s Executive Council (1942–46). In independent India he was the first Law Minister and chairman of the drafting committee of the Constitution. He resigned from the cabinet in 1951 when the Hindu Code Bill, which would have given Hindu women rights of inheritance and divorce, was shelved. On 14 October 1956 at Nagpur he converted to Buddhism together with several hundred thousand followers, and he died on 6 December 1956 (Zelliot 1992; Jaffrelot 2005).

    12.3.2 Ambedkar and Dewey¶

    Ambedkar’s thought was itself a synthesis, though not of the kind usually celebrated. At Columbia he studied with John Dewey, the leading American philosopher of pragmatism and democracy, and he acknowledged his debt to Dewey throughout his life. In Annihilation of Caste he quoted Dewey on the need for a society to select and preserve what is good in its past and discard what is harmful, and he defined democracy, in terms close to Dewey’s, as not merely a form of government but primarily a mode of associated living, of conjoint communicated experience. For Ambedkar, as for Dewey, the test of a social order was whether it permitted free communication and shared interests among all its members; caste failed the test because it divided society into isolated, graded groups. Scott Stroud has traced in detail how Ambedkar adapted Dewey’s pragmatism (Stroud 2023), and other scholars how he combined this pragmatist conception of democracy with his reading of the Buddha, and with the constitutional traditions of Britain and the United States, to produce a political philosophy grounded in Indian experience but addressed to universal questions (Jaffrelot 2005). His example shows that a synthesis of Indian and Western thought need not take the form of Neo-Vedānta; it could equally take the form of a democratic and egalitarian critique of tradition.

    12.3.3 Caste and the Hindu scriptures¶

    Ambedkar’s analysis of caste combined social science with textual criticism. In a paper written at Columbia in 1916, “Castes in India”, he argued that caste is fundamentally a system of endogamy, maintained by the control of women’s marriage. In Annihilation of Caste (1936), written as the presidential address for a conference of the Jat-Pat-Todak Mandal of Lahore — which cancelled the conference when it saw the text — he argued that caste was not merely a division of labour but a division of labourers, graded one above another; that it destroyed public spirit and made a Hindu society impossible; and that because it was sanctioned by the śāstras, it could not be abolished without destroying the authority of the śāstras themselves. “Caste is a notion; it is a state of the mind,” he wrote, and the mind could be changed only if people ceased to believe in the sanctity of the texts that taught it (Ambedkar 1936). In Who Were the Shudras? (1946) and The Untouchables (1948) he offered historical theories of the origin of the lowest classes, which later historians have debated but which opened new questions (Ambedkar 1946).

    12.3.4 The village¶

    In the Constituent Assembly, on 4 November 1948, Ambedkar replied to those who wished to make the village the basic unit of the Constitution: “What is the village but a sink of localism, a den of ignorance, narrow-mindedness and communalism?” He was glad that the draft Constitution had discarded the village and adopted the individual as its unit. The remark was directed at Gandhian ideals, and it expressed the experience of untouchables for whom the village was the place where caste was most strictly enforced.

    12.3.5 Constitutional democracy¶

    Ambedkar’s political philosophy rested on the three principles of liberty, equality and fraternity, which he said he had derived not from the French Revolution but from the teaching of the Buddha. He argued that political democracy could not last unless it rested on social democracy, a way of life that recognised these principles, and he warned in his final speech to the Constituent Assembly, quoted at the head of this chapter, that India was entering a life of contradictions in which political equality coexisted with social and economic inequality. He insisted on “constitutional morality” — respect for constitutional forms and the renunciation of unconstitutional methods once constitutional ones were available — and warned against hero-worship in politics. The Constitution abolished untouchability (Article 17), prohibited discrimination and provided for reserved seats in legislatures and positions in public employment for the Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes (Jaffrelot 2005).

    12.3.6 Religion, the state and Pakistan¶

    Ambedkar wrote on many subjects besides caste. In Thoughts on Pakistan (1940), later revised as Pakistan or the Partition of India (1945), he analysed the demand of the Muslim League with detachment, setting out the arguments of both sides; he concluded that if Muslims as a community were determined to have a separate state, it would be better to concede it than to keep an unwilling community within a single state, and he discussed the exchange of populations that this might require. The book was read by leaders of both the Congress and the League. In the Constituent Assembly he argued for a secular state, in the sense that the state should not establish or favour any religion, while defending the power of the state to reform religious practices that violated equality. His posthumously published Riddles in Hinduism, a sharply critical examination of Hindu doctrines and myths, provoked protests in Maharashtra when it was issued by the state government in 1987 as part of his collected works; the episode shows that Ambedkar remains a living and contested voice, not merely a revered founder.

    12.3.7 Economist and planner¶

    Ambedkar’s training was in economics as much as in law. His Columbia doctoral dissertation, later published as The Evolution of Provincial Finance in British India (1925), and his London thesis, The Problem of the Rupee (1923), dealt with public finance and currency. He wrote on the problems of small agricultural holdings and argued that the remedy for rural poverty lay in industrialisation, which would draw surplus labour from the land and break the economic dependence of untouchables on caste Hindu landowners. In a memorandum submitted to the Constituent Assembly in 1947, States and Minorities, he proposed that key industries and agricultural land be owned by the state, a form of state socialism to be written into the Constitution itself. The Assembly did not adopt the proposal, but the memorandum shows that his vision of justice extended from social equality to economic organisation.

    12.3.8 Buddhism¶

    In 1935, at Yeola, Ambedkar announced that although he had been born a Hindu he would not die one. He considered Sikhism, Islam and Christianity before choosing Buddhism, which he presented as a rational, egalitarian and Indian religion. His Buddhism, set out in The Buddha and His Dhamma (published posthumously in 1957), reinterpreted the tradition: it emphasised social liberation, treated the four noble truths and the doctrines of karma and rebirth in ways that differed from canonical teaching, and rejected the idea of a soul that transmigrates (Ambedkar 1957). At the conversion ceremony he administered twenty-two vows, which included explicit rejections of belief in Hindu gods, in the avatāras and in the idea that the Buddha was an incarnation of Viṣṇu. Ambedkarite Buddhism, often called Navayāna, is now the largest Buddhist community in India (Chapter 18) (Omvedt 2003).

    12.3.9 The Buddha or Karl Marx¶

    One of Ambedkar’s last essays, “Buddha or Karl Marx”, written shortly before his death and published posthumously, compared the two teachers whom he regarded as the most important for the modern world. He agreed with Marx that the purpose of philosophy is to reconstruct the world rather than to explain it, that private property and exploitation produce suffering, and that society must be reorganised to abolish them. He found these aims anticipated in the Buddha’s teaching on suffering and in the communal life of the Buddhist monastic order, which held no private property. But he rejected the Marxist means — violence and the dictatorship of the proletariat — on the ground that a society transformed by force must be held together by force, and he argued that the Buddha’s method of persuasion, moral training and voluntary change would produce a more lasting equality and would preserve liberty. The essay shows Ambedkar choosing Buddhism, in part, as an alternative to communism, at a time when many educated Dalits were attracted to the Left; and it shows that his religious choice was also a political philosophy.

    12.4 Religion in the Constituent Assembly¶

    The Constituent Assembly that sat from December 1946 to January 1950 had to decide how the new state would treat the religions of its citizens, and its debates show the ideas of this chapter at work. Three issues were especially contested.

    Propagation.

    Article 25 guarantees to all persons freedom of conscience and the right freely to “profess, practise and propagate” religion. Some members wished to omit the right to propagate, fearing missionary conversion; Christian members and others insisted on it as part of religious freedom; the word was retained. The question of conversion has nonetheless remained contentious, and several states have since passed laws regulating conversion by “force, fraud or inducement”.

    Reform and personal law.

    The Constitution empowers the state to regulate secular activities associated with religion, to provide for social welfare and reform, and to throw open Hindu religious institutions of a public character to all classes of Hindus. It directs the state to endeavour to secure a uniform civil code, but leaves personal laws in place. Ambedkar, as Law Minister, sought to reform Hindu personal law through the Hindu Code Bill; Nehru’s government later enacted its main provisions in separate acts in 1955–56, while Muslim, Christian and Parsi personal laws were left to their communities. The arrangement reflected both the reforming ambitions of the framers and their caution in dealing with minorities.

    Cow protection.

    Some members pressed for a ban on cow slaughter as a fundamental right. The compromise was a Directive Principle (Article 48) asking the state to organise agriculture and animal husbandry on modern scientific lines and to take steps for preserving breeds and prohibiting the slaughter of cows and calves and other milch and draught cattle. The wording placed the matter on economic rather than religious grounds, while leaving it to the states. The history of non-violence and diet traced in Chapter 7 thus entered the Constitution in a carefully secular form.

    The Assembly’s work shows that Indian secularism, as its framers designed it, was not a strict separation of religion and state on the American or French model but a principled distance: the state would protect all religions, reform some of their practices in the name of equality, and favour none. Whether that balance has held is a matter of continuing debate.

    12.5 The Debate¶

    12.5.1 The Poona Pact¶

    The sharpest confrontation came in 1932. The British Communal Award granted the Depressed Classes separate electorates, as Ambedkar had demanded. Gandhi, then in Yeravda jail, announced a fast unto death against the separation of the untouchables from the Hindu electorate. Under intense pressure, with Gandhi’s life at stake, Ambedkar agreed to the Poona Pact (24 September 1932), which abandoned separate electorates in favour of a larger number of seats reserved for the Depressed Classes within joint electorates. Gandhi regarded the pact as saving the unity of Hindu society; Ambedkar regarded it as having been extracted by coercion, and later wrote in What Congress and Gandhi Have Done to the Untouchables (1945) that the fast had been a form of political blackmail.

    12.5.2 Annihilation of Caste and Gandhi’s reply¶

    When Annihilation of Caste was published, Gandhi replied in Harijan (July 1936). He argued that the śāstras should be judged by the conduct of the saints rather than by texts of doubtful authority, that caste had nothing to do with religion, and that varṇa, rightly understood as a law of hereditary vocation without superiority, was not the same as caste. Ambedkar’s rejoinder, appended to later editions, asked how a Hindu could reject the scriptures while claiming to be a Hindu, and why the saints, however personally free of caste feeling, had never attacked the system (Ambedkar 1936).

    12.5.3 Comparison¶

    Table 12.1 summarises the main points of difference.

    Table 12.1. Gandhi and Ambedkar compared (simplified)
    Question Gandhi Ambedkar
    Untouchability a sin to be removed by a change of heart among caste Hindus a structure of domination to be abolished by law, organisation and the rejection of its scriptural basis
    Varṇa defensible as hereditary vocation without hierarchy (in his earlier and middle writings) inseparable from caste; to be rejected
    Scripture to be judged by reason and the conduct of the saints its authority must be destroyed, since it sanctions caste
    Political representation joint electorates to preserve Hindu unity separate representation for the Depressed Classes; accepted reserved seats under pressure
    Village the basis of a just, non-violent society “a sink of localism” and the stronghold of caste
    Modern state suspicious of centralised power and industry constitutional democracy, rule of law, state action for social justice
    Religion reformed Hinduism; equal respect for all religions Buddhism as a rational and egalitarian dhamma
    Means non-violent resistance, fasting, constructive work constitutional methods, education, organisation (“educate, agitate, organise”)

    Christophe Jaffrelot and Eleanor Zelliot, among others, have shown that the two men shared more than their quarrel suggests: both condemned untouchability, both sought a moral basis for politics, both drew on Indian religious traditions to articulate it (Jaffrelot 2005; Zelliot 1992). But they spoke from different places. Gandhi spoke as a caste Hindu asking his own community to repent; Ambedkar spoke as an untouchable demanding rights. The comparison is a lesson in why the identity of the speaker matters in any synthesis.

    12.6 After Gandhi: Nehru, Vinoba, Lohia and Jayaprakash Narayan¶

    The debate between Gandhi and Ambedkar was overlapped by a third vision, that of Jawaharlal Nehru. Nehru admired Gandhi and followed him politically, but he did not share his suspicion of industry, the modern state or science. As Prime Minister he pursued planned industrialisation, built dams that he called “the temples of modern India”, and founded institutes of science and technology. On this question he was closer to Ambedkar than to Gandhi, though Ambedkar left Nehru’s cabinet in 1951 over the delay in passing the Hindu Code Bill and over policy towards the Scheduled Castes. Gandhi’s economics survived in the village and cottage industries programmes and in later movements for decentralised and sustainable development.

    His most direct heir was Vinoba Bhave (1895–1982), whose “land gift” (bhūdān) campaign began in Telangana in 1951 and, over the following years, persuaded landowners across India to give land to the landless, later extending to the gift of whole villages (grāmdān). Vinoba presented the campaign as a non-violent alternative to the communist uprisings of the same region and period; much of the land given proved poor or was never distributed, but the movement was the largest attempt to apply Gandhi’s “trusteeship”. His description of the Emergency of 1975–77 as a “time of discipline” estranged many of his followers.

    Two socialists gave Gandhian ideas a different political form. Ram Manohar Lohia (1910–1967), who studied in Berlin and was a founder of the Congress Socialist Party in 1934, argued in Marx, Gandhi and Socialism (1963) for a socialism equidistant from capitalism and communism, built on small machines, a decentralised “four-pillar” state of village, district, province and centre, and civil disobedience as a permanent right. He made caste central to socialist politics, as few Marxists of his generation did, demanding preferential opportunity for the backward castes, Dalits, Adivasis, women and poor minorities, and he campaigned against the dominance of English in public life. His followers carried these ideas into the politics of the “backward classes” in north India. Jayaprakash Narayan (1902–1979), educated in the United States and a Marxist in his youth, joined Vinoba’s movement in 1954, and in A Plea for Reconstruction of Indian Polity (1959) proposed a “communitarian” democracy built upward from village assemblies, without the competition of parties. In 1974 he led the student movement in Bihar under the slogan of “total revolution” (sampūrṇa krānti); the agitation it inspired was one of the occasions of the Emergency, and its defeat of the Congress in 1977 restored parliamentary democracy. Thomas Pantham and Kenneth Deutsch’s collection places these thinkers within the wider history of modern Indian political thought (Pantham and Deutsch 1986).

    The relation between these visions — village and city, tradition and modern science, moral transformation and state action — remains one of the central arguments of Indian public life. The Hindu nationalist philosophy of Deendayal Upadhyaya, which drew on some of the same Gandhian themes of decentralisation and self-reliance for different ends, is discussed in Chapter 9.

    12.7 Legacies¶

    12.7.1 Gandhi in the world¶

    Gandhi’s methods were adopted, adapted and argued about across the world: by Khan Abdul Ghaffar Khan on the North-West Frontier and by Martin Luther King Jr. from the Montgomery bus boycott (1955–56) onwards (Chapter 7), and by many others. In South Africa the African National Congress used non-violent campaigns in the 1950s before turning to armed struggle in 1961; Nelson Mandela later described non-violence as a tactic rather than a principle for his movement. The American scholar Gene Sharp systematised the techniques of non-violent action, many drawn from Gandhi’s campaigns (Sharp 1973), and Erica Chenoweth and Maria Stephan, studying more than three hundred resistance campaigns between 1900 and 2006, found that non-violent campaigns had succeeded about twice as often as violent ones (Chenoweth and Stephan 2011). Gandhi’s critique of industrial civilisation influenced E. F. Schumacher’s Small Is Beautiful (1973) and the environmental movement, including the Chipko movement in India (Chapter 21).

    Gandhi has also been reinterpreted and criticised. Faisal Devji has read his thought as an engagement with violence rather than simply a rejection of it, and Shruti Kapila has placed him among Indian political thinkers who made the relation between fraternity and violence the central problem of the new nation (Devji 2012; Kapila 2021). Critics have pointed to his early statements about black Africans in South Africa, which Ashwin Desai and Goolam Vahed have documented (Desai and Vahed 2015); for his treatment of the women around him and his experiments in celibacy; for his opposition to modern technology; and, by Ambedkar and the Dalit movement, for his defence of varṇa. An honest account must include these criticisms alongside the admiration.

    12.7.2 Ambedkar in India and beyond¶

    Ambedkar’s reputation grew enormously after his death. The Dalit Panthers of Maharashtra (1972), inspired partly by the Black Panthers, took his name as their banner; Dalit literature, beginning in Marathi with writers such as Namdeo Dhasal and spreading to other languages, has made his thought the centre of a new literary tradition (Volume 8); and the political mobilisation of the Scheduled Castes, notably through the Bahujan Samaj Party founded by Kanshi Ram in 1984, has transformed Indian elections. His image, usually in a blue suit holding a copy of the Constitution, stands in villages across India. Internationally, he is increasingly read as a theorist of democracy and social justice, and his critique of caste has informed movements against discrimination by descent among South Asian diaspora communities.

    Gail Omvedt and others have placed Ambedkar at the culmination of a long line of Indian critics of Brahmanical tradition that begins with the Buddha (Omvedt 2003). Whether or not one accepts that reading of history, a volume concerned with synthesis must take his challenge seriously: that a tradition which claims to see the divine in all beings must be judged by how it has treated the least of them.
