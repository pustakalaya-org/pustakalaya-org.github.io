    Chapter 13: Tagore and His Contemporaries: Poets, Nationalists and Independent Thinkers¶

    Where the mind is without fear and the head is held high;
    Where knowledge is free;
    Where the world has not been broken up into fragments by narrow domestic walls …
    — Rabindranath Tagore, Gitanjali (1912), no. 35

    13.1 Introduction¶

    The thinkers discussed in the previous three chapters were religious teachers and political leaders who reinterpreted the Vedāntic and Gandhian inheritance. Modern India also produced poets, scholars, novelists and independent teachers who responded to the encounter with the West in other ways, and some who rejected the religious framing of the question altogether. This chapter begins with Rabindranath Tagore, whose universalism was the most sustained alternative to nationalism in colonial India, and sets beside him three writers who shaped how modern Indians imagined their past: the sceptical scholar Vidyāsāgar, the novelist Bankimchandra and Jawaharlal Nehru. It then turns to four figures who stood outside every school: Jiddu Krishnamurti, Ramana Maharshi, the psychoanalyst Girindrasekhar Bose and the radical humanist M. N. Roy. The thinkers who spoke for those excluded from the high tradition — anti-caste intellectuals, women writers and Adivasi leaders — are the subject of Chapter 14; the academic philosophers of Chapter 15; and the Muslim thinkers of the same years, among them Sayyid Ahmad Khan, Abul Kalam Azad and Muhammad Iqbal, of Chapter 17. Nalini Bhushan and Jay Garfield’s anthology and study of Indian philosophy in English in the colonial period are indispensable guides (Bhushan and Garfield 2011; Bhushan and Garfield 2017).

    13.2 Rabindranath Tagore¶

    13.2.1 Life and work¶

    Rabindranath Tagore (1861–1941) was born in Calcutta into the wealthy and cultivated family of Debendranath Tagore, the leader of the Brahmo Samaj (Chapter 9). He wrote poems, songs, novels, short stories, plays and essays in Bengali, composed more than two thousand songs which form the tradition of Rabīndrasaṅgīt, and in his late sixties began to paint. The English Gitanjali (1912), a selection of 103 prose poems translated by himself from several Bengali collections and introduced by W. B. Yeats, won him the Nobel Prize for Literature in 1913, the first awarded to an Asian (Tagore 1913). He was knighted in 1915 and renounced the knighthood in 1919 in protest at the massacre at Jallianwala Bagh in Amritsar. Two of his songs are the national anthems of India and Bangladesh. Krishna Dutta and Andrew Robinson’s biography gives a balanced account of his life (Dutta and Robinson 1995).

    13.2.2 Religion of Man¶

    Tagore’s religious thought grew from the Upaniṣadic theism of the Brahmo Samaj, from the Vaiṣṇava poetry of Bengal and from the songs of the Bāuls, wandering singers who sought the divine “man of the heart” (maner mānuṣ) within the human body and rejected caste and scripture. His poems speak of a “lord of life” (jīvandevatā), an intimate divine presence encountered in nature, love and creative work rather than in temple or doctrine. In his Hibbert Lectures at Oxford, published as The Religion of Man (1931), he argued that the divine is realised in humanity’s capacity for creative surplus, for love and for the ideal, and devoted a chapter to the Bāul singers (Tagore 1931). He drew on Indian sources, but his humanism also owed much to European Romanticism and to his conversations with Western friends.

    13.2.3 Critique of nationalism¶

    Tagore supported Indian freedom, wrote patriotic songs during the agitation against the partition of Bengal in 1905, and then withdrew from the movement when it turned to violence and to the boycott of everything foreign. His novel Ghare Baire (The Home and the World, 1916) dramatises the corruption of a household by the passions of nationalist politics. In lectures delivered in Japan and America in 1916–17 and published as Nationalism (1917), he distinguished the life of a people from “the Nation” as an organisation of power, which he regarded as the great menace of the modern age (Tagore 1917). The Japanese audiences who had welcomed him as an Asian sage were not pleased to hear their own imperial nationalism criticised. Ashis Nandy has argued that Tagore’s refusal of nationalism was a deliberate defence of a civilisational, plural self against the homogenising state, and that it has proved more prophetic than its contemporaries believed (Nandy 1994).

    13.2.4 Tagore and Gandhi¶

    Tagore and Gandhi admired each other, and their disagreements were conducted in public with remarkable courtesy. During the Non-Cooperation Movement Tagore published “The Call of Truth” (1921), questioning the burning of foreign cloth, the boycott of government schools and the demand that everyone spin. Gandhi replied in “The Great Sentinel”, defending non-cooperation as a discipline of self-purification. In “The Cult of the Charka” (1925) Tagore returned to the charge, arguing that mechanical spinning could not be the path to freedom of mind. In 1934, when Gandhi described the Bihar earthquake as a divine chastisement for the sin of untouchability, Tagore protested that such a view was unscientific and unworthy, since natural disasters do not discriminate between the guilty and the innocent. Sabyasachi Bhattacharya’s edition of their letters and debates shows two thinkers who shared the goal of an India free in spirit and differed about almost everything else (Bhattacharya 1997). Amartya Sen has called Tagore a defender of reason and freedom who resisted both colonial domination and the cultural nationalism that answered it (Sen 2005).

    13.2.5 Santiniketan and Visva-Bharati¶

    In 1901 Tagore founded a school at Santiniketan, the retreat his father had established in rural Bengal, where classes were held outdoors, the arts were central and examinations were not. In 1921 he founded Visva-Bharati, a university intended as a meeting-place of the cultures of the world, taking as its motto a line from the Vājasaneyi Saṃhitā of the White Yajurveda (32.8): yatra viśvaṃ bhavaty ekanīḍam, “where the whole world becomes one nest”. Scholars from Europe, China, Japan and elsewhere taught there; the Englishman Leonard Elmhirst helped him found the institute of rural reconstruction at Sriniketan, and later founded Dartington Hall in England on principles learned from him. The ideal was a synthesis in which each culture contributes its own gifts, not one in which all are dissolved into one. Tagore also travelled widely — to Japan, China, Southeast Asia, Latin America, the Soviet Union and Iran — and his conversation with Albert Einstein near Berlin in 1930, on whether truth exists independently of the human mind, was published and remains much discussed. His last public essay, “Crisis in Civilisation” (1941), written as the world war raged, condemned the betrayal of Western civilisation’s ideals in imperialism while affirming his faith in humanity.

    13.3 Vidyāsāgar: Reform Without Metaphysics¶

    Īśvaracandra Vidyāsāgar (1820–1891), principal of the Sanskrit College of Calcutta, is remembered for his campaign for widow remarriage (Chapter 5), for his work for the education of girls and for his Bengali primer, Varṇaparicay (1855), from which generations of Bengali children learned to read. He is less often remembered as a sceptic in philosophy. A master of Sanskrit learning, he nevertheless argued, in a letter of 1853 to the colonial Council of Education, that Vedānta and Sāṃkhya were false systems of philosophy which had to be taught at the Sanskrit College for the sake of scholarship, and that students should be given Western philosophy, such as Mill’s logic, to counteract their influence. Unlike Rammohun and the Brahmos, he made no attempt to found a reformed religion; unlike Vivekananda, he did not claim that Vedānta was the summit of human thought. He grounded his reforms in compassion, in common sense and, where it served his purpose, in the interpretation of the śāstras. Vidyāsāgar shows that nineteenth-century Indian reform included a secular and sceptical strand, often overshadowed in later accounts by the religious reformers, and that a scholar could know the tradition intimately while rejecting much of it.

    13.4 Bankimchandra and Nehru: Two Nationalist Readings of the Past¶

    Two other writers shaped the way modern Indians imagined their civilisation, and they make a useful contrast with Tagore. The Bengali novelist Bankimchandra Chattopadhyay (1838–1894) wrote the novel Ānandamaṭh (1882), which contains the hymn “Vande Mātaram” (“I bow to thee, Mother”) addressed to the motherland as goddess; the song became the anthem of the nationalist movement, and the novel’s depiction of Hindu ascetics fighting Muslim rulers has made it a contested text. In Kṛṣṇacaritra (1886) Bankim applied the methods of historical criticism to the Kṛṣṇa legends, rejecting the miraculous and the erotic as later accretions and presenting Kṛṣṇa as an ideal man and statesman. Partha Chatterjee has analysed how Bankim’s rationalised Hinduism combined a critique of Western power with an acceptance of Western forms of knowledge (Chatterjee 1986).

    Jawaharlal Nehru (1889–1964), who became India’s first Prime Minister, wrote The Discovery of India (1946) while in prison during the Second World War. A self-described agnostic educated at Harrow and Cambridge, he presented India as a civilisation that had absorbed many peoples and cultures — Vedic, Buddhist, Islamic, European — in successive layers, comparing it to an ancient palimpsest, and found in this capacity for synthesis the basis of a composite national identity in which no religious community had priority (Nehru 1946). Nehru’s “unity in diversity” became an official ideal of independent India, embodied in the secular Constitution. It was an inclusivism of a different kind from Vivekananda’s: civilisational rather than Vedāntic, and aimed at a modern, scientific state. Hindu nationalists criticised it for neglecting the Hindu majority, and Muslim separatists for disguising Hindu dominance; historians have praised its breadth and noted its romanticism.

    13.5 Jiddu Krishnamurti¶

    Jiddu Krishnamurti (1895–1986) occupies a unique place among modern Indian thinkers. Born in Andhra Pradesh, he was taken up as a boy by the leaders of the Theosophical Society, who proclaimed him the vehicle of a coming “World Teacher” and founded an organisation, the Order of the Star in the East, to prepare for his mission. In 1929 he dissolved the Order, declaring that “truth is a pathless land” which cannot be approached by any religion, sect or organisation, and spent the rest of his life travelling and speaking in India, Europe and America without disciples or doctrines. He taught that psychological freedom comes from “choiceless awareness” of the workings of one’s own mind, and that all authority in spiritual matters — of gurus, scriptures and traditions — is an obstacle. His dialogues with the physicist David Bohm are mentioned in Chapter 19, and the schools he founded in India and England pursued his ideal of education free from fear and competition. Krishnamurti rejected the labels “Hindu” and “Indian philosopher”, and in his teaching the modern Indian response to tradition took the form of a radical refusal of all tradition.

    13.6 Ramana Maharshi¶

    Venkataraman Iyer (1879–1950), known as Ramana Maharshi, left his home in Madurai at the age of sixteen after an overwhelming experience of the deathless self, and lived for the rest of his life at the sacred hill of Arunachala at Tiruvannamalai in Tamil Nadu. He wrote little, mostly short verse works in Tamil, and taught chiefly through silence and through answers to visitors’ questions. His central teaching was the practice of self-enquiry (ātma-vicāra): to ask persistently “Who am I?” and to trace the sense of “I” back to its source, where, he taught, the individual ego disappears and the self alone remains. His teaching is close to Advaita, but it gives little place to the study of scripture that Śaṅkara regarded as essential, and it made direct inquiry available to anyone, without distinction of caste, sex or learning. Ramana became known in the West through the British journalist Paul Brunton’s A Search in Secret India (1934), and he was visited by many seekers and writers; Somerset Maugham’s novel The Razor’s Edge (1944) drew on a visit. Ramana illustrates a different modern pattern from that of Vivekananda or Aurobindo: not a synthesis of traditions, nor a programme for society, but a traditional ascetic’s teaching that reached a global audience largely through the interest of others.

    13.7 Indian Psychoanalysis¶

    The encounter between Indian thought and modern psychology began early. The Calcutta physician Girindrasekhar Bose (1887–1953), who took a doctorate in psychology at the University of Calcutta, founded the Indian Psychoanalytical Society in 1922 and corresponded with Sigmund Freud from 1921 until the 1930s. Bose accepted much of psychoanalysis but proposed his own theory of “opposite wishes”, according to which every wish is accompanied by its repressed opposite, and he argued that the Oedipus complex did not have the central place in Indian patients that Freud gave it in European ones. He also wrote on the Yogasūtra and the Gītā in psychological terms. Freud’s replies were courteous but sceptical. The episode is an early example of the attempt, continued later in the work of Sudhir Kakar, to test a Western theory of the mind against Indian experience and to ask how far its claims are universal (Kakar 1982). Kakar’s studies of Indian childhood, family and religious healing have argued that psychoanalysis can illuminate Indian experience if its culture-bound assumptions are recognised, and that Indian materials can in turn enrich psychoanalytic theory. The exchange shows comparative thinking at its most useful: neither side’s theory is accepted as given, and each is tested against the other’s evidence.

    13.8 M. N. Roy and Radical Humanism¶

    Not every modern Indian thinker sought to renew religion. Manabendra Nath Roy (1887–1954), born Narendranath Bhattacharya in Bengal, began as a revolutionary nationalist, travelled to Mexico, where he helped to found the Mexican Communist Party in 1919, and became a leading figure in the Communist International, debating with Lenin on the colonial question in 1920. Expelled from the International in 1929, he returned to India, was imprisoned by the British, and gradually broke with Marxism. In the 1940s he developed a philosophy he called Radical or New Humanism, which placed the free individual at the centre of political life, rejected both nationalism and communist dictatorship, and called for a decentralised democracy of people’s committees. He was a severe critic of the idea that India had a special “spiritual” civilisation, which he regarded as an obstacle to the scientific and secular modernisation the country needed, and he wrote on the history of materialism in India and elsewhere. Roy’s influence was small in numbers but significant among Indian rationalists and civil-liberties activists, and it gave secular humanism a place in modern Indian thought.

    13.10 Assessment¶

    The writers of this chapter did not agree with one another, and their disagreements are their most valuable legacy. Tagore’s religion of man was a universalism without a nation; Bankim’s rationalised Kṛṣṇa and Nehru’s palimpsest were two different nationalisms built from the same past; Vidyāsāgar reformed custom without a metaphysics, Roy rejected the idea of a spiritual India altogether, and Krishnamurti refused every tradition, his own included. Ramana’s self-enquiry and Bose’s psychoanalysis, at opposite ends of the century’s interest in the mind, both made inner experience a field of inquiry rather than of inherited doctrine. None of these answers converges on Vedānta, and a synthesis that admitted only those that did would leave them all out. The voices that challenged the tradition from below, examined in the next chapter, widen the range further.
