    Chapter 10: Swami Vivekananda: Vedānta for the Modern World¶

    “Each soul is potentially divine. The goal is to manifest this Divinity within by controlling nature, external and internal. Do this either by work, or worship, or psychic control, or philosophy — by one, or more, or all of these — and be free.”
    — Swami Vivekananda, Raja Yoga (1896)

    10.1 Introduction¶

    Swami Vivekananda (1863–1902) was the first Indian religious teacher to win a large following in the West, and the most influential single formulator of modern Hindu self-understanding. His address to the World’s Parliament of Religions in Chicago in 1893, his books on the four yogas, his “Practical Vedānta” and the Ramakrishna Math and Mission that he founded shaped the way Hinduism was presented abroad and understood by educated Indians for a century. He is honoured in India as a patriot-saint; his birthday, 12 January, has been celebrated as National Youth Day since 1985. He has also been the subject of critical scholarship that questions some of his claims and examines the uses made of him. This chapter attempts to do justice to both.

    10.2 Life¶

    Narendranath Datta was born in Calcutta in 1863 into a prosperous Kāyastha family. He studied Western philosophy at the General Assembly’s Institution (later Scottish Church College), read Mill, Spencer and Hume, and attended meetings of the Brahmo Samaj. In 1881 he met Rāmakṛṣṇa at Dakshineswar (Chapter 9). After initial scepticism he became his most prominent disciple, and after Rāmakṛṣṇa’s death in 1886 he and a group of fellow disciples took monastic vows. From 1888 to 1893 he travelled as a wandering monk across India, an experience that brought him face to face with the poverty of the country and convinced him that religion without service to the poor was hollow.

    In 1893, with the support of the Raja of Khetri and of friends in Madras, he sailed to the United States to attend the World’s Parliament of Religions, held in Chicago in conjunction with the Columbian Exposition. His success there led to more than three years of lecturing in America and England. He founded the Vedanta Society of New York (1894), returned to India in 1897 to an enthusiastic reception, delivering a series of lectures from Colombo to Almora, and on 1 May 1897 founded the Ramakrishna Mission Association. The monastery at Belur, on the Ganges near Calcutta, was established in 1898. After a second journey to the West (1899–1900), he died at Belur on 4 July 1902, aged thirty-nine. His lectures, letters and conversations fill the nine volumes of his Complete Works (Vivekananda 1992).

    10.3 Chicago, 1893¶

    The World’s Parliament of Religions opened on 11 September 1893. Delegates included Christians of many denominations, Jews, Muslims, Buddhists (among them Anagārika Dharmapāla from Ceylon), the Jain lay scholar Virchand Gandhi, and Brahmo representatives such as P. C. Mozoomdar. Vivekananda’s brief opening address began with the words “Sisters and Brothers of America”, which drew prolonged applause. He continued:

    I thank you in the name of the most ancient order of monks in the world; I thank you in the name of the mother of religions; and I thank you in the name of millions and millions of Hindu people of all classes and sects. … I am proud to belong to a religion which has taught the world both tolerance and universal acceptance. We believe not only in universal toleration, but we accept all religions as true.

    He illustrated his theme with two quotations: a verse from the Śivamahimnaḥ Stotra comparing the different paths of human beings, straight or crooked, to streams that all reach the ocean, and the Bhagavadgītā’s declaration (4.11) that the Lord meets people by whatever path they approach him. In his longer “Paper on Hinduism” he presented Hinduism as a religion grounded in the Vedas, the eternal laws of spiritual life discovered by the sages, and as a hierarchy of spiritual stages in which even image-worship had its place as a step towards higher realisation.

    The success of the address owed much to its moment: a Western audience curious about “Oriental” religions and increasingly uncertain about its own, and a speaker of great presence who answered missionary caricatures with confidence. The claims it made — that Hinduism is the “mother of religions” and that it uniquely accepts all religions as true — were rhetorical as much as historical, and other delegates at the Parliament, Buddhist and Jain among them, did not regard their traditions as offspring of Hinduism.

    10.4 The Other Indians at Chicago¶

    Vivekananda was not the only Indian at the Parliament, and the presence of the others is a useful corrective to accounts that present him as India’s sole voice. Pratap Chandra Mozoomdar spoke for the Brahmo Samaj, as he had already done on an earlier American tour, and presented a liberal, theistic Hinduism close to Unitarian Christianity; he later became critical of Vivekananda. Anagārika Dharmapāla represented Theravāda Buddhism (Chapter 18); Virchand Gandhi spoke for the Jains; B. B. Nagarkar of Bombay for the Prarthana Samaj; and G. N. Chakravarti and Annie Besant appeared for the Theosophical Society. Christian speakers from India also took part. The Parliament itself, organised by liberal American Protestants, was designed to display the religions of the world as approaching, by different routes, the truths best expressed in Christianity, and several of its organisers expected the “Oriental” speakers to confirm this. Vivekananda’s success lay partly in reversing the expected hierarchy, presenting Vedānta as the most universal religion and Christianity as one of its partial expressions — an inclusivism that mirrored that of his hosts.

    10.5 The American Setting¶

    Vivekananda’s success in America is easier to understand when one remembers what his audiences already knew. The Transcendentalists, Emerson and Thoreau, had read the Bhagavadgītā and other Indian texts in translation earlier in the century (Chapter 8). Unitarian and liberal Protestant circles were open to the idea that truth could be found in many religions; the “New Thought” movement taught the power of mind over body; and the Theosophical Society had popularised the idea of an ancient Eastern wisdom (Chapter 8). Many of Vivekananda’s most devoted supporters were educated women, such as Sara Bull and Josephine MacLeod, who financed his work and later supported the Ramakrishna movement in India.

    His audiences also brought stereotypes, which he exploited and resisted. He was received as an exotic sage, and some of his success depended on his presence and eloquence as much as his arguments. He used his platform to attack the work of Christian missionaries in India: at the Parliament he told his hearers that what India needed was not religion, of which it had plenty, but bread, and that it was an insult to a starving people to offer them doctrine. Missionaries and some Indian Christians responded critically, and controversies about his statements followed him through his American years. His lectures, collected in the Complete Works, show him adapting his message to audiences in New York, London and Madras with considerable skill (Vivekananda 1992).

    10.6 Vivekananda on Other Religions¶

    Vivekananda’s attitudes to the other religions he encountered were generous but not neutral. He admired the Buddha intensely, calling him the greatest of men and the model of selfless work, and lectured on him in America and India. But he described Buddhism, in a speech at the Parliament, as the “fulfilment” of Hinduism and elsewhere as its “rebel child”, and attributed its disappearance from India partly to its own decadence and partly to its reabsorption into Hinduism. Dharmapāla, whom he met at Chicago, insisted on the contrary on the independence of the Buddha’s teaching (Chapter 18), and the question — reformer within Hinduism, or founder of a separate tradition? — still shapes how Buddhists in modern India, including the followers of Ambedkar, understand themselves. He admired Jesus and quoted the Gospels often, while denying that Christianity had any monopoly of truth. He praised Islam for practising the equality that Vedānta taught in theory, but he also repeated, in some lectures, the common nineteenth-century picture of Muslim rule in India as a period of oppression.

    These views were shaped by his framework. In a hierarchy in which the Vedānta is the most comprehensive religion, other religions could be honoured as true, and even as necessary for the peoples who held them, while remaining partial. The framework enabled him to speak respectfully of all religions to audiences used to missionary polemic; it also made it hard for him to hear them on their own terms. This is the inclusivism discussed in Chapter 1, in one of its most attractive forms.

    10.7 Practical Vedānta and Service¶

    Vivekananda’s most original contribution was his insistence that Advaita Vedānta had practical, social consequences. If every being is in essence Brahman, then the poor, the hungry and the outcaste are God in disguise, and to serve them is to worship God. His lectures on “Practical Vedānta” in London (1896) argued that the Vedānta should be brought “from the forest” into ordinary life, into the market-place and the home. He popularised the term daridranārāyaṇa, “God in the form of the poor”, later taken up by Gandhi. The Ramakrishna Mission’s motto, “for one’s own liberation and for the welfare of the world” (ātmano mokṣārthaṃ jagaddhitāya ca), expresses the combination. The Mission’s hospitals, schools, colleges and famine and disaster relief, begun in his lifetime, made it one of the best-known service organisations in India.

    Hacker and others have pointed out that classical Advaita did not draw this conclusion: for Śaṅkara the liberated sage has transcended action, and service is at most a preparation for knowledge (Hacker 1995). Vivekananda’s ethic owed as much to the Christian and humanitarian ideals of nineteenth-century Bengal as to Śaṅkara. That does not make it less valuable; it does make it new.

    10.8 The Four Yogas¶

    Vivekananda organised religious practice under four “yogas”, each suited to a different temperament: karmayoga, the path of selfless work; bhaktiyoga, the path of devotion; jñānayoga, the path of philosophical discrimination; and rājayoga, the path of meditation, which he identified with Patañjali’s Yogasūtra. He published books on each, based on his lectures in America and England, the most influential being Raja Yoga (1896), which included a translation and commentary on the Yogasūtra (Vivekananda 1992).

    The Bhagavadgītā had already discussed action, knowledge and devotion as disciplines (yoga), but Vivekananda’s fourfold scheme, with the paths presented as complementary and ideally combined, was his own systematisation. His Raja Yoga was particularly consequential. Elizabeth De Michelis has shown that it presented Patañjali’s yoga as a “science” of the mind, verifiable by experiment, in terms shaped by Western esotericism, Unitarianism and the “New Thought” movement of his American audiences, and that it became the founding text of what she calls “modern yoga” (De Michelis 2004).

    The book departs from its classical source in ways worth noting. Patañjali’s Yogasūtra is a dualist work in the Sāṃkhya tradition, whose goal is the isolation (kaivalya) of the conscious self from matter, not union with an absolute; Vivekananda read it through his Vedāntic universalism, so that the goal of yoga became the realisation of the divinity of the soul. He gave great prominence to prāṇa, which he explained in the language of nineteenth-century physics as a universal energy, whereas the classical commentators Vyāsa and Vācaspati Miśra had located yoga within a scheme of scriptural authority, ritual purity and renunciation. The postures (āsana) that dominate yoga today play almost no part in his account; for him, as for Patañjali, āsana meant a steady seat for meditation. None of this makes Raja Yoga a fraud — every interpreter reads an ancient text in the light of his own concerns, and this reading proved enormously fruitful — but it means that the “yoga” Western readers met through him was already a modern synthesis. Its later history is taken up in Chapter 20.

    10.9 Religion as Experience and as Science¶

    Vivekananda argued that religion, like science, rests on experience: the sages had experienced the truths they taught, and anyone who followed their methods could verify those truths for himself. Religion should therefore be “scientific”, and the Vedānta, being based on experience rather than on dogma or on historical events, was the religion best suited to the scientific age. He drew analogies between Vedāntic monism and the physics of his time, especially the conservation of energy, and taught that behind all forces there is one cosmic energy (prāṇa) and behind all matter one universal substance (ākāśa). A letter of 1896 records his meeting with the inventor Nikola Tesla, who, he wrote, was interested in these ideas.

    Physics has not developed in the direction Vivekananda expected, and his specific analogies are now of historical interest only. The more lasting issue is philosophical. Rambachan has shown that Vivekananda’s appeal to experience as the final authority reverses Śaṅkara’s view that the Upaniṣads are the means of knowing Brahman, and that the scriptures are for Vivekananda secondary records of the sages’ experience (Rambachan 1994). The appeal to experience made the Vedānta attractive to modern audiences and compatible with the idea of a universal mysticism; it also exposed it to the objection, discussed in Chapter 9, that there is no experience independent of the concepts of a tradition.

    10.10 The Harmony of Religions¶

    Vivekananda’s doctrine of the harmony of religions drew on Rāmakṛṣṇa’s example and on Advaita. Because the ultimate reality is infinite, no single religion can exhaust it; each religion expresses some aspect of the truth suited to a particular people and temperament; therefore all should be accepted, not merely tolerated. At the same time, he held that the Vedānta, which recognised the impersonal Absolute behind all personal gods, was the most comprehensive religion and could provide the philosophical basis for a universal religion.

    He did not think that India’s own communities should simply merge. In a letter of 1898 to Mohammed Sarfaraz Husain of Naini Tal, he wrote that mankind should be led to the place where there is “neither the Vedas, nor the Bible, nor the Koran”, but that this should be done by harmonising them, and that the hope of India lay in “Vedanta brain and Islam body” — the philosophy of Vedānta joined to the practical equality of Islam (Vivekananda 1992). The letter shows both the generosity of his vision and its characteristic hierarchy, in which the brain is Vedāntic.

    10.11 Society, Caste and Nation¶

    Vivekananda was a fierce critic of the social practices of his day. He mocked the orthodox preoccupation with ritual purity as “don’t-touchism”, condemned the oppression of the lower castes and the neglect of the poor, and urged the education of women and of the masses. He supported the girls’ school that his disciple Sister Nivedita opened in Calcutta (see below). On caste he was less radical than some of his admirers suppose: he sometimes defended caste as originally a natural division of labour corrupted over time, and he proposed raising the lower castes to the cultural level of the Brahmins rather than abolishing caste distinctions altogether.

    His call to national self-respect — “Arise, awake, and stop not till the goal is reached”, adapted from the Kaṭha Upaniṣad (1.3.14) — inspired a generation of nationalists, including the revolutionaries of Bengal, Aurobindo and Subhas Chandra Bose. Tapan Raychaudhuri has shown that his attitude to the West combined admiration for its energy, organisation and science with contempt for its materialism and resentment of its imperialism (Raychaudhuri 1988). His image has since been claimed by many political movements, including Hindu nationalist organisations that emphasise his patriotism and his defence of Hinduism while giving less weight to his universalism and his social criticism.

    10.12 The Bengali Writings¶

    Vivekananda’s English lectures are well known; his Bengali writings, addressed to his own countrymen, are less so outside Bengal, and they show a sharper social analysis. In Bartamān Bhārat (“Modern India”, 1899) he offered a theory of Indian history as a succession of dominant classes: first the priests, whose power rested on knowledge and ritual; then the warriors, whose power rested on arms; then, under British rule, the merchants, whose power rested on wealth. Each had risen by drawing on the strength of the labouring masses and had then exploited them; he foresaw a time when the Śūdras, the labouring people, would themselves rise to power, and he called on educated Indians to identify with them. In Prācya o Pāścātya (“East and West”), he compared Indian and European customs, food, dress and ideals with humour and some acerbity, criticising both. These writings reveal a thinker who could combine his Vedāntic universalism with a materialist sense of social power, and they help to explain why Indian socialists as well as religious nationalists have claimed him (see below).

    10.13 Education¶

    Vivekananda wrote no treatise on education, but his scattered remarks were later collected and have been very influential. He defined education as “the manifestation of the perfection already in man” and contrasted the “man-making” education he wanted with the clerk-making education of colonial schools. In a lecture he said that education is not information put into the brain that “runs riot there, undigested, all your life”, and that a person who has assimilated five ideas and made them his life and character has more education than one who has learned a whole library by heart. He stressed self-confidence (śraddhā), concentration, physical strength and service, and favoured teaching in the mother tongue. His ideas are often cited in support of India’s National Education Policy of 2020, discussed in Chapter 22.

    10.14 Vivekananda and the Orientalists¶

    Vivekananda’s relation to European scholarship was close and ambivalent. In 1896 he visited Max Müller at Oxford and was moved by the old scholar’s devotion to India; Müller, for his part, published an essay on Rāmakṛṣṇa and in 1898 a book, Rāmakrishna: His Life and Sayings, based partly on material supplied by Vivekananda’s circle. Vivekananda praised Müller and Paul Deussen, whom he also visited in Germany, as true friends of India who had understood the Vedānta better than many Indians. At the same time he rejected the Orientalists’ historical criticism of the Veda and their theories of Indian history, and he insisted that the authentic interpretation of Indian religion belonged to those who lived it. His career illustrates the point made in Chapter 8: modern Hindu self-understanding was shaped in dialogue with European scholarship, appropriating its admiration while resisting its authority.

    10.15 Women, Disciples and Sister Nivedita¶

    Vivekananda’s work in the West drew a circle of disciples and supporters, many of them women, who played a large part in establishing his movement. Sara Bull, the widow of the Norwegian violinist Ole Bull, and Josephine MacLeod became lifelong supporters; Josiah John Goodwin, an English stenographer, recorded many of the lectures that survive. The most important of these disciples was Margaret Noble (1867–1911), an Irish schoolteacher who met Vivekananda in London in 1895, came to India in 1898 and took the name Nivedita (“dedicated”). In November 1898 she opened a school for girls in the Bagbazar quarter of Calcutta, and she spent the rest of her life in India, writing on Indian society and religion (The Web of Indian Life, 1904), supporting the scientific work of Jagadish Chandra Bose (Chapter 19), encouraging the artists of the Bengal School (Chapter 21) and moving, after Vivekananda’s death, into open sympathy with the nationalist movement, which led her to separate formally from the Ramakrishna Order.

    Vivekananda’s views on women combined reform and conservatism in ways typical of his period. He insisted that the condition of Indian women was one of the causes of India’s decline, called for their education, and envisaged a monastic order for women; the Sri Sarada Math, named after Rāmakṛṣṇa’s wife Sāradā Devī, was eventually established in 1954. At the same time he idealised the self-sacrificing Hindu wife and mother, defended many existing customs against missionary criticism, and held that Indian women must solve their own problems in their own way, without foreign interference. Historians of gender in colonial India have placed him within the wider nationalist reconstruction of the “Indian woman” as the guardian of spiritual tradition (Chakravarti 2003).

    10.16 Uses of Vivekananda¶

    Few modern Indians have been claimed by so many different movements. Bengali revolutionaries of the early twentieth century read his call for strength and fearlessness as a summons to political action; Subhas Chandra Bose acknowledged him as a formative influence. Socialists and secular nationalists admired his concern for the poor and his attack on priestcraft; Nehru wrote of him with respect in The Discovery of India (Nehru 1946). Hindu nationalist organisations have made him a central figure: the Vivekananda Rock Memorial at Kanyakumari, inaugurated in 1970, was built by a committee led by Eknath Ranade, a senior worker of the Rashtriya Swayamsevak Sangh, and his image is widely used in their publications. Scholars have noted that these uses select different parts of a large and sometimes inconsistent body of speeches and letters — the universalist and the nationalist, the defender of Hinduism and its severe critic. A careful reading of the Complete Works does not support any single appropriation; it shows a man of strong and changing views, speaking to different audiences, whose thought cannot be reduced to a slogan.

    10.17 After Vivekananda: Indian Teachers in the West¶

    Vivekananda opened a path that many Indian teachers followed, with very different teachings. Paramahansa Yogananda (1893–1952) arrived in Boston in 1920 for a congress of religious liberals, stayed in America, founded the Self-Realization Fellowship in Los Angeles and taught a technique he called Kriya Yoga; his Autobiography of a Yogi (1946) became one of the most widely read books on Indian spirituality. A. C. Bhaktivedanta Swami Prabhupāda (1896–1977) founded the International Society for Krishna Consciousness in New York in 1966, bringing the devotional Gauḍīya Vaiṣṇavism of Bengal — a tradition that explicitly rejects Advaita — to young Westerners. Maharishi Mahesh Yogi (c. 1917–2008) taught Transcendental Meditation, a simple mantra technique, to millions from the late 1950s, and attracted the Beatles to his ashram at Rishikesh in 1968. Swami Chinmayananda (1916–1993) founded the Chinmaya Mission in 1953 to teach Advaita Vedānta in English to the urban middle class in India and the diaspora.

    These movements differ from one another in doctrine as much as any of the classical schools did: some are non-dualist, some devotional and theistic, some centred on technique. They share a modern form — an international organisation, publications in English, a teacher presented as a guide for people of any background — that owes much to Vivekananda’s example. Several of them also produced scandals and controversies over authority, money and the treatment of followers, as new religious movements in every tradition have done. Their history is part of the global life of Indian religions, and it shows that “Hinduism abroad” was never a single message.

    10.18 Who Is a Hindu? The Mission and the Courts¶

    The universalism of the Ramakrishna movement produced a curious legal episode, which illustrates the difficulty of defining religious identity in modern India. In the 1980s the Ramakrishna Mission, seeking the protection given by the Constitution to educational institutions run by religious minorities, argued before the courts that its followers constituted a distinct religion, “Ramakrishnaism”, rather than a Hindu sect, since they accepted all religions as true. The Calcutta High Court accepted the argument, but in 1995 the Supreme Court reversed the decision and held that the Mission was a Hindu religious denomination. The case shows how an inclusivist claim — that one’s own tradition embraces all others — can turn into a claim to be outside every particular tradition, and how the courts, like the census, are compelled to draw boundaries that the traditions themselves leave vague.

    10.19 Influence and Assessment¶

    Vivekananda’s influence in the West was considerable but specific. The Vedanta Societies remained small, but their members and associates included writers such as Christopher Isherwood and Aldous Huxley, whose Perennial Philosophy drew on Vedāntic sources (Chapter 9). William James quoted Vivekananda’s lectures in The Varieties of Religious Experience (1902). Through Raja Yoga he gave the West its first widely read presentation of yoga as a practical discipline.

    In India his influence was immense and continues. Admirers see in him the man who restored Hindu self-confidence, joined spirituality to social service and gave India a voice in the world. Critics have pointed out the historical claims that do not stand up (Hinduism as “the mother of religions”, Buddhism as a branch of Hinduism), the departure from classical Advaita that his “experience” and “service” represent, and the ease with which his rhetoric of Hindu pride could be used for exclusive nationalism. Both views can be held at once. Vivekananda was a creative reformer who used the resources of his tradition to answer the questions of his time; his synthesis is best understood, and best honoured, as one argued position among others, not as the final truth about Indian religion.
