    Chapter 11: Sri Aurobindo: Nationalism, Evolution and Integral Yoga¶

    “In the right view both of life and of Yoga all life is either consciously or subconsciously a Yoga.”
    — Sri Aurobindo, The Synthesis of Yoga, Introduction

    11.1 Introduction¶

    Aurobindo Ghose (1872–1950), known after his withdrawal from politics as Sri Aurobindo, was successively a classically educated product of Victorian England, a radical nationalist leader, a political prisoner and, for the last forty years of his life, a yogi and philosopher in the French enclave of Pondicherry. His major works — The Life Divine, The Synthesis of Yoga, The Human Cycle, The Ideal of Human Unity, The Secret of the Veda and the epic poem Savitri — constitute the most ambitious attempt of modern India to reinterpret the Vedic and Vedāntic tradition as a philosophy of evolution and world-affirmation. This chapter outlines his life, his thought and his legacy, and considers how it stands as a “synthesis”. Peter Heehs’s biography is the fullest critical account (Heehs 2008).

    11.2 Life¶

    Aurobindo was sent to England at the age of seven by his anglicised father, with instructions that he should have no contact with Indian culture. He studied classics at St Paul’s School, London, and at King’s College, Cambridge, passed the examination for the Indian Civil Service but failed to present himself for the riding test and was not appointed. He returned to India in 1893 to serve the Maharaja of Baroda, where he learned Sanskrit, Bengali and Marathi and began reading the Indian classics. From 1906 he was in Bengal, as principal of the new Bengal National College and as a leading voice of the “extremist” wing of the Indian National Congress, writing in the newspaper Bande Mataram. He advocated complete independence and the boycott of British goods, and in his pamphlet The Doctrine of Passive Resistance (1907) he argued for organised non-cooperation while refusing to rule out other means.

    In 1908 he was arrested in connection with the Alipore bomb case, in which his younger brother Barindra was a leading conspirator. He spent a year in jail before being acquitted, and later described spiritual experiences in prison that changed the direction of his life. In 1910, to avoid further arrest, he moved secretly to Pondicherry, then French territory, where he remained until his death in 1950. From 1914 to 1921 he published the monthly Arya, in which most of his major works first appeared serially. Mirra Alfassa (1878–1973), born in Paris to Jewish parents from Turkey and Egypt, whom he first met in 1914 and who settled in Pondicherry in 1920, became his closest collaborator, known as “the Mother”. After 1926 Aurobindo withdrew into seclusion and the Mother organised the Sri Aurobindo Ashram.

    11.3 Religious Nationalism¶

    Aurobindo’s early nationalism was explicitly religious. In the pamphlet Bhawani Mandir (1905) he proposed a temple and an order of renouncers dedicated to the Mother-goddess as the embodiment of India, and described the nation not as a piece of earth or a figure of speech but as a mighty power (śakti) made up of the energies of its millions of people, as the goddess Durgā had been formed from the energies of the gods. In a speech at Uttarpara in 1909, shortly after his release from prison, he declared that “it is the Sanatan Dharma which for us is nationalism”, and described India’s mission as the preservation and spread of that eternal religion. These texts made him a hero of later Hindu nationalism. They also show a problem that recurs throughout this volume: a nationalism defined by the religion of the majority could not easily include India’s Muslims, Sikhs and Christians on equal terms. In his later writings, especially The Ideal of Human Unity, Aurobindo moved towards a vision of world union in which nations would be stages in the evolution of humanity, and in 1947 he welcomed independence while lamenting partition.

    11.4 The Life Divine¶

    The Life Divine, serialised in the Arya (1914–1919) and revised as a book in 1939–40, is Aurobindo’s principal philosophical work (Aurobindo 1970a). Its starting point is a rejection of what he calls two “negations”. The materialist negation denies the reality of spirit; the ascetic negation, of which he took Śaṅkara’s Advaita and Buddhism to be the extreme forms, denies the reality or value of the world. Both, he argues, are one-sided. Reality is saccidānanda — existence, consciousness and bliss — which has “involved” itself in matter, so that matter is a form of concealed consciousness; evolution is the progressive unfolding of what has been involved, from matter to life and from life to mind. Mind is not the last term. Above it lie a series of higher levels of consciousness — higher mind, illumined mind, intuition and overmind — culminating in “supermind” (vijñāna), a truth-consciousness in which the One knows itself as the Many without division. The next stage of evolution, Aurobindo held, would be the emergence of a supramental consciousness on earth and the transformation of human nature, and eventually of the body itself.

    Aurobindo reinterpreted māyā: not illusion, but the creative power of the divine to measure out forms, a meaning he found in the Ṛgveda. Against Śaṅkara he held that the world is real as a manifestation of Brahman, a position closer to the “real transformation” (pariṇāma) views of earlier Vedāntins than to Advaita’s “appearance” (vivarta). His system is thus a Vedānta, but an evolutionary and world-affirming one, consciously addressed to the questions raised by Darwin, Bergson and Nietzsche.

    11.5 Integral Yoga¶

    The Synthesis of Yoga (serialised 1914–1921) set out a “yoga” corresponding to this metaphysics (Aurobindo 1970b). Aurobindo surveyed the paths of works, knowledge and devotion described in the Gītā, the rājayoga of Patañjali, haṭhayoga and tantra, and argued that each takes up one part of human nature. An “integral” yoga would take up all of them, and would aim not at the individual’s escape from the world but at the transformation of the whole being — body, life, mind and soul — and, through it, of terrestrial life. Its means are aspiration, the rejection of the movements of the lower nature, and surrender to the Divine Mother; its stages, in his later formulation, are a “psychic” transformation, in which the inmost soul (the “psychic being”) comes forward, a “spiritual” transformation, in which the higher levels of consciousness descend, and a “supramental” transformation. Aurobindo was sober about the dangers of the path: his letters warn repeatedly against mistaking vivid inner experiences in what he called the “intermediate zone” for genuine realisation.

    11.6 The Veda, Society and Poetry¶

    The Veda.

    In The Secret of the Veda, also first published in the Arya, Aurobindo argued that the Ṛgvedic hymns had an inner, psychological meaning: Agni was the divine will, Soma the delight of existence, the cows of the Aṅgirases the rays of illumination. The interpretation is ingenious and has been influential among his followers, but it has not been accepted by Vedic scholars, who read the hymns in the context of the ritual and society that produced them (Volume 1).

    Society and politics.

    The Human Cycle interprets social history as a movement from symbolic and conventional ages through an age of individualism and reason towards a “subjective” and finally spiritual age; The Ideal of Human Unity, written during the First World War, foresees a world-union of free nations. Both drew on European social thought, including Karl Lamprecht’s theory of historical stages, which Aurobindo acknowledged.

    The Gītā.

    In Essays on the Gita, also serialised in the Arya, Aurobindo read the Bhagavadgītā as a synthesis of the paths of works, knowledge and devotion, culminating in the surrender of the whole being to the Divine and in action as the Divine’s instrument. He rejected both Śaṅkara’s view that the Gītā ultimately subordinates action to knowledge and renunciation, and the purely ethical reading of those who saw in it a gospel of duty; and he treated the setting on the battlefield as part of the teaching, not as an allegory to be explained away, since the Gītā accepts that the world includes struggle and destruction. His reading thus differed both from Tilak’s activism and from Gandhi’s non-violent allegory (Chapter 6).

    Translations.

    Aurobindo was also a translator. In his Baroda years he translated Kālidāsa’s play Vikramorvaśīya into English verse as The Hero and the Nymph, and passages of the epics; later he translated and commented on the Īśa, Kena and other Upaniṣads, and on hymns of the Ṛgveda in the light of his psychological interpretation. His translations are free and literary rather than philological, and they show the same tendency as his commentaries to read the texts as expressions of his own spiritual vision. They also show a poet’s engagement with Sanskrit literature that was unusual among the religious teachers of his generation.

    Savitri.

    Aurobindo’s longest work is Savitri: A Legend and a Symbol, an epic in English blank verse of some 24,000 lines on which he worked from about 1916 until his death. It retells the story from the Mahābhārata of the princess Sāvitrī who wins back her husband Satyavān from Death, and makes it a symbol of the soul’s victory over death and ignorance. His followers regard it as a revelation; literary critics have been more divided, some admiring its visionary sweep and others finding its diction archaic.

    11.7 The Arya and the Defence of Indian Culture¶

    Almost all of Aurobindo’s major prose works first appeared in the Arya, a monthly “review of pure philosophy” that he published from Pondicherry between August 1914 and January 1921, initially with Paul and Mirra Richard. Each issue carried instalments of several works at once — The Life Divine, The Synthesis of Yoga, The Secret of the Veda, Essays on the Gita, the essays later collected as The Human Cycle and The Ideal of Human Unity — so that the whole system was composed in parallel, over six and a half years, in a journal with a few hundred subscribers.

    Among the last series in the Arya was a response to the English drama critic William Archer, whose India and the Future (1917) had dismissed Indian civilisation as barbarous and its religion and art as obstacles to progress. Aurobindo’s reply, later published as The Foundations of Indian Culture, defended Indian religion, art, literature and polity, arguing that each must be judged by its own aims rather than by the standards of nineteenth-century European rationalism. The work belongs to a genre familiar from Chapter 8: the Indian answer to European disparagement. It is eloquent and often penetrating, especially on Indian art, where Aurobindo drew on Havell and Coomaraswamy; it also shares the genre’s tendency to set an idealised “spiritual” India against a “materialist” West, a contrast that later scholars on both sides have questioned.

    11.8 Aurobindo and European Philosophy¶

    Aurobindo was educated in England, read Greek and Latin fluently, and knew European literature and philosophy well. His system invites comparison with three European thinkers in particular.

    Hegel.

    Like Hegel, Aurobindo describes reality as Spirit that realises itself through a historical and cosmic process, and like Hegel he treats earlier philosophies as partial truths taken up into a larger whole. The difference is that Aurobindo’s Spirit is known in the last resort not through dialectical reason but through spiritual experience, and that his process is not complete in the present: the supramental stage lies ahead.

    Bergson.

    Henri Bergson’s Creative Evolution (1907) presented evolution as the work of a vital impulse (élan vital) and contrasted analytic intellect with intuition. Aurobindo used similar language of intuition and of a creative evolutionary force, but he regarded Bergson’s “life” as only one level in an ascending series that continues beyond mind.

    Nietzsche.

    Aurobindo’s “superman” or supramental being has been compared with Nietzsche’s Übermensch. Aurobindo himself discussed Nietzsche, admiring his vision of human self-surpassing but rejecting the ideal of power without spiritual transformation.

    Such comparisons were made by Aurobindo’s early interpreters, including S. K. Maitra, and they remain useful as long as they are not turned into claims of priority. Aurobindo did not anticipate Hegel or Bergson; he read them and others, and built something new from European and Indian materials together. That is itself a form of samanvaya.

    11.9 The Message of 15 August 1947¶

    India became independent on 15 August 1947, Aurobindo’s seventy-fifth birthday. At the request of All India Radio he wrote a message for the occasion, in which he described the coincidence as a sign and set out five “dreams” that he had held since his youth: a free and united India; the resurgence and liberation of the peoples of Asia; a world-union forming the outer basis of a fairer and nobler life for all humanity; the spiritual gift of India to the world; and a new step in the evolution of consciousness. He regarded the partition of the country, then taking place, as a settled fact that must not be accepted as final, and expressed the hope that it would in some way disappear. The message shows the continuity in Aurobindo’s thought between the nationalism of his youth and the evolutionary internationalism of his later works, and it also shows the limits of prophecy: partition did not disappear, and the conflicts it produced were bloodier than almost anyone foresaw.

    11.10 The Ashram and Auroville¶

    The Sri Aurobindo Ashram in Pondicherry grew under the Mother’s direction into a large community with its own farms, workshops, press and, from 1943, a school that became the Sri Aurobindo International Centre of Education, known for its emphasis on physical education and the arts. In 1968, five years before her death, the Mother founded Auroville, an international township on a barren plateau north of Pondicherry, intended as a place where people of all nations could live in harmony beyond religion and politics. Its inauguration was supported by UNESCO, and its residents, now numbering a few thousand from several dozen countries, have reforested much of the land and experimented with renewable energy, architecture and education. Disputes between the residents and the Sri Aurobindo Society led the Government of India to take over its administration, and the Auroville Foundation Act of 1988 placed it under a governing board; tensions over development and governance have continued.

    11.11 Aurobindo and His Contemporaries¶

    Aurobindo’s relations with the other major figures of his time illuminate his position. In 1907, when Aurobindo was on trial for sedition, Rabindranath Tagore published a Bengali poem saluting him as the voice of India’s soul. Aurobindo shared with Tilak, his ally in the extremist wing of the Congress, the conviction that the Gītā taught action in the world, and with Vivekananda the belief that India had a spiritual mission. With Gandhi he had little personal contact, and he did not share Gandhi’s commitment to non-violence as an absolute principle: in his political years he refused to rule out armed resistance, and from Pondicherry he supported the Allies in the Second World War and in 1942 urged acceptance of the Cripps proposals for Indian self-government, which the Congress rejected. In his later works he regarded nationalism, and politics generally, as a stage in the evolution of a spiritual humanity rather than an end.

    Aurobindo’s partnership with the Mother was itself unusual. After 1926 she, a woman and a European, directed the Ashram and was regarded by its members as an embodiment of the divine Mother; Aurobindo wrote a short work, The Mother (1928), explaining her role. In a tradition in which spiritual authority had mostly been held by men, and in a period of nationalist assertion against Europe, the arrangement was a notable departure.

    11.12 The Mother’s Later Teaching¶

    After Aurobindo’s death in 1950, the Mother led the Ashram for another twenty-three years and continued, in her own way, the work he had described. She announced to the Ashram that on 29 February 1956 the “supramental” consciousness had manifested in the earth’s atmosphere, an event that her followers commemorate and that others have no means of assessing. In her last years she described, in conversations with her disciple Satprem later published in many volumes as Mother’s Agenda, an experimental yoga of the cells of the body, aimed at a physical transformation that would overcome death. She died in 1973. Her role as a spiritual authority in her own right — a European woman leading an Indian religious community, and regarded by its members as divine — made the Pondicherry Ashram unusual among modern Indian movements. After her death the Ashram, the Sri Aurobindo Society and Auroville entered a period of disputes over property and authority, a common fate of religious communities after the death of a charismatic founder. For a comparative account, the Mother’s teaching illustrates once again how modern Indian spirituality was shaped by persons from several cultures, and how claims of extraordinary experience, which cannot be checked, sit beside institutions that can be studied historically.

    11.13 Integral Education¶

    The Mother developed, on the basis of Aurobindo’s thought, a theory of “integral education” that aimed at the development of the whole person in five aspects: physical, vital (emotional and energetic), mental, psychic and spiritual. The Ashram school, later the Sri Aurobindo International Centre of Education, emphasised physical education for both girls and boys, the arts and a “free progress” system in which students chose their own subjects and pace, without competitive examinations. Similar ideas have influenced schools in India and elsewhere. Like Tagore’s Santiniketan and Gandhi’s “basic education”, the experiment was a criticism of the examination-centred colonial system, and it is often cited in support of the holistic aims of India’s National Education Policy of 2020 (Chapter 22).

    11.14 Influence¶

    Aurobindo’s influence has been strongest among his own followers in India and abroad, in the field of “integral” education, and in some Western movements. Haridas Chaudhuri, a Bengali philosopher influenced by Aurobindo, founded in San Francisco in 1968 the institute that became the California Institute of Integral Studies; Michael Murphy, co-founder of the Esalen Institute, had spent time at the Pondicherry Ashram; and the American writer Ken Wilber developed an “integral theory” that cites Aurobindo among its inspirations. Commentators have often compared his evolutionary spirituality with that of the Jesuit palaeontologist Pierre Teilhard de Chardin, whose The Phenomenon of Man (published 1955) also described evolution as the ascent of consciousness towards a divine goal; the two developed their ideas independently, and the parallel says more about the intellectual climate of the early twentieth century, shaped by Bergson and by the challenge of Darwin, than about direct influence.

    11.15 Four Modern Syntheses Compared¶

    Aurobindo’s synthesis is best understood beside the others discussed in this part of the volume. Table 11.1 compares four of the most influential modern Indian visions on a few central questions. The summaries simplify large bodies of work, and each thinker changed his views over time.

    Table 11.1. Four modern syntheses compared (simplified)
    Vivekananda Aurobindo Radha­krishnan Gandhi
    Ultimate reality Brahman of Advaita, approached by many paths Saccidānanda unfolding in evolution Spirit known in intuitive experience Truth, which is God
    The world real for practice; field of service real; to be divinised real; a stage for spiritual growth real; field of service and non-violent action
    Other religions all true, Vedānta the most universal partial expressions of one spiritual truth expressions of one experience; Hinduism the most tolerant all true and all imperfect; equal respect
    Caste criticised hereditary privilege; idealised varṇa a stage of social evolution ideal varṇa by aptitude untouchability a sin; varṇa long defended
    Politics national regeneration through character revolutionary nationalism, then world union liberal internationalism non-violent mass action; village self-rule
    Method experience and service integral yoga philosophical reflection experiments with truth

    The table shows how much these visions differ beneath their common vocabulary of spirituality and harmony. It also shows what all four share and what their critics, above all Ambedkar, rejected: the conviction that India’s renewal would come through a reinterpretation of its spiritual tradition rather than through a break with it (Chapter 12).

    11.16 Assessment¶

    Aurobindo’s synthesis is a philosophical vision on a grand scale, written with great erudition and literary power. As philosophy, it can be compared with the speculative systems of Hegel, Bergson and Whitehead, and judged by the same criteria of coherence and illumination. Several points need to be made in a comparative account.

    • It is not a scientific theory. Aurobindo’s evolution of consciousness is a metaphysical interpretation of evolution, not an alternative biological mechanism. It cannot be tested by the methods of biology, and comparisons with recent philosophical critiques of materialism, such as Thomas Nagel’s, are comparisons between philosophical positions.

    • It is a new Vedānta. Aurobindo’s world-affirming, evolutionary reading of the Upaniṣads is his own; it departs from Śaṅkara as consciously as Rāmānuja did, and it draws heavily on European thought. Its value does not depend on its being the “true” meaning of the Veda.

    • Its politics changed. The religious nationalism of 1905–09 and the internationalism of the later works coexist in his writings, and those who invoke his authority today tend to select one or the other.

    • History and hagiography. The publication of Heehs’s documented biography in 2008 provoked protests from some devotees who objected to its treatment of their master as a historical figure. Writers on other modern Indian religious teachers, whose followers often regard critical history as disrespect, have met the same difficulty.
