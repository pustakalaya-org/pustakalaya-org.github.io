    Chapter 18: Buddhism and Jainism in the Modern World¶

    vayadhammā saṅkhārā appamādena sampādethā
    All conditioned things are subject to decay. Strive on with diligence.
    — The last words of the Buddha, Mahāparinibbāna Sutta (Dīgha Nikāya 16)

    18.1 Introduction¶

    Buddhism and Jainism are among India’s oldest living religious traditions, and both are treated historically in Volume 4. This chapter is concerned with their modern histories: the near-disappearance of Buddhism from India and its revival; the global spread of Buddhism and its encounter with Western science and psychology; the Ambedkarite conversion movement; and the modern life of the Jain community in India and abroad. Both traditions are frequently described in Hindu writing as “offshoots” or “reform movements” of Hinduism. Their adherents generally reject that description, and this chapter follows them in treating Buddhism and Jainism as independent traditions with their own histories.

    18.2 The Decline and Rediscovery of Buddhism in India¶

    18.2.1 Decline¶

    Buddhism flourished in India for some fifteen centuries after the Buddha. By the thirteenth century it had all but disappeared from most of the subcontinent, surviving mainly in the Himalayan regions and in small pockets elsewhere. Historians have proposed several causes, which probably worked together. Buddhism depended heavily on large monasteries supported by royal and merchant patronage, and lost that patronage as kings turned to Śaiva and Vaiṣṇava devotional religion. The devotional and tantric forms of Hinduism absorbed many Buddhist practices and figures, including the Buddha himself as an avatāra of Viṣṇu, and offered lay people religious resources that the monastic tradition did not. Brahmanical polemic and, in some regions, persecution played a part. Finally, the great monasteries of the east, already in decline, were destroyed in the course of the Turkish conquests: Nālandā was sacked around 1200 in the campaigns associated with Bakhtiyār Khaljī (c. 1193–1205), as was Vikramaśīla. The Tibetan pilgrim Dharmasvāmin, who visited Nālandā in 1234, found a few monks still teaching amid the ruins. Ambedkar emphasised Brahmanical hostility and the Muslim invasions; most historians now see a longer and more complex process (Omvedt 2003; Harvey 2013). Volume 4 discusses the evidence, and also corrects the popular myth that Nālandā’s library held “nine million” manuscripts, a figure for which there is no early source.

    18.2.2 Rediscovery¶

    Buddhism’s Indian past was recovered in the nineteenth century largely by European scholars and by Buddhists from other Asian countries. James Prinsep’s decipherment of the Brāhmī script in 1837 made the edicts of Aśoka legible; Alexander Cunningham, first director of the Archaeological Survey of India (1861), used the travel records of the Chinese pilgrims Faxian and Xuanzang to identify the sites of the Buddha’s life. The Mahābodhi temple at Bodh Gaya was restored in the 1880s. Edwin Arnold’s poem on the life of the Buddha, The Light of Asia (1879), was a best-seller in Britain, America and India. Philip Almond has shown how Victorian Britain “discovered” Buddhism as a textual religion, often contrasting a pure, rational “original” Buddhism found in the Pali texts with the living Buddhism of Asia (Almond 1988).

    18.2.3 Monuments and art¶

    The monuments of Indian Buddhism were rediscovered in the same period. The painted caves of Ajanta, abandoned for more than a millennium, were found in 1819 by a party of British officers hunting in the Waghora valley; copies of the paintings made in the following decades, many of them later destroyed by fire, brought them to international attention. The great stūpa at Sanchi, surveyed in 1818, was excavated carelessly and then restored under John Marshall, Director-General of the Archaeological Survey from 1902, who also excavated at Taxila and Sarnath and later at the Indus cities. The Gandhāra sculptures of the north-west, with their Greco-Roman features, fascinated colonial scholars, some of whom argued that Indian art owed its finest achievements to Greek influence — a view that Indian and other historians of art, including Coomaraswamy, later contested by tracing the independent development of the Buddha image at Mathurā (Chapter 21). The archaeological recovery of Buddhist India had political consequences too: the Aśokan lion capital excavated at Sarnath in 1905 became the emblem of the Indian republic.

    18.2.4 Anagārika Dharmapāla¶

    The Sri Lankan reformer Anagārika Dharmapāla (1864–1933), inspired partly by the Theosophists Blavatsky and Olcott, founded the Maha Bodhi Society in 1891 and campaigned for the return of Bodh Gaya, then controlled by a Hindu abbot, to Buddhist management. He spoke at the Chicago Parliament of 1893 alongside Vivekananda. The dispute over Bodh Gaya lasted more than half a century; the Bodh Gaya Temple Act of 1949 placed the temple under a committee of four Buddhists and four Hindus, with the district magistrate as chairman. Dharmapāla’s Buddhism was an example of what David McMahan calls “Buddhist modernism”: a reformulation of the tradition that emphasised its rationality, its compatibility with science, its ethics and its meditation, and played down ritual, cosmology and the supernatural (McMahan 2008).

    18.3 Buddhism in Modern India¶

    The 2011 census recorded about 8.4 million Buddhists in India, about 0.7 per cent of the population. Three groups account for most of them.

    Ambedkarite Buddhists.

    The largest group, concentrated in Maharashtra, consists of Dalits who followed B. R. Ambedkar into Buddhism at Nagpur on 14 October 1956 and in the conversions that have continued since (Chapter 12). Their Buddhism, often called Navayāna, is centred on Ambedkar’s The Buddha and His Dhamma, on the twenty-two vows he administered and on the anniversary of the conversion, celebrated at the Dīkṣābhūmi in Nagpur. It is a Buddhism of social liberation and dignity, critical of Hinduism and of caste, and it has produced a vigorous literature and political movement (Omvedt 2003; Zelliot 1992).

    Ambedkarite Buddhism has its own festivals, pilgrimages and literature. Every year on the anniversary of the conversion, hundreds of thousands of people gather at the Dīkṣābhūmi in Nagpur, and on 6 December, the anniversary of Ambedkar’s death, at his memorial in Mumbai. Dalit Buddhist writers in Marathi — poets such as Namdeo Dhasal and autobiographers such as Daya Pawar — made Buddhism a literary as well as a religious identity. The movement has also produced debates within itself: about the relation of Ambedkar’s Navayāna to the Theravāda and Mahāyāna traditions of other countries, about ritual and monasticism, and about the relation between religious conversion and political mobilisation. It has spread beyond Maharashtra, to Dalit communities in Uttar Pradesh, Punjab, Karnataka and elsewhere, and to the diaspora.

    Himalayan and Tibetan Buddhists.

    The Buddhist communities of Ladakh, Sikkim, Arunachal Pradesh and other Himalayan regions belong to the Tibetan traditions. After the Chinese takeover of Tibet and the flight of the Fourteenth Dalai Lama in 1959, India gave refuge to many thousands of Tibetans; the Dalai Lama’s government in exile is based at Dharamsala in Himachal Pradesh, and Tibetan monasteries have been re-established in Karnataka and elsewhere. India thus became a centre of Tibetan Buddhist learning.

    The Vipassanā movement.

    S. N. Goenka (1924–2013), born in Burma to an Indian family and trained by the Burmese lay teacher Sayagyi U Ba Khin, began teaching ten-day silent retreats in India in 1969 and established the centre Dhamma Giri at Igatpuri, Maharashtra. His non-sectarian presentation of Theravāda insight meditation spread throughout India and the world, attracting many people who would not call themselves Buddhists.

    The modern Indian state has also used the Buddhist heritage. The Aśokan lion capital became the state emblem and the Dharma wheel appears on the national flag. A new Nālandā University was established by Act of Parliament in 2010 near the ancient site, with the support of several East Asian governments, and began teaching in 2014. India’s diplomatic outreach to Buddhist Asia draws on the pilgrimage sites of Bodh Gaya, Sarnath, Kushinagar and (across the border in Nepal) Lumbini.

    18.3.1 Buddhist studies in modern India¶

    The modern study of Buddhism in India owes much to Indian scholar-monks and laymen as well as to Europeans. Dharmanand Kosambi (1876–1947), a Goan Brahmin who learned Pali in Sri Lanka, where he entered the Buddhist order, and later lived as a monk in Burma, taught Pali at Calcutta, Pune and Harvard, edited Buddhaghosa’s Visuddhimagga for the Harvard Oriental Series, and wrote in Marathi on the Buddha’s life and on non-violence; his son was the historian D. D. Kosambi. The monk Jagdish Kashyap (1908–1976) founded the Nava Nalanda Mahavihara near the ancient site in 1951 and directed the publication of the Pali canon in Devanāgarī script, making it accessible to Indian readers for the first time in centuries. The Vipassana Research Institute at Igatpuri later produced an electronic edition of the canon based on the Burmese Sixth Council text. These efforts show that the “rediscovery” of Buddhism in India was not only a colonial enterprise but also an Indian and Asian one.

    18.3.2 Buddhism, Hinduism and the avatāra question¶

    Relations between modern Buddhists and Hindus have often turned on a single question: is the Buddha an avatāra of Viṣṇu? From about the sixth century CE several Purāṇas include the Buddha among Viṣṇu’s descents, sometimes with the explanation that he came to mislead the wicked with false doctrine, sometimes as a teacher of compassion and non-violence; Jayadeva’s Gītagovinda (twelfth century) praises him for abolishing animal sacrifice. Many modern Hindus regard the doctrine as a sign of respect and of the unity of Indian traditions. Many Buddhists regard it as an act of absorption that denies the independence of their tradition. Among the twenty-two vows that Ambedkar administered at Nagpur in 1956 was a refusal to believe that the Buddha was an incarnation of Viṣṇu, and Ambedkarite Buddhists regard the doctrine as part of the process by which Buddhism was erased in India. The disagreement is a clear instance of the inclusivism discussed in Chapter 1: a gesture of inclusion from one side can be an act of appropriation from the other.

    18.3.3 Women in modern Buddhism¶

    The order of fully ordained nuns (bhikkhunī) died out in the Theravāda countries many centuries ago and was never established in Tibet, although it survived in China, Korea, Taiwan and Vietnam. Since the late twentieth century, Buddhist women and their supporters have worked to restore it. An ordination of Sri Lankan women held at Sarnath in 1996 with the assistance of Korean nuns and monks was one of the first steps in a revival that has since spread in Sri Lanka and elsewhere, though it remains contested by conservative monastic authorities in several countries. India, as the land where the Buddha first admitted women to the order, has thus again become a site of debate about the tradition’s treatment of women.

    18.4 Global Buddhism¶

    18.4.1 The traditions¶

    Buddhism spread from India across Asia in three broad streams (Table 18.1). Theravāda, “the teaching of the elders”, using the Pali canon, was established in Sri Lanka by the third century BCE (by tradition through the mission of Aśoka’s son Mahinda) and later became dominant in Myanmar, Thailand, Laos and Cambodia. Mahāyāna Buddhism reached China in the first centuries CE along the trade routes of Central Asia, and from China passed to Korea, Japan and Vietnam, where it developed schools such as Chan (Zen), Pure Land, Tiantai and Huayan. The tantric Buddhism of the late Indian monasteries (Vajrayāna) was transmitted to Tibet from the eighth century onwards, and from Tibet to Mongolia. The word “Hīnayāna” (“lesser vehicle”), used polemically in Mahāyāna texts for the older schools, is now generally avoided. Estimates of the number of Buddhists in the world are uncertain because of the overlap of Buddhist, Confucian, Daoist and Shinto practice in East Asia; the Pew Research Center’s estimate for 2010 was about 490 million.

    18.4.2 South Asian Buddhism beyond India¶

    Two South Asian Buddhist communities outside India are of particular interest for the history traced in this chapter. In the Kathmandu valley of Nepal, the Newar Buddhists preserved a Sanskrit-based Mahāyāna and Vajrayāna tradition continuously from the period of the great Indian monasteries. Their householder priests (vajrācārya), married and hereditary rather than celibate, perform rituals in Sanskrit, and their libraries preserved many Sanskrit Buddhist manuscripts lost in India; it was from Nepal that the British resident Brian Houghton Hodgson sent Sanskrit Buddhist texts to Calcutta, London and Paris in the 1820s and 1830s, enabling Eugène Burnouf’s pioneering study of Indian Buddhism (1844). In the twentieth century Theravāda missionaries from Sri Lanka and Burma established a reform movement among Newars, so that both forms of Buddhism now coexist in the valley.

    Bhutan, the only state in which a form of Tibetan Buddhism (the Drukpa Kagyu school) is the state religion, has drawn international attention for its policy of “Gross National Happiness”, a phrase associated with its fourth king in the 1970s and later developed into a set of indicators of well-being. The policy is often described as an application of Buddhist values to economics; observers have also noted the expulsion of many Nepali-speaking Lhotshampa residents from southern Bhutan in the early 1990s, which shows that the ideal and the practice of a Buddhist state can diverge, as they did in the reign of Aśoka.

    18.4.3 Buddhism in the West¶

    Buddhism entered Western intellectual life through scholarship in the nineteenth century and through Asian teachers and Western converts in the twentieth. D. T. Suzuki’s writings popularised Zen; the Beat writers Jack Kerouac, Allen Ginsberg and Gary Snyder drew on it; Japanese and Tibetan teachers founded centres in America and Europe; and the Vietnamese monk Thich Nhat Hanh (1926–2022), exiled in France, developed an “engaged Buddhism” that applied mindfulness and compassion to social and political action (Queen and King 1996). Engaged Buddhism also took other forms in Asia, among them the Sarvodaya movement of Sri Lanka, which drew on Gandhi, and the Ambedkarite movement in India.

    18.4.4 Buddhism and science¶

    Since the late nineteenth century, Buddhist modernists have presented Buddhism as the religion most compatible with science, because it rejects a creator god, emphasises causation and invites investigation. The Fourteenth Dalai Lama has made dialogue with scientists a central part of his work. The Mind and Life Institute, founded in 1987 by the Dalai Lama, the lawyer and entrepreneur Adam Engle and the neuroscientist Francisco Varela, has organised dialogues with physicists, psychologists and neuroscientists and supported research on meditation. In The Universe in a Single Atom (2005), the Dalai Lama wrote that if science conclusively disproved a Buddhist claim, Buddhists should abandon the claim, and gave as his example the Meru-centred cosmology of the Abhidharma (Chapter 3) (Dalai Lama XIV 2005). Research on the effects of meditation is discussed in Chapter 20.

    Donald Lopez has traced the long history of claims that Buddhism is “scientific”, from nineteenth-century Asian reformers and Western Theosophists to modern neuroscience, and has shown how much each version reshaped Buddhism to fit the science of its day (Lopez 2008). His critique does not imply that the dialogue is worthless; it implies that it should be conducted without the assumption that the tradition must turn out to have been right all along.

    18.4.5 Recovering the Indian Buddhist heritage¶

    Much of the Sanskrit literature of Indian Buddhism had been lost in India and survived only in Tibetan and Chinese translation, or in manuscripts carried to Nepal and Tibet. In the 1930s the scholar and traveller Rahul Sankrityayan (1893–1963; Section 18.6) made several journeys to Tibet and brought back photographs of Sanskrit manuscripts of important Buddhist works, including texts of the logician Dharmakīrti, that were thought to be lost. After 1959 the Tibetan exile community made India once again a centre of Buddhist learning: the Central Institute of Higher Tibetan Studies at Sarnath (founded in 1967, now a university) and the Library of Tibetan Works and Archives at Dharamsala (1970) teach and preserve the tradition, and Tibetan scholars have collaborated with Indian ones to translate lost Sanskrit works back from Tibetan.

    18.4.6 Buddhist philosophy in world philosophy¶

    Buddhist philosophy has become one of the most active fields of cross-cultural philosophy. Nāgārjuna’s arguments for emptiness have been compared with Wittgenstein, Derrida and contemporary anti-realism; the Yogācāra analysis of consciousness with phenomenology; Dharmakīrti’s epistemology with modern theories of perception and inference; and the Buddhist denial of a self with debates about personal identity initiated by Derek Parfit. Jay Garfield’s translation of Nāgārjuna’s Mūlamadhyamakakārikā, published as The Fundamental Wisdom of the Middle Way, with a philosophical commentary, and Mark Siderits’s introduction, are examples of this work (Nāgārjuna 1995; Siderits 2007). Here, as elsewhere, the most fruitful comparisons are between particular arguments rather than between “Buddhism” and “the West”.

    18.4.7 Buddhism and nationalism¶

    Modern Buddhism is not only a religion of peace and meditation. In Sri Lanka and Myanmar, Buddhist nationalist movements, some led by monks, have supported violence against Tamil, Muslim and Rohingya minorities. These movements are a reminder, parallel to the Hindu examples discussed in Chapter 7, that no tradition’s ideals guarantee the conduct of those who claim them.

    18.5 Jainism in the Modern World¶

    18.5.1 The community¶

    The 2011 census recorded about 4.5 million Jains in India, concentrated in Gujarat, Rajasthan, Maharashtra, Madhya Pradesh and Karnataka; there are significant communities abroad, in Britain, North America and East Africa. The community is divided between the Śvetāmbara (“white-clad”) and Digambara (“sky-clad”) traditions, which differ on the nudity of monks, the possibility of liberation for women and the authority of the canon; the Śvetāmbaras are further divided into image-worshipping (mūrtipūjaka) and non-image-worshipping (Sthānakavāsī and Terāpanthī) traditions. Most Jains are lay people, many of them in commerce; monks and nuns, though few, are central to the community’s religious life. John Cort’s study of Jain lay and monastic values in Gujarat and Jeffery Long’s introduction are good guides (Cort 2001; Long 2009). The Government of India recognised the Jains as a national minority community in 2014.

    18.5.2 Monks, nuns and the question of women¶

    The ascetic community remains the centre of Jain religious life. Among the Śvetāmbaras, nuns substantially outnumber monks; they preach, teach and travel on foot like their male counterparts, though they are formally subordinate to monks. The Digambara tradition has historically held that a woman cannot attain liberation in a female body, since liberation requires complete nudity and women cannot practise it; Digambara women may become renunciants of a lower grade (āryikā). The question of the liberation of women (strīmukti) was debated between the two traditions in medieval philosophical texts, the Śvetāmbaras affirming that women can be liberated and that the nineteenth Tīrthaṅkara, Mallī, was a woman (Jaini 1979; Dundas 2002). The debate is one of the most interesting cases in Indian thought of a philosophical argument conducted explicitly about gender.

    18.5.3 Jain identity and the law¶

    The legal position of the Jains illustrates the problem of classification that runs through this volume. Under Article 25 of the Constitution, for the purpose of laws on religious institutions, “Hindus” is defined to include “persons professing the Sikh, Jaina or Buddhist religion”, and the Hindu Code laws of the 1950s applied to Jains, Buddhists and Sikhs. Many Jains objected that this treated them as a Hindu sect. The question of whether they should be recognised as a minority community was contested in the courts and in state legislation for decades before the central government’s notification of 2014. Jains themselves have been divided: some emphasise their distinct identity, others their close social and family ties with Hindu merchant communities, with whom intermarriage has been common in some regions.

    18.5.4 Libraries and learning¶

    Jain communities were among the great preservers of Indian manuscripts. From the medieval period, monks and wealthy lay patrons maintained libraries (jñāna bhaṇḍāra) attached to temples and monasteries, notably at Patan and Cambay in Gujarat and at Jaisalmer in Rajasthan, where palm-leaf and paper manuscripts were copied, stored in sealed rooms and protected from damp and insects. These collections preserved not only Jain canonical and philosophical works but also Brahmanical and Buddhist texts on logic, poetics, grammar and other sciences, some of which survive nowhere else. Jain scholars were prolific authors in Sanskrit, Prakrit and the regional languages: Hemacandra (1088/89–1172), at the court of the Caulukya kings of Gujarat, wrote on grammar, lexicography, poetics, logic and Jain doctrine and composed a universal history of the Jain heroes. In the twentieth century, the Jain monks Muni Puṇyavijaya and Muni Jinavijaya catalogued and edited many of these collections, and Jain foundations continue to support manuscript conservation. The history of Indian learning as a whole owes much to these libraries.

    18.5.5 Modern Jain figures¶

    Jainism’s modern history includes figures who made it known beyond the community. The German Indologist Hermann Jacobi (1850–1937), who translated Jain canonical texts for The Sacred Books of the East, established that Jainism was an independent tradition and not, as earlier European scholars had supposed, an offshoot of Buddhism. Virchand Gandhi (1864–1901) represented the Jains at the Chicago Parliament of 1893 and lectured in America and Britain. Śrīmad Rājacandra (1867–1901), a Jain jeweller, poet and mystic, was one of the strongest influences on the young M. K. Gandhi (Chapter 12). In 1949 Ācārya Tulsī, head of the Terāpanthī order, launched the Aṇuvrata movement, which invited people of every religion to adopt small vows of non-violence and honesty in daily life.

    18.5.6 Temples, pilgrimage and festivals¶

    Jain religious life centres on temples and pilgrimage as well as on ascetic teachers. The temple-cities on the hills of Śatruñjaya near Palitana in Gujarat and Girnar near Junagadh, the marble temples of Dilwara at Mount Abu (eleventh to thirteenth centuries) and the great four-faced temple at Ranakpur in Rajasthan (fifteenth century) are among the finest monuments of Indian architecture, built largely with the donations of merchant families. Digambara pilgrims travel to Shravanabelagola in Karnataka, where the colossal monolithic image of Bāhubali, erected in the late tenth century, is anointed in a great ceremony (mahāmastakābhiṣeka) roughly every twelve years, and to Sammeta Śikhara in Jharkhand, the hill of the Tīrthaṅkaras’ liberation (Chapter 3). The festival of Paryuṣaṇa (for Digambaras, Daśalakṣaṇa), held during the rainy season, is a period of fasting, scripture reading and confession that ends with the asking and granting of forgiveness among family, friends and community. These practices show that Jainism, often described abroad only through its doctrines of non-violence and many-sidedness, is also a richly ritual and devotional religion (Cort 2001).

    18.5.7 Jains abroad¶

    Gujarati Jain traders settled in East Africa from the late nineteenth century, and after the expulsions and emigrations of the 1960s and 1970s many moved to Britain; others emigrated to North America from the 1960s. Diaspora communities have built temples — often shared between Śvetāmbara and Digambara worshippers, and sometimes housing images of several traditions in one building — and have adapted Jain practice to new circumstances, for example by emphasising the tradition’s ethical and environmental teachings for a generation brought up outside India. Long’s and Cort’s studies discuss these developments (Long 2009; Cort 2001).

    18.5.8 Jain ethics and modern concerns¶

    Jain ideas have entered modern conversations in three main areas. Jain non-violence and vegetarianism have influenced animal-welfare and environmental movements; Jain institutions run hospitals for birds and animals (pāñjrāpoḷ), and Jain scholars have presented the tradition as an ecological ethic, a presentation that is partly a modern interpretation of an ethic originally directed at the soul’s liberation. The doctrine of many-sidedness (anekāntavāda) has been presented as a philosophy of tolerance and dialogue; Chapter 7 noted that it was classically also a technique of debate. And the practice of fasting to death (sallekhanā) has raised legal and ethical questions about the right to die. In each case the modern presentation adapts an old idea to new questions; the adaptation is legitimate, but it should be acknowledged as an adaptation.

    18.6 Modern Buddhist and Jain Thinkers¶

    The institutional histories above can hide the fact that modern Buddhism and Jainism in India produced thinkers of their own, who reinterpreted their traditions for a new age as boldly as the Hindu and Muslim reformers did theirs. Ambedkar’s Navayāna is described in Chapter 12; this section treats some of the others. Several of them knew one another or one another’s work, and together they show that the modern Indian argument about religion, reason and caste was not conducted only in Vedāntic terms.

    18.6.1 Lakshmi Narasu: a rationalist Buddhism¶

    P. Lakshmi Narasu (1861–1934), a teacher of science in Madras who was associated with Iyothee Thass’s Sakya Buddhist Society (Chapter 14), published The Essence of Buddhism in 1907, with an introduction by Dharmapāla. It presented the Buddha’s teaching as a rational ethics that required no creator, no soul and no priesthood, compatible with modern science and opposed in principle to caste; a later book, A Study of Caste (1922), turned the same rationalism on the institution itself. Narasu’s Buddhism was closer to the Victorian rationalists than to the living Buddhism of Sri Lanka or Burma, and it shared the limits of Buddhist modernism described above. Its importance lies in its afterlife: Ambedkar reissued the book in 1948 with a preface praising it as the best book on Buddhism, and it was one of the sources from which he built his own interpretation.

    18.6.2 Dharmanand Kosambi: Buddhism, non-violence and socialism¶

    Dharmanand Kosambi (1876–1947), whose work as a scholar of Pali is described above, was also a thinker who tried to bring together the Buddha, Gandhi and socialism. His Marathi writings, collected and translated by his granddaughter Meera Kosambi, include a life of the Buddha written for the general reader, an autobiography and studies of the history of non-violence (Kosambi 2010). He argued that ahiṃsā had entered Indian culture through the śramaṇa traditions of the Buddha and Mahāvīra rather than through the Vedic sacrificial religion, and he was critical of the Bhagavadgītā’s justification of war, a point on which he differed from Gandhi while admiring him. He taught Pali for a time in Leningrad in 1929, took part in the salt satyagraha, and saw in the early Buddhist community a model of a society without private property. In 1947, having decided to end his life by fasting in the manner that both Buddhist and Jain traditions allow to the dying, he did so at Gandhi’s ashram at Sevagram. Kosambi’s synthesis of Buddhist ethics, Gandhian method and socialist aims was idiosyncratic, but it anticipated the engaged Buddhism of later decades, and through his son D. D. Kosambi (Chapter 15) it entered the Marxist historiography of India.

    18.6.3 Rahul Sankrityayan: Buddhism and Marxism¶

    Rahul Sankrityayan (1893–1963), whose journeys to Tibet in search of Sanskrit manuscripts are described above, passed through more of modern India’s religious options than any other thinker of his time. Born Kedarnath Pandey in a Brahmin family of eastern Uttar Pradesh, he became a Vaiṣṇava ascetic, then an Arya Samaj preacher, then, after studying Pali in Sri Lanka, a Buddhist monk under the name by which he is known, and finally a Marxist and a member of the Communist Party, without ceasing to call himself a follower of the Buddha’s method. He wrote more than a hundred books in Hindi, among them translations of Pali suttas, a survey of world philosophy, Darśan-Digdarśan, and Volgā se Gaṅgā (1943), both written in prison in the early 1940s, the second a cycle of stories that follows a human community from the prehistoric steppes to modern India and treats religion and caste as historical creations. He read the Buddha as an empiricist who rejected authority and speculation and taught that all things are impermanent and conditioned, and he regarded the Buddhist analysis of change as a precursor of dialectical thinking. His Buddhism, like Narasu’s, was selective; unlike Narasu’s, it was subordinated to a theory of history.

    18.6.4 Śrīmad Rājacandra and the inward path¶

    Among Jain thinkers the most influential of the period was Śrīmad Rājacandra (1867–1901), the Gujarati jeweller and poet whose influence on Gandhi is noted in Chapter 12. Known in his youth for feats of memory and attention (avadhāna), he turned to a life of introspection while continuing in business. His Ātmasiddhi Śāstra (“The Treatise on Self-Realisation”, 1896), 142 verses in Gujarati, sets out six fundamental propositions — that the soul exists, that it is eternal, that it is the agent of its actions, that it experiences their fruits, that liberation exists and that there is a path to it — and defends each against objections in the manner of a classical dialogue. Gandhi had sent him a series of questions from South Africa in 1894 about religion, God, the soul and the claims of Christianity, and Rājacandra’s replies, which advised him to look for the truth within his own tradition before changing it, are among the documents of Gandhi’s formation. Rājacandra was critical of sectarian divisions among Jains and of ritual without understanding, and the communities of his followers, which are not attached to either the Śvetāmbara or the Digambara order, have spread in India and abroad.

    18.6.5 Kanji Swami and the Digambara revival¶

    A different kind of inward turn produced the largest new Jain movement of the twentieth century. Kanji Swami (1890–1980), a Sthānakavāsī monk in Saurashtra, was drawn to the Samayasāra of the Digambara teacher Kundakunda, with its distinction between the conventional standpoint (vyavahāra-naya), from which the soul acts and is bound, and the ultimate standpoint (niścaya-naya), from which the pure soul is untouched by karma. In 1934 or 1935 at Songadh he gave up his monastic status and declared himself a lay follower of the Digambara teaching; he never took Digambara ascetic initiation, and the movement that grew around him remained largely a lay movement, centred on the study of Kundakunda and on the realisation of the soul’s pure nature. Digambara monks and scholars criticised it for neglecting the conventional discipline of vows and asceticism on which the tradition had always insisted, and the controversy recalls older Indian debates about whether insight can make practice unnecessary (Dundas 2002).

    18.6.6 Jain scholarship and the modern self-image¶

    Jain learning also entered the modern academy. Pandit Sukhlāl Saṅghavī (1880–1978), blind from his youth, taught Jain philosophy at Banaras Hindu University and with Becardās Dośī edited Siddhasena Divākara’s Sanmatitarka, a foundational work of Jain logic; in Gujarati and Hindi essays he presented Jain thought historically, as a tradition that had changed through argument with Buddhists and Brahmanical schools, rather than as a timeless system. English-language writers, among them the barrister Champat Rai Jain, presented Jainism to Western and Indian readers in the vocabulary of comparative religion, and in the Terāpanthī order Ācārya Mahāprajña developed from the 1970s a system of meditation, prekṣā dhyāna, that drew on the canonical texts and on modern psychology. These efforts produced the image of Jainism, now familiar worldwide, as a religion of non-violence, many-sidedness and ecological restraint. As with the other modernisms of this chapter, the image selects from a larger tradition, much of which — the temple ritual, the cosmology, the place of renunciation — it leaves in the background.

    18.7 Assessment¶

    The modern histories of Buddhism and Jainism illustrate the themes of this volume. Both were reinterpreted in the nineteenth and twentieth centuries in conversation with Western scholarship, Theosophy and science; both produced “modernist” versions emphasising reason, ethics and meditation; both have been claimed by Hindu writers as parts of a larger Hindu or “Indic” whole, and both have resisted that claim. Ambedkar’s conversion made Buddhism once again a living Indian religion of the poor and the excluded, and it did so in explicit opposition to Hinduism. A synthesis that respects these traditions must allow them to speak for themselves, including when what they say is a refusal.
