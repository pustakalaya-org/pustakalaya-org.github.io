    Chapter 20: Yoga, Meditation and Āyurveda in the Global Age¶

    yogaś cittavṛttinirodhaḥ
    Yoga is the stilling of the fluctuations of the mind.
    — Yogasūtra 1.2 (c. 325–425 CE)

    20.1 Introduction¶

    Of all the forms of Indian knowledge, yoga, meditation and Āyurveda have travelled furthest in the modern world. Hundreds of millions of people practise some form of yoga; mindfulness meditation, derived from Buddhist practice, is taught in hospitals, schools and businesses; Āyurvedic products are sold across the world, and the Government of India promotes Āyurveda through a dedicated ministry. This global success is often cited as proof of the value of Indian knowledge. It also raises difficult questions: How much of modern yoga is ancient? What does the scientific evidence on meditation and Āyurvedic treatments show? Who “owns” these traditions? And how can the public be protected from unsafe products and exaggerated claims? This chapter addresses these questions. The classical traditions are described in Volumes 5 (yoga) and 13 (Āyurveda), and psychology in Volume 22.

    20.2 Yoga: Classical and Modern¶

    20.2.1 Classical yoga¶

    The Yogasūtra attributed to Patañjali, which Philipp Maas dates to c. 325–425 CE, defines yoga as the stilling of the mind’s fluctuations, describes an eightfold path (aṣṭāṅga) of restraints, observances, posture, breath control, withdrawal of the senses, concentration, meditation and absorption, and aims at the isolation (kaivalya) of the conscious self from matter (Maas 2013). Posture (āsana) receives three short sūtras: it should be “steady and comfortable”. Elaborate postures and breathing techniques developed later, in the haṭhayoga texts of the eleventh century onward, such as the fifteenth-century Haṭhapradīpikā, which aimed at bodily transformation and the raising of inner energy. Yoga was practised by renouncers of many sects — Śaiva, Vaiṣṇava, Buddhist and Jain — and by Sufis, and its techniques were adapted for many purposes, including worldly power.

    Table 20.1 sets the eight “limbs” of Patañjali’s yoga beside the emphasis they typically receive in modern postural yoga. The contrast is not a criticism of either; it shows how much the meaning of the word has changed.

    Table 20.1. The eight limbs of yoga (Yogasūtra 2.29) and modern practice (simplified)

    yama five restraints: non-injury, truthfulness, non-stealing, celibacy, non-possession often mentioned as ethics; rarely the centre of a class
    niyama five observances: purity, contentment, austerity, study, devotion to the Lord variable

    samādhi absorption, leading to the isolation of the self rarely a stated goal

    20.2.2 The making of modern yoga¶

    Modern yoga took shape in the late nineteenth and twentieth centuries. Vivekananda’s Raja Yoga (1896) presented Patañjali’s yoga as a science of the mind and gave little place to postures, which he associated with disreputable ascetics (Chapter 10). In the early twentieth century, Indian reformers re-created posture practice as a form of physical culture and therapy. Shri Yogendra founded the Yoga Institute in Bombay in 1918; Swami Kuvalayananda founded Kaivalyadhama at Lonavla in 1924, where he studied the physiology of yoga with laboratory instruments and published a scientific journal; and T. Krishnamacharya in Mysore trained the teachers who spread posture practice worldwide (see below). Swami Sivananda’s Divine Life Society (1936) and Maharishi Mahesh Yogi’s Transcendental Meditation (from the late 1950s) carried other forms abroad.

    Historians have shown how much this modern yoga owes to its time. Mark Singleton argues that the posture-based practice of today drew on European gymnastics and physical culture as well as on haṭhayoga (Singleton 2010); Joseph Alter shows how Kuvalayananda and others made yoga into a “science” of the body in the idiom of modern physiology (Alter 2004); Elizabeth De Michelis traces the influence of Western esotericism on Vivekananda’s formulation (De Michelis 2004); and Andrea Jain describes how yoga became a global consumer product (Jain 2015). These findings do not mean that modern yoga is “inauthentic”. They mean that it is a modern tradition with ancient roots, like most living traditions, and that claims that today’s studio practice is unchanged from the time of the Vedas are false.

    20.2.3 Global recognition and its politics¶

    On the initiative of the Government of India, the United Nations General Assembly proclaimed 21 June as the International Day of Yoga in December 2014, and UNESCO inscribed yoga on its list of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity in 2016. India’s Traditional Knowledge Digital Library has documented yoga postures from classical texts to prevent their being patented. Debates over ownership have taken several forms: some Hindu organisations have argued that yoga’s Hindu roots are being concealed in the West; some Christian groups have objected to yoga in schools as a Hindu practice; and attempts to copyright particular sequences of postures have been rejected by American courts. A comparative view suggests that yoga, like Buddhism, belongs to the traditions that created it and also to the many people who have made it part of their lives, and that acknowledging its origins is a matter of historical honesty, not of ownership.

    20.2.4 Haṭha yoga before modern yoga¶

    Between the classical Yogasūtra and modern postural yoga lies the tradition of haṭha yoga, the “yoga of force”, which developed in the early second millennium CE, partly in tantric Śaiva and Buddhist circles and among ascetic orders such as the Nāths. Its best-known text, the Haṭhapradīpikā of Svātmārāma (c. fifteenth century), describes a small number of postures, breath-retentions, cleansing techniques and “seals” (mudrā) intended to control the vital breath and the seminal fluid, to awaken the coiled power (kuṇḍalinī) and to lead to the meditative absorption (samādhi) that the text calls rājayoga. Later texts such as the Gheraṇḍasaṃhitā (c. seventeenth–eighteenth century) increased the number of postures. Recent research, including a large anthology of translated sources by James Mallinson and Mark Singleton, has shown that haṭha yoga was more varied, older in some of its techniques and more widely practised by householders than earlier scholarship assumed (Mallinson and Singleton 2017). It has also shown that the goals of these practices were soteriological and magical — liberation, immortality, supernatural powers — rather than fitness or health in the modern sense. Modern yoga drew on this heritage selectively, as it drew on the Yogasūtra.

    20.2.5 The Mysore lineages¶

    Much of the posture-based yoga practised worldwide descends from a single teacher. Tirumalai Krishnamacharya (1888–1989) taught yoga at the Jaganmohan Palace in Mysore from the 1930s under the patronage of the Maharaja Krishnaraja Wadiyar IV. Singleton has shown that the vigorous sequences taught there drew on haṭha techniques but also on the physical-culture movements of the period, including Indian wrestling and gymnastics and the Scandinavian and British systems used in Indian schools and the army (Singleton 2010). Krishnamacharya’s students carried different versions of his teaching abroad: K. Pattabhi Jois (1915–2009) developed the flowing “Ashtanga Vinyasa” system; B. K. S. Iyengar (1918–2014) a precise, alignment-based method using props, made famous by Light on Yoga (1966); Indra Devi (born Eugenie Peterson in Riga) taught Hollywood actors from the 1940s; and his son T. K. V. Desikachar adapted practice to individual needs and therapeutic purposes. The diversity of these styles, all descending from one teacher within a single lifetime, shows how recently modern postural yoga took its present forms.

    20.2.6 Yoga and other religions¶

    Yoga’s spread has raised questions for adherents of other religions. In 1989 the Congregation for the Doctrine of the Faith of the Roman Catholic Church issued a letter on Christian meditation that warned against adopting Eastern methods in ways that would displace prayer to a personal God, while allowing that some elements could be used. In 2008 the National Fatwa Council of Malaysia advised Muslims against practising yoga that involved Hindu chanting and worship, while permitting physical exercise alone. In the United States, parents who objected to a yoga programme in the public schools of Encinitas, California, as the teaching of Hinduism lost their case; the courts held in 2013 and on appeal in 2015 that the programme, as taught, was a secular physical-education class. Christian and Muslim practitioners have developed their own adaptations. These debates mirror those within India, mentioned below. They show that whether yoga is “religious” depends less on its origins than on what is done and said in a particular class.

    20.2.7 Yoga in India today¶

    In India itself, yoga has become a mass practice and an instrument of public policy. Television teachers, of whom Ramdev became the best known from the early 2000s, taught breathing exercises and postures to millions, often with claims of cures for chronic diseases that went well beyond the evidence; the business that grew from his following became one of India’s largest makers of consumer goods. The Ministry of AYUSH promotes a “common yoga protocol” for the International Day of Yoga, when mass demonstrations are held across the country, and yoga is taught in many schools. These developments have made yoga part of national identity and public health promotion. They have also revived questions about its relation to Hinduism — some Muslim and Christian organisations objected to compulsory practices such as the sun salutation and the chanting of “Om” — and about the regulation of health claims (see below).

    20.3 Meditation and Psychology¶

    20.3.1 Jung and the first encounters¶

    Carl Gustav Jung was among the first Western psychologists to engage seriously with Indian traditions. His seminar on Kuṇḍalinī yoga, given in Zurich in 1932 with the Indologist J. W. Hauer, interpreted the cakras as symbols of stages of psychological development (Jung 1996). He also warned that Western people should not simply imitate Eastern methods, which had grown from a different culture and could be psychologically harmful if used without understanding. The transpersonal psychology movement of the 1960s and 1970s, associated with Abraham Maslow and others, drew on Indian and Buddhist sources to describe “peak” and mystical experiences. Modern Indian psychologists have developed an “Indian psychology” that draws on the Yoga, Vedānta and Buddhist traditions, discussed in Volume 22.

    20.3.2 From meditation to mindfulness¶

    Scientific study of meditation began in earnest in the 1970s, with research on Transcendental Meditation and Herbert Benson’s account of a “relaxation response” common to many meditative practices. In 1979 Jon Kabat-Zinn, a molecular biologist trained in Zen and Vipassanā meditation, founded the Mindfulness-Based Stress Reduction programme at the University of Massachusetts Medical School, presenting Buddhist-derived meditation in a secular, clinical form (Kabat-Zinn 1990). Mindfulness-Based Cognitive Therapy, developed by Zindel Segal, Mark Williams and John Teasdale, combined mindfulness with cognitive therapy to prevent relapse in recurrent depression, and has been recommended for that purpose by the National Institute for Health and Care Excellence in England. Mindfulness programmes are now widespread.

    20.3.3 What the evidence shows¶

    The research literature on meditation and yoga is large and uneven. A carefully conducted meta-analysis of randomised trials of mindfulness-based interventions for psychiatric disorders by Simon Goldberg and colleagues found that they were more effective than no treatment and comparable to other active treatments such as cognitive-behavioural therapy, with the strongest evidence for depression, pain and smoking (Goldberg et al. 2018). Neuroscientific studies have reported differences in brain activity and structure in experienced meditators, such as the high-amplitude gamma-band activity found by Antoine Lutz and Richard Davidson in Tibetan monks practising compassion meditation, and differences in cortical thickness reported by Sara Lazar and colleagues (Lutz et al. 2004; Lazar et al. 2005). Many studies, however, are small, lack adequate control conditions or rely on self-report; some practitioners report adverse experiences in intensive meditation; and a group of researchers led by Nicholas Van Dam has warned against the “hype” surrounding mindfulness and called for more rigorous definitions and methods (Van Dam et al. 2018). Systematic reviews of yoga for conditions such as chronic low back pain have found small to moderate benefits.

    The balanced conclusion is that meditation and yoga are useful practices for some conditions and for general well-being, on a par with other behavioural interventions, and that research on them is valuable. The evidence does not show that they cure serious disease, that they “prove” any metaphysical claim of the traditions, or that the ancient texts anticipated neuroscience. Nor does secular mindfulness reproduce the aims of the Buddhist path, which seeks liberation rather than stress reduction, a point made by Buddhist critics of the mindfulness movement.

    20.3.4 Difficulties and context¶

    Two further points deserve mention. First, meditation is not free of risk. Classical texts in several traditions warn of the dangers of intensive practice — disturbing visions, “meditation sickness”, the mistaking of unusual experiences for liberation — and Aurobindo, as noted in Chapter 11, warned his disciples of the same. Modern researchers have begun to document challenging experiences reported by some practitioners, including anxiety, depersonalisation and the re-emergence of traumatic memories, particularly during intensive retreats; the scientific critique of hype in meditation research cited above calls for these effects to be monitored and reported (Van Dam et al. 2018). Second, the secular “mindfulness” taught in clinics, schools and companies has been criticised by some Buddhist teachers and scholars for detaching a technique from the ethical framework in which it was embedded — the precepts, the cultivation of compassion, the goal of liberation — and for adapting it to help people tolerate stressful working conditions rather than question them. Defenders reply that the techniques have value in their own right and that the people who learn them are free to explore their origins. The debate is a modern instance of a question that runs through this volume: what is lost, and what gained, when a practice is taken from one context and put to work in another?

    20.4 Āyurveda Today¶

    20.4.1 From classical system to modern institution¶

    The classical Āyurveda of the Carakasaṃhitā, the Suśrutasaṃhitā and Vāgbhaṭa’s Aṣṭāṅgahṛdaya is described in Volume 13; Dominik Wujastyk’s anthology is a good introduction to the texts (Wujastyk 2003). Its colonial history is outlined below. Independent India recognised Āyurveda, Unani, Siddha and Homoeopathy as systems of medicine with their own colleges and registration; a Department of Indian Systems of Medicine and Homoeopathy, later AYUSH (Āyurveda, Yoga and Naturopathy, Unani, Siddha and Homoeopathy), became a full ministry in 2014. A large Āyurvedic industry has grown up, and Āyurveda has been marketed abroad, often in forms adapted to Western wellness culture (Wujastyk and Smith 2008).

    The relation between the systems in India’s public health service has been a recurring policy question. Committees appointed before and after independence debated whether Indian medicine should be integrated with biomedicine in a single course of training, developed separately in “pure” form, or confined to a supporting role; the answers changed with each generation of policy. The National Rural Health Mission, launched in 2005, sought to “mainstream” AYUSH by locating practitioners in primary health centres, partly to fill gaps in rural staffing. Proponents argued that this made use of a large trained workforce and of systems familiar to patients; critics, including many biomedical doctors, objected to AYUSH practitioners prescribing modern drugs without adequate training, a practice that several state governments have permitted and courts have repeatedly examined. The debate is not about whether Āyurveda has value, but about how a health system should combine practitioners trained in different and partly incompatible frameworks while protecting patients. Medical anthropologists have noted that patients themselves have long combined systems pragmatically, consulting different practitioners for different complaints, and that a realistic policy must start from that practice.

    20.4.2 The classical theory in brief¶

    The classical system is set out in the compendia attributed to Caraka (redacted c. 100 BCE–200 CE and completed by Dṛḍhabala in the fourth or fifth century) and Suśruta (in its present form by c. 500 CE), and in the Aṣṭāṅgahṛdaya of Vāgbhaṭa (c. 600 CE), which became the most widely used textbook (Wujastyk 2003). Health is understood as the balance of three bodily “faults” (doṣa) — wind (vāta), bile (pitta) and phlegm (kapha) — which, when disturbed, cause disease; of seven bodily tissues (dhātu), from chyle and blood to semen, each formed from the one before by a process of “cooking”; and of the digestive fire (agni) on which the whole process depends. Diagnosis attends to the patient’s constitution, the season, the place, the stage of the disease and its signs. Treatment includes diet and regimen, which the texts place first; medicines, mostly of plant origin in the classical compendia, with mineral preparations becoming prominent later; purification procedures; and, in Suśruta, surgery. The texts also contain extended reflections on the method of medical reasoning and on the qualities of a good physician. Like the humoral medicine of Galen with which it is often compared, this is a coherent and sophisticated system of explanation; and like Galenic humoral theory, its explanatory concepts do not correspond to the entities of modern physiology. This is why the evaluation of Āyurveda today proceeds mainly through testing particular treatments.

    20.4.3 Āyurveda under colonial rule¶

    The colonial history of Āyurveda is more complicated than a story of simple suppression. In its early decades the East India Company employed Indian practitioners, and from 1822 to 1835 the Native Medical Institution in Calcutta trained Indian students in both Western and Indian medicine, with lectures on Āyurvedic texts at the Sanskrit College. After 1835, in line with Macaulay’s policy (Chapter 8), state support was confined to Western medicine, and Indian practitioners were excluded from official medical institutions. Āyurvedic physicians responded by organising themselves: they founded associations and journals, established colleges, standardised and printed the classical texts, and set up pharmaceutical firms that manufactured remedies on a commercial scale. In doing so they also changed Āyurveda, adopting anatomy and other elements of Western medicine and turning a diverse craft into a professional system with a defined curriculum. The debates of this period — between “pure” and “integrated” Āyurveda, and over whether its practitioners should be regulated by the state — continue today (Wujastyk and Smith 2008).

    20.4.4 Evidence¶

    Some Āyurvedic herbs and formulations have been tested in clinical trials, and a few have shown promise for particular conditions; most trials, however, have been small and of limited quality, and the evidence base is far weaker than promotional literature suggests. Turmeric illustrates the difficulty. Curcumin, its main pigment, has been the subject of thousands of laboratory papers reporting effects on many biological targets; a critical review by medicinal chemists concluded that curcumin is unstable and poorly absorbed, behaves as a “promiscuous” compound that interferes with many laboratory assays, and has not been shown in well-designed clinical trials to be effective for any condition (Nelson et al. 2017). Traditional preparations combining turmeric with other ingredients may have different properties, but these too need to be tested rather than assumed.

    Researchers have also investigated whether the Āyurvedic classification of constitutions (prakṛti) by the predominance of vāta, pitta and kapha corresponds to measurable biological differences. Studies led by Mitali Mukerji and colleagues at the Institute of Genomics and Integrative Biology in Delhi reported differences in gene expression and genetic variants between small groups of people classified as of extreme constitutional types (Prasher et al. 2008; Govindaraj et al. 2015). These are interesting preliminary findings in a young field. They do not validate the doṣa theory as a whole, and claims that genomics has “proved” Āyurveda go far beyond the evidence.

    The Chinese experience offers a useful comparison. The Chinese scientist Tu Youyou, searching classical Chinese medical texts for leads against malaria, isolated artemisinin from sweet wormwood and received the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine in 2015. Her success came from treating the traditional literature as a source of hypotheses to be tested by the methods of modern pharmacology, not as a body of proven cures.

    20.4.5 Safety¶

    The most serious problem in modern Āyurveda is safety. Classical Āyurveda, and especially the alchemical tradition of rasaśāstra (Volume 14), prescribes preparations of mercury, lead, arsenic and other metals, which are said to be rendered safe by purification and incineration. Studies of products sold in the United States found that about one in five Āyurvedic products bought in Boston-area shops, and a similar proportion of products sold over the internet, contained detectable lead, mercury or arsenic, some at levels that could result in intakes exceeding safety limits (Saper et al. 2004, 2008). Cases of lead poisoning linked to Āyurvedic medicines have been reported in many countries. Herbal products can also interact with prescription drugs and cause liver injury.

    Health and Safety Note

    Āyurvedic and other traditional preparations can contain toxic metals (lead, mercury, arsenic) and can interact with prescribed medicines. Do not take metal-based (bhasma or rasa) preparations, and do not use any traditional remedy in place of diagnosis and treatment by a qualified physician, especially for serious illness, during pregnancy or for children. Inform your doctor of any herbal products you take. Nothing in this volume is medical advice.

    20.4.6 Research institutions¶

    India has built a substantial public apparatus for research in its traditional medical systems. Research councils for Āyurveda, Unani, Siddha, homoeopathy and yoga and naturopathy were established as separate bodies in 1978, when an earlier combined council was divided; the Central Council for Research in Ayurvedic Sciences runs a network of institutes and conducts clinical, pharmacological and literary research, including the editing of classical texts. An All India Institute of Ayurveda, modelled on the All India Institute of Medical Sciences, opened in New Delhi in 2017. Since 2009 clinical trials conducted in India, including trials of AYUSH interventions, have been required to be registered in advance with the Clinical Trials Registry–India, a measure intended to reduce selective reporting of results. The quality of the research produced by this apparatus varies: some studies meet international standards, while many are small, unblinded or poorly reported, and systematic reviews have repeatedly called for larger and better-designed trials. The institutions provide the means for rigorous evaluation; whether they are used for that purpose or for promotion is the question on which the credibility of the traditional systems will depend.

    20.4.7 Why testing is difficult, and why it is still necessary¶

    Defenders of Āyurveda sometimes argue that the randomised controlled trial is unsuited to a system that treats each patient individually according to constitution, season and stage of disease, often with complex multi-ingredient preparations and changes of diet and regimen. The objection has some force: a trial that gives a single standardised product to every patient tests something different from what a classical physician does. But the difficulty is not unique to Āyurveda — psychotherapy, surgery and nutrition present similar problems — and methods exist to address it, including pragmatic trials of whole treatment packages, trials in which the practitioner individualises treatment within each arm, and careful observational studies. What the objection cannot justify is exemption from testing altogether. A claim that a treatment works is a claim about outcomes, and outcomes can be measured. Respect for the tradition is best shown by taking its claims seriously enough to test them.

    20.4.8 Āyurveda and COVID-19¶

    The COVID-19 pandemic exposed both the potential and the dangers of Āyurveda’s place in public health. The AYUSH ministry issued advisories recommending traditional practices to support immunity and sponsored trials of some formulations. In June 2020 the company Patanjali Ayurved announced a product, Coronil, as a cure for COVID-19; the ministry asked it to stop advertising the claim until the evidence had been examined, and the episode drew sharp criticism from medical bodies. The lesson is the same as in other cases: traditional remedies may be worth testing, but claims of cure must wait for the tests.

    20.4.9 Other systems and healing traditions¶

    Āyurveda is not the only traditional medicine in India. Siddha medicine, practised mainly in Tamil Nadu and attributed to the legendary siddhar adepts, shares many concepts with Āyurveda but has its own Tamil literature and makes extensive use of mineral and metallic preparations, to which the safety concerns above apply equally. Unani medicine, of Graeco-Islamic origin, is discussed in Chapter 16. Sowa-Rigpa, the Tibetan system practised in the Himalayan regions and among Tibetan exiles, was recognised by the Government of India in 2010. Its principal text, the Four Tantras (rGyud bzhi), was compiled in Tibet in the twelfth century and combines Āyurvedic doctrine, received through translations of Vāgbhaṭa, with Chinese and Tibetan elements; the Men-Tsee-Khang, the Tibetan Medical and Astrological Institute established at Dharamsala in 1961, trains practitioners in exile. The history of Sowa-Rigpa is itself an example of the transmission and transformation of Indian knowledge across cultures, comparable to the passage of Indian astronomy into the Islamic world (Chapter 19). Homoeopathy, a German system of the early nineteenth century, is also included under AYUSH; systematic reviews by medical bodies have found no reliable evidence that its remedies work better than placebo.

    Naturopathy, now grouped with yoga under AYUSH, is a European import that acquired Indian roots. The “nature cure” movements of nineteenth-century Germany, associated with figures such as Vincenz Priessnitz, Sebastian Kneipp and Louis Kuhne, prescribed water treatments, diet, fasting, fresh air and sunlight. Gandhi read Kuhne and Adolf Just in South Africa, practised nature cure on himself and his family throughout his life, and in 1946 founded a nature-cure centre at Uruli Kanchan near Pune, intended for the village poor. Indian naturopaths later combined these methods with yoga and with Āyurvedic dietary ideas. The history is a small example of the global circulation of health practices: what is now presented as traditional Indian medicine includes a German system adopted by the most famous of Indian nationalists.

    Much healing in India, as elsewhere, takes place outside any formal system: at the shrines of Sufi saints, at temples famous for curing affliction by spirits, through folk healers, midwives and bonesetters. The psychoanalyst Sudhir Kakar’s study of such healing — at the Bālājī temple of Mehndipur in Rajasthan, among Sufi healers and among tantric practitioners — compared it with Western psychotherapy and argued that it addresses real psychological suffering through culturally meaningful symbols (Kakar 1982). Such healing deserves respect as a cultural practice and study as a human phenomenon; it is not a substitute for treatment of serious mental or physical illness.

    Unani Graeco-Islamic medicine (Galen, Ibn Sīnā), brought to India from the 13th century four humours; developed in India by physicians such as the family of Ajmal Khan
    Siddha Tamil texts attributed to the siddhars shares concepts with Āyurveda; extensive use of mineral and metallic preparations
    Sowa-Rigpa Tibetan Four Tantras (12th century) draws on Āyurveda via Vāgbhaṭa; recognised in 2010
    Homoeopathy Samuel Hahnemann, Germany (from 1796) a European system; no reliable evidence of effects beyond placebo

    20.4.10 Regulation¶

    Indian law has long restricted misleading medical claims. The Drugs and Magic Remedies (Objectionable Advertisements) Act of 1954 prohibits advertisements claiming to cure a list of serious diseases, and the regulation of Āyurvedic, Siddha and Unani drugs has been progressively strengthened, including a pharmacovigilance programme to report adverse effects. Enforcement has been uneven. In 2023–24 the Supreme Court of India, hearing a petition by the Indian Medical Association, censured a prominent Āyurvedic company for advertisements claiming cures for chronic diseases and required public apologies. Promoting traditional medicine and protecting the public are not always in harmony, and the credibility of the traditional systems themselves depends on independent regulation.

    20.4.11 Intellectual property and biopiracy¶

    The value of traditional knowledge has also been contested in law. In 1995 a United States patent was granted for the use of turmeric in wound healing; the Indian Council of Scientific and Industrial Research challenged it with evidence from Sanskrit texts and earlier publications, and it was revoked in 1997. A European patent on a fungicidal use of neem was revoked in 2000. To make such challenges easier, India created the Traditional Knowledge Digital Library, which translates formulations from classical texts into a searchable database available to patent examiners. The World Health Organization’s strategy on traditional medicine calls for the integration of safe and effective traditional practices into health systems, and in 2022 the WHO and the Government of India agreed to establish a Global Centre for Traditional Medicine at Jamnagar, Gujarat. International recognition of this kind is welcome; it does not substitute for evidence of safety and effectiveness for each practice and product.

    20.5 Assessment¶

    Yoga, meditation and Āyurveda show the best and the worst of the global life of Indian knowledge. At their best, they offer practices and hypotheses that have been carefully tested and found useful, and they have given millions of people a means of health and reflection. At their worst, they have been sold with false claims of antiquity, of scientific proof and of cures, and some products have harmed their users. A responsible synthesis takes from the tradition what can be shown to be valuable, describes the rest as history, and is honest about the difference.
