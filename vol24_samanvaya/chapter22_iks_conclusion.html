    Chapter 22: Indian Knowledge Systems Today: Samanvaya Reconsidered¶

    ā no bhadrāḥ kratavo yantu viśvataḥ
    May auspicious intentions come to us from every side.
    — Ṛgveda 1.89.1

    22.1 The “Indian Knowledge Systems” Movement¶

    22.1.1 Background¶

    This series is itself part of a movement. Since the late twentieth century, and with growing official support in the twenty-first, scholars, educators and governments in India have sought to recover and teach what are now called “Indian Knowledge Systems” (IKS): the sciences, arts, philosophies and practical skills developed in the subcontinent before and outside the colonial system of education. The movement responds to a real history. Macaulay’s Minute of 1835 (Chapter 8) expressed a contempt for Indian learning that shaped colonial education, and the institutions of traditional learning — the Sanskrit pāṭhaśālās, the madrasas, the guilds of craftsmen — lost their patronage and much of their standing. In the 1970s and 1980s the Gandhian historian Dharampal used British records to argue that India had possessed in the eighteenth century a widespread system of indigenous schools and a range of technologies, from inoculation against smallpox to the making of steel, that colonial rule had undermined (Dharampal 1971, 1983). Ashis Nandy’s The Intimate Enemy analysed the psychological effects of colonialism on the colonised (Nandy 1983), and historians of the “subaltern” and post-colonial schools, such as Partha Chatterjee and Dipesh Chakrabarty, questioned the assumption that European categories are universal (Chatterjee 1986; Chakrabarty 2000).

    22.1.2 Policy¶

    India’s National Education Policy of 2020 gave the movement official form. It calls for the incorporation of “Indian Knowledge Systems”, including tribal and indigenous knowledge, into the curriculum at all levels, the promotion of classical languages and of instruction in the mother tongue, and multidisciplinary education (Ministry of Human Resource Development, Government of India 2020). An IKS Division was established in the Ministry of Education in 2020 to fund research and develop courses, and universities have introduced compulsory and elective courses in Indian knowledge; a widely used textbook for engineering students was published in 2022 (Mahadevan et al. 2022). The National Mission for Manuscripts, established in 2003, has surveyed and catalogued manuscript collections across the country, whose total size is estimated in the millions; digital projects in India and abroad — among them the Sanskrit Heritage platform developed by Gérard Huet at INRIA, the GRETIL archive at Göttingen and the SARIT library of Indic texts — have made thousands of texts searchable and developed tools for the computational analysis of Sanskrit.

    22.1.3 The debate¶

    The IKS movement has been welcomed by many and criticised by many. Supporters argue that it corrects a colonial imbalance, gives students access to their own intellectual heritage and recovers knowledge of real value in fields from mathematics to ecology. Critics argue that some of what is taught under its name repeats the myths and priority claims discussed in Chapter 19; that it tends to identify Indian knowledge with Sanskrit and with the upper-caste Hindu tradition, neglecting Tamil, Persian and the vernaculars, Buddhist, Jain, Islamic and Sikh learning, and the knowledge of Dalit, Adivasi and artisan communities; and that it can serve a nationalist politics more than a scholarly purpose. Both sides are describing something real. Whether the movement succeeds as education will depend on whether it adopts the standards of evidence and argument that its best practitioners — Plofker, Matilal and the scholars cited throughout this series — have shown to be possible. The official initiative launched in 2020, and the arguments for and against it, are examined in Section 22.2.

    22.1.4 A longer history of institutions¶

    The IKS movement is often presented as the first attempt to bring Indian learning into modern institutions. In fact there is a long institutional history. The East India Company founded the Calcutta Madrasa in 1781 and the Sanskrit College at Benares in 1791 to train scholars of Islamic and Hindu law; the Asiatic Society of Bengal was founded in 1784 (Chapter 8); the Sanskrit College of Calcutta followed in 1824. The Poona Sanskrit College, opened in 1821 from funds once used by the Peshwas for the patronage of Brahmin scholars, later became Deccan College, a centre of Indological and linguistic research. Indian initiatives followed: the Bhandarkar Oriental Research Institute at Pune (1917), the Oriental Research Institute at Mysore, the Adyar Library of the Theosophical Society (1886), the Sarasvati Mahal Library at Thanjavur, and in independent India the Sanskrit universities and the regional manuscript libraries. Many of these institutions did outstanding work — the Pune critical edition of the Mahābhārata is the best known example — and many have struggled for funds and successors. A new movement would do well to build on them rather than to start afresh.

    22.2 The IKS Initiative Since 2020: Promise and Criticism¶

    The phrase “Indian Knowledge Systems” was in use before the policy of 2020; a two-volume collection with that title, edited by Kapil Kapoor and Avadhesh Kumar Singh for the Indian Institute of Advanced Study at Shimla, appeared in 2005 (Kapoor and Singh 2005). What changed after 2020 was the scale of official support, and with it the scale of the debate. This section describes the initiative and sets out the strongest form of the case made for it and of the objections raised against it, since both are part of the history this volume records.

    22.2.1 What the initiative does¶

    The IKS Division, housed at the All India Council for Technical Education under the Ministry of Education, funds centres for the study of Indian knowledge in universities and technical institutes, research projects, internships for students, programmes for training teachers and the preparation of course materials. In 2023 the University Grants Commission issued guidelines for incorporating Indian knowledge into higher education, which recommended that students in undergraduate and postgraduate programmes take courses in Indian knowledge amounting to at least five per cent of their total credits, at least half of them related to their main discipline, and that undergraduates begin with a foundational course introducing the field (University Grants Commission 2023). The guidelines list subjects ranging from mathematics, astronomy, medicine, architecture and metallurgy to linguistics, aesthetics, philosophy, governance and agriculture. National curriculum frameworks for schools have adopted similar aims. Universities have responded unevenly: some have created substantial programmes with qualified staff, others have added short courses taught from secondary compilations.

    22.2.2 The case for the initiative¶

    Its supporters make several arguments, and the strongest of them deserve to be stated fully. The first is the argument from intellectual freedom that K. C. Bhattacharya made a century ago (Chapter 15): an educated Indian who knows Euclid and Descartes but has never read Āryabhaṭa, Pāṇini or the Tirukkuṟaḷ has been given a narrower education than he or she deserves, and the narrowness is a legacy of a colonial judgement that Indian learning had nothing to teach. The second is the argument from neglect. Manuscripts remain uncatalogued and unedited, the classical languages are taught in fewer places each decade, and the traditional scholars who could read technical texts are ageing without successors; money and institutional recognition are preconditions for reversing this. The third is pedagogical: students of engineering, medicine or management who meet the history of their own fields in Indian sources may learn that science and scholarship are human, historical and plural enterprises, and not only Western ones. The fourth concerns living knowledge: the policy explicitly includes tribal and local knowledge, and the rights of communities that hold it (Section 22.7). On this view, the errors that critics point to are reasons to do the work better, not reasons to abandon it.

    These arguments have named scholarly advocates. Kapil Kapoor and Avadhesh Kumar Singh’s collection presented the classical disciplines — grammar, poetics, logic, the sciences and the arts — as systematic bodies of knowledge (śāstra) with their own concepts and methods, to be studied as knowledge and not only as cultural heritage (Kapoor and Singh 2005). Dharampal’s use of British records to document eighteenth-century schooling and technology, mentioned above, gave the movement its historical charter. A more radical argument comes from the comparative study of cultures. S. N. Balagangadhara, in The Heathen in His Blindness (1994), argued that “religion” as a category was formed within Christian theology and that Western scholarship, by looking for religions in Asia, created “Hinduism” in its own image; Indian traditions, on his view, must be described in categories drawn from their own practice (Balagangadhara 1994). Rajiv Malhotra’s Being Different (2011), the most widely read popular statement of this position, contrasted what he called the “integral unity” of the dharmic traditions with the history-centred “synthetic unity” of the Abrahamic West, and urged Indians to examine Western thought from their own standpoint rather than only being examined by it (Malhotra 2011). The textbook of Mahadevan and his colleagues translates such arguments into a university course (Mahadevan et al. 2022), and Upadhyaya’s Integral Humanism (Chapter 9) stands behind the political version of the same claim.

    22.2.3 The objections¶

    The critics’ objections also take several forms. The first concerns content. Some textbooks, lectures and popular materials circulated under the IKS label repeat the claims examined in Chapter 19 — ancient aircraft and weapons, “Vedic” anticipations of modern physics, invented verses and inflated statistics. Episodes before 2020, such as a paper on ancient Indian aviation presented at the Indian Science Congress in 2015, and claims made at the Congress of 2019 that the hundred Kaurava brothers of the Mahābhārata had been born by stem-cell and test-tube techniques, which scientists’ organisations publicly rejected, had already made many scientists wary. The second concerns scope: the objection, noted above, that “Indian” knowledge is too often equated with Sanskrit and Hindu learning, leaving out Persian and Urdu, Pali and Prakrit, Tamil and the regional languages, Islamic, Christian and Sikh learning and the knowledge of Dalit and Adivasi communities. The third concerns capacity: few universities have teachers able to read the sources in their languages, and a compulsory requirement may be met with courses that teach slogans rather than texts. The fourth concerns autonomy and purpose. Some academics have described compulsory credits as a form of indoctrination, and have argued that the initiative uses the legitimate aim of decolonising the curriculum to promote a particular cultural nationalism.

    These objections, too, have a scholarly literature, and it is wider than any one author. Meera Nanda has argued that the “Vedic science” literature uses the language of postcolonial critique to shelter religious nationalism from scientific scrutiny (Nanda 2003). Historians of science approach the question differently: Gyan Prakash has shown that the claim that modern science was already Hindu was itself a product of colonial-era nationalism, and Dhruv Raina has traced how the historiography of Indian science has been shaped by the needs of successive periods (Prakash 1999; Raina 2003). The astrophysicist Jayant Narlikar, who has written appreciatively of the real achievements of Indian astronomy and mathematics, has criticised astrology and inflated claims of Vedic science as damaging to them (Narlikar 2003), and specialists such as Kim Plofker have shown in practice how the history of Indian science can be written without either belittlement or inflation (Chapter 19). The argument about the categories of Indian thought is itself contested among scholars: Sheldon Pollock’s account of a Sanskrit culture that lost its creative force in the early modern period provoked Malhotra’s reply in The Battle for Sanskrit (2016), while Jonardon Ganeri’s study of early modern Navya-Nyāya argued, on the evidence of the texts, for a vigorous “new reason” in the same centuries (Pollock 2001; Malhotra 2016; Ganeri 2011). A reader who follows these exchanges will find that the sharpest disagreements are not between Indian and foreign scholars, but between different ways of reading the evidence, on both sides.

    22.2.4 Judging the outcome¶

    Neither side’s case settles the matter in advance, because the outcome depends on what is actually taught. The initiative can be judged by the same tests that this series has applied to every subject. Are students reading primary sources, in reliable editions and translations, with dates and uncertainties stated? Are claims about health, agriculture or technology tested, and are negative results reported? Do the courses include the full range of Indian languages, religions and communities, and the critics of the tradition as well as its admirers? Is the work open to review and criticism by specialists, including those outside India? The principles for teaching set out in Section 22.6 are offered in that spirit. Some of the best work described in this chapter (Section 22.10) was done long before the initiative existed, by scholars in India and abroad who held themselves to exactly these standards; whether the initiative builds on that work or bypasses it will decide whether it is remembered as a recovery of Indian learning or as another chapter in the history of its misuse.

    22.3 Who Owns the Past?¶

    The question of access runs through the history of Indian knowledge. For most of that history, Vedic learning was restricted to twice-born men, and much technical knowledge was held within particular castes and families. Modern India has changed this in ways that would have been unthinkable to the authors of the Dharmaśāstras. Sanskrit is taught in schools and universities to anyone who chooses it; women study and teach the Veda in institutions founded for the purpose; Dalit and other non-Brahmin scholars occupy chairs of Sanskrit and Indian philosophy. Some state governments have trained and appointed priests from non-Brahmin castes, including Dalits, to temples under their administration — in Kerala, where the Travancore Devaswom Board made such appointments in 2017, and in Tamil Nadu after long litigation. These changes have been resisted as well as welcomed, and they remain incomplete.

    Access also has a modern form. Digitisation and open publication can make manuscripts and texts available to anyone with an internet connection, but the competence to read them — in Sanskrit, Persian, classical Tamil or Old Kannada — is scarce and unevenly distributed. A genuinely inclusive programme for Indian knowledge would invest in that competence across communities and languages, rather than in slogans about the past.

    22.4 Many Languages, Many Classics¶

    Indian knowledge was produced in many languages, and any programme to recover it must reckon with that fact. Sanskrit was the most widely used language of learning across the subcontinent for most of the first and early second millennia CE, a “cosmopolitan” language in Sheldon Pollock’s analysis, used by Buddhists, Jains and Brahmins alike and spreading across Southeast Asia (Pollock 2006). But the Buddhist canon of the Theravāda was preserved in Pali, the Jain canon in Ardhamāgadhī and other Prakrits, and a great literature of poetry, ethics and grammar in classical Tamil. From the thirteenth century Persian became a language of administration, history, poetry and science across much of the subcontinent, and Indo-Persian learning produced dictionaries, histories, medical works and translations from Sanskrit (Chapter 16). From the end of the first millennium the regional languages — Kannada, Telugu, Marathi, Bengali, Hindi and others — developed literatures of their own, often first in the hands of devotional poets who deliberately addressed people excluded from Sanskrit learning. Arabic was the language of Islamic jurisprudence and theology; Urdu and later English became languages of modern Indian thought.

    The Government of India’s designation of “classical languages” reflects this plurality in part. Tamil was the first to be so designated, in 2004, followed by Sanskrit (2005), Kannada and Telugu (2008), Malayalam (2013) and Odia (2014); in 2024 Marathi, Pali, Prakrit, Assamese and Bengali were added. The inclusion of Pali and Prakrit, the languages of the Buddhist and Jain canons, is a welcome recognition that Indian classical learning is not only Brahmanical. Persian and Arabic, though they were for centuries languages of Indian learning, are not on the list: official definitions of the “Indian” heritage are themselves choices.

    22.5 Translation as Samanvaya¶

    If there is a single practice that embodies the ideal of samanvaya in the history traced by this volume, it is translation. Buddhist texts were translated from Indian languages into Chinese and Tibetan over a thousand years, with careful attention to terminology and method; the Pañcatantra passed through Middle Persian and Arabic into most of the languages of Europe (Chapter 21); al-Bīrūnī translated Patañjali and the Sāṃkhya into Arabic; Dārā Shikoh’s scholars translated the Upaniṣads into Persian, and their version, rendered into Latin, introduced the Upaniṣads to Europe (Chapter 8). Indian poets retold the epics in every regional language, not as literal translations but as new works, and the Malay, Javanese, Thai and Khmer versions continued the process abroad.

    Modern India has built institutions for translation among its own languages and between them and others. The Sahitya Akademi, the national academy of letters founded in 1954, translates literary works among the Indian languages, and the National Book Trust (1957) publishes translations for a general readership. Bilingual series have made classical texts available with facing translations: the Clay Sanskrit Library (2005–2009) published some fifty-six volumes of Sanskrit literature, and the Murty Classical Library of India, launched in 2015 at Harvard University Press, publishes texts in Sanskrit, Pali, Prakrit, Persian, Tamil, Telugu, Kannada, Bengali, Hindi, Punjabi, Urdu and other languages with English translations. Such projects do the slow work that a knowledge movement needs. They also show what genuine synthesis requires: competence in more than one language, attention to difference, and the patience to let a text say what it says before deciding what it means for us.

    22.6 Teaching Indian Knowledge Honestly¶

    The introduction of courses on Indian knowledge into schools and universities raises practical questions of pedagogy, and the rules of comparison set out at the beginning of this volume (Section 1.11) translate readily into the classroom. Students learn more from sources than from slogans: a class that reads, in reliable translation with dates and context, a chapter of the Āryabhaṭīya, a dialogue from the Upaniṣads, a passage of the Tirukkuṟaḷ and a page of al-Bīrūnī will learn more than one that memorises claims of priority. Students should meet the arguments as well as the conclusions — the Cārvāka objection to inference, the Buddhist objection to the self, the Mīmāṃsaka objection to God and the Advaitin’s reply (Chapter 4) — and be asked to judge for themselves. They should meet the whole of the past: Buddhist, Jain, Islamic, Sikh and Christian learning, the regional languages, the knowledge of artisans, farmers, women and Adivasi communities, and the critics of the tradition, Phule, Ambedkar and Periyar among them, as part of the Indian intellectual heritage. And every claim should come with an account of how it is known, from which texts, inscriptions or finds and with what uncertainty, and be set where possible beside the achievements of China, the Islamic world, Greece and Europe, since comparison teaches both what was distinctive and the habit of fairness.

    22.7 Knowledge Beyond Texts¶

    Much of the knowledge discussed in Volumes 15, 17 and 23 of this series — of crops and seeds, forests and medicinal plants, weaving and metalwork, water management and animal husbandry — is held by farmers, artisans and tribal communities rather than in texts. Recent law has begun to recognise these communities as holders of knowledge and rights. The Geographical Indications of Goods Act (1999) protects products such as Darjeeling tea and Banarasi silk associated with particular places; the Biological Diversity Act (2002) requires that communities share in the benefits when their biological resources and knowledge are used, and provides for local People’s Biodiversity Registers; and the Forest Rights Act (2006) recognises the rights of forest-dwelling communities, many of them Adivasi, to the forests they have traditionally used. These measures express in law an idea that the IKS movement often states in rhetoric: that traditional knowledge is valuable and belongs to those who created it. Their implementation has been uneven, and conflicts between conservation, development and community rights continue. An “Indian Knowledge Systems” curriculum that taught Sanskrit treatises while ignoring the living knowledge and the rights of these communities would repeat the exclusions of the past.

    22.8 An Agenda for Research¶

    What would a serious programme of research on Indian knowledge systems involve? Its foundation would be editions and translations, since many important texts in Sanskrit, Persian, Tamil and other languages exist only in manuscript or in unreliable editions. On that foundation the history of Indian mathematics, astronomy, medicine and technology can be pursued with the methods that made the work of Pingree and Plofker authoritative: close reading, attention to dates and transmission, and comparison with other traditions. Knowledge recorded in the regional languages, in Persian and in oral tradition is less studied than Sanskrit learning and is disappearing faster. Where traditional knowledge makes claims about health, agriculture or the environment, those claims should be tested by the best available methods and the results reported whether positive or negative. Indian philosophical arguments should be studied as contributions to continuing debates, as Matilal, Ganeri and others have shown is possible. And the social history of knowledge — who produced, controlled and was excluded from it, by caste, gender, region and religion — belongs to the history of knowledge itself.

    22.9 Knowledge, Identity and the Nation¶

    Behind the debates about Indian knowledge lies a question about identity: whose heritage is it, and what does it mean to call it “Indian”? Several answers have been given in the history traced by this volume. For the Orientalists, the Indian heritage was above all the Sanskrit heritage, and its value lay in its antiquity. For Hindu reformers from Rammohun to Radhakrishnan, it was the Upaniṣadic and Vedāntic tradition, understood as a universal spirituality. For Hindu nationalists, it is the heritage of a Hindu nation defined by territory and ancestry. For Nehru, it was the composite inheritance of all the peoples who had lived in the subcontinent, Hindu, Buddhist, Jain, Muslim and others, layered like a palimpsest (Nehru 1946). For Ambedkar and the Dalit movement, it included above all the Buddha’s teaching and the long record of resistance to caste. For Dravidian and other regional movements, it is plural and centred in the regional languages.

    These answers are not merely academic. They determine which texts are taught, which languages are funded, which monuments are protected and which histories are remembered. A comparative study cannot choose among them on behalf of its readers. It can, however, insist on two things. First, the historical record does not support any account of Indian knowledge that excludes its Buddhist, Jain, Islamic, Sikh, Christian, tribal or regional components, or the contributions of women and of castes excluded from Sanskrit learning; whatever “Indian” means, it must include them. Second, the value of a piece of knowledge — a theorem, a medical treatment, an argument about the self — does not depend on the identity of the person who discovered it. Pride in the achievements of one’s ancestors is natural, but knowledge belongs to whoever can understand and use it. The mathematicians of Kerala, like Euclid or al-Khwārizmī, are part of the heritage of humanity.

    22.10 Examples of Good Practice¶

    The research agenda above is not hypothetical; much of it is already being pursued, in India and abroad, and a few examples show what careful work can achieve.

    The Kerala mathematics.

    The Malayalam Yuktibhāṣā, the main source for the demonstrations of the Kerala school (Chapter 19), was published in a critical edition with English translation and detailed mathematical notes by K. V. Sarma, K. Ramasubramanian, M. D. Srinivas and M. S. Sriram (2008). The edition makes it possible for any mathematician to follow the original arguments step by step, and it has done more for the reputation of Indian mathematics than any number of claims of priority.

    The census of the exact sciences.

    David Pingree’s Census of the Exact Sciences in Sanskrit (five volumes, 1970–1994) listed the authors and manuscripts of Sanskrit astronomy, astrology and mathematics, laying the foundation for the work of Plofker and others (Pingree 1981; Plofker 2009).

    Yoga and medicine.

    Research projects at European universities in the 2010s — among them the Haṭha Yoga Project at SOAS, London, and a project on the modern history of Āyurveda and yoga at Vienna — have edited and translated previously unpublished texts and studied their modern transformations, in collaboration with Indian scholars and practitioners (Mallinson and Singleton 2017).

    The epics.

    The critical editions of the Mahābhārata (Pune, 1919–1966) and of the Rāmāyaṇa (Baroda, 1960–1975), produced by teams of Indian scholars from hundreds of manuscripts, remain the indispensable basis for the study of both epics, whatever their limitations.

    Living knowledge.

    Community biodiversity registers, participatory studies of seed varieties and documentation projects in tribal languages, many of them led by Indian non-governmental organisations and universities, show how the knowledge of farmers and forest communities can be recorded with their consent and to their benefit (Volumes 15 and 23).

    These examples share the features this series has tried to embody: they work from sources, state their methods, collaborate across institutions and countries, and let the evidence decide.

    22.11 Comparisons from Other Countries¶

    India is not alone in seeking to recover and teach traditional knowledge, and the experience of other countries is instructive. China integrated Traditional Chinese Medicine into its health system after 1949, with parallel colleges and hospitals, extensive state-funded research and one Nobel Prize — Tu Youyou’s for artemisinin — as well as persistent controversy about the evidence for many treatments and the use of endangered animal products. In New Zealand, the knowledge of the Māori people (mātauranga Māori) has been incorporated into education and environmental management, and the legal personhood of the Whanganui River was negotiated with Māori communities (Chapter 21); proposals to teach it alongside science in schools provoked a public debate in 2021 about whether traditional knowledge and science are different kinds of knowledge. In Canada and Australia, indigenous knowledge has entered environmental management through formal partnerships with indigenous communities. Across Africa, “indigenous knowledge systems” have become a field of research and policy, with similar questions about evidence, ownership and the risk of romanticism.

    Three lessons emerge. First, traditional knowledge is most credible when its practical claims are tested rather than assumed. Second, the communities that hold it should be partners, not merely sources. Third, the relation between traditional knowledge and science should be discussed openly: traditional knowledge can complement science, inform it and supply it with questions, but it does not replace the methods by which empirical claims are established.

    22.12 Two Failures and How to Avoid Them¶

    22.12.1 Uncritical celebration¶

    The first failure is uncritical celebration: presenting every Indian text as wise, every claim as true and every modern discovery as anticipated. Its symptoms are familiar from the preceding chapters: invented or garbled verses, quotations attributed to scientists who never wrote them, statistics without sources, the ancient aeroplane, “Vedic mathematics”, the kalpa as the age of the Earth, the claim that “all religions are one” presented as a fact about the religions. It treats the tradition as a monument to be revered rather than a conversation to be joined, and it silently excludes everyone whom the monument does not commemorate — the Śūdra excluded from the Veda, the woman denied independence, the Muslim and the Sikh whose traditions do not fit, the Buddhist who rejects Hinduism.

    22.12.2 Colonial dismissal¶

    The second failure is dismissal: the assumption, inherited from Mill and Macaulay, that pre-modern Indian thought is myth and superstition, of interest only to antiquarians. It ignores the logic of Navya-Nyāya, the mathematics of the Kerala school, the grammar of Pāṇini, the medicine of Caraka and Suśruta, the ethics of the Mahābhārata, the aesthetics of Abhinavagupta, and a vast literature of argument that is as rigorous as any in the world. It also ignores the role of Indian scholars in the making of modern science and scholarship.

    22.12.3 The path between¶

    Between these failures lies the path that this series has tried to follow, whose rules are stated in Section 1.11 and need not be repeated here. They are not foreign impositions. Indian intellectual traditions developed their own exacting standards of evidence (pramāṇa), textual criticism and debate, and the best of their practitioners would have recognised them.

    22.13 How to Check a Claim¶

    Those rules can be made practical. Many of the errors that circulate in writing about Indian knowledge — verses that do not exist, quotations that no one wrote, statistics without a source — could have been caught by a few simple steps, which any student or teacher can follow.

    1. Find the source.

    A quotation from a Sanskrit text should come with a reference precise enough to find it: the text, the edition and the chapter and verse. If the reference is missing, or if it points to a text that does not contain the passage, the quotation should not be used. “As the Vedas say” is not a reference. For modern authors, the reference should give the book or article, with the page.

    2. Read the context.

    A verse that has been located should be read with the verses before and after it, and with a classical commentary if one exists. Many famous “quotations” change their meaning in context: the Ṛgvedic line “what is one, the inspired poets speak of in many ways” (1.164.46) concerns the names of the gods of the Vedic sacrifice, not the unity of all religions (see the end of this chapter); and the line “for the noble-minded, the whole earth is one family” (vasudhaiva kuṭumbakam), now widely used as a motto, is spoken in a fable of the Hitopadeśa (1.71) by a jackal who wants to be accepted as the friend of a deer he plans to eat (it also occurs, in a contemplative setting, in the late Mahā Upaniṣad, 6.71). Neither modern use is illegitimate, but the reader should know the difference between the source and the slogan.

    3. Date the source.

    A text should be dated by the best available scholarship, with the uncertainty stated. A claim that a discovery was made “in the Vedic period” on the evidence of a medieval commentary, or that a text is “ten thousand years old” on the evidence of tradition alone, should be rejected.

    4. Check the attribution.

    Quotations attributed to famous scientists and writers — Einstein, Oppenheimer, Schrödinger, Twain, Voltaire, Max Müller — are especially prone to invention. They should be traced to a primary source: a book, letter or recorded speech. If the earliest source is a website or a popular book of the 1970s, the attribution is probably false.

    5. Ask for the numbers.

    Statistics — of manuscripts, students, patents, markets, gene variants — need a source and a date. Round numbers repeated without a source (“nine million manuscripts”, “ten thousand students”) are usually legends.

    6. Distinguish analogy from history.

    A resemblance between an ancient idea and a modern theory is an analogy. It becomes history only if there is evidence of a causal connection, such as a translation, a citation or a teacher. The Kerala series and the Gregory–Newton series are a striking parallel; the transmission of Indian numerals to Europe is a documented history. The two kinds of claim must be kept apart.

    7. Prefer the specialist.

    On any technical question — the date of a text, the meaning of a term, the effect of a medicine — the work of specialists published in reviewed journals and academic presses should be preferred to popular summaries, including this one. The Further Reading lists at the end of each chapter point to such work.

    22.14 Questions for Further Study¶

    The comparative study of Indian traditions is far from finished, and the chapters of this volume leave many questions open. We still do not know exactly by which texts and persons Indian mathematics and astronomy travelled to the Islamic world and Europe, or what travelled in the other direction before the colonial period. We know far more about what learned texts taught about the cosmos, karma and rebirth than about what ordinary people believed, which inscriptions, vernacular literature and ethnography may yet reveal. The interaction of Persianate and Sanskrit learning in particular scholarly circles and households (Chapter 16) has only begun to be studied, as has the production and transmission of knowledge by women, Dalits, Adivasis and artisans who left few texts. Which traditional practices in medicine, agriculture and resource management have measurable benefits is an empirical question still largely unanswered. And the political history of Indian ideas of argument and toleration — how far they protected dissenters and minorities — and the reception of the modern thinkers in different regions and languages invite comparison with Chinese, Islamic, African and indigenous traditions as well as with European ones.

    22.15 Synthesis in Practice: Three Places¶

    General statements about synthesis are easily made; it is more instructive to look at particular places where people of different traditions have met.

    Ajmer.

    The shrine (dargāh) of the Chishtī saint Muʿīn al-Dīn at Ajmer in Rajasthan, who died in the 1230s, has been a place of pilgrimage for seven centuries. Akbar walked there on foot to give thanks for the birth of a son. Today it is visited by Muslims, Hindus, Sikhs and others, who offer cloths and flowers and ask for the saint’s intercession; its annual festival marks the anniversary of his death (ʿurs). The visitors do not thereby abandon their own religions, and Islamic reformers have criticised shrine practices that they regard as un-Islamic. The shrine is an example of shared devotion without doctrinal agreement.

    Sabarimala and Erumeli.

    Pilgrims to the hill shrine of Ayyappan at Sabarimala in Kerala traditionally stop at Erumeli to honour Vāvar, a Muslim warrior who, according to legend, became Ayyappan’s companion, and whose shrine stands in a mosque. The custom embodies a local story of friendship across communities. Sabarimala has also been the centre of a different kind of conflict: the Supreme Court’s decision in 2018 that the exclusion of women of menstruating age violated the Constitution provoked mass protests, and the question was referred to a larger bench. The same place thus displays both inter-religious accommodation and conflict between tradition and constitutional equality.

    Bodh Gaya.

    At the site of the Buddha’s awakening, temples and monasteries built by Buddhists from Thailand, Japan, Tibet, Bhutan, Sri Lanka, Myanmar and elsewhere surround the Mahābodhi temple, which is managed under the Act of 1949 by a committee of Buddhists and Hindus (Chapter 18). Ambedkarite Buddhists have campaigned for exclusive Buddhist control; Hindu pilgrims perform rites for their ancestors in the nearby town of Gaya. Here a single site belongs, in different ways, to a global Buddhist community, to Indian Buddhists with their own history, and to Hindus.

    These three places show what samanvaya has looked like in practice: neither the dissolution of differences nor permanent hostility, but negotiated coexistence, sustained by stories, customs and institutions, and always open to renegotiation.

    22.16 What This Volume Has Found¶

    It may be useful to summarise the main findings of the comparative chapters.

    Shared words, different meanings.

    Hindu, Buddhist and Jain traditions shared a large vocabulary — dharma, karma, saṃsāra, mokṣa, loka, kalpa, ahiṃsā — and a common stock of images, such as Meru and Jambudvīpa. But they used them in different ways: dharma as class duty, as the Buddha’s teaching and as a medium of motion; karma as moral potency, as intention and as matter; liberation as identity with Brahman, as the blowing out of craving and as the soul’s ascent to the top of the universe (Chapters 2–7).

    Argument, not consensus.

    The traditions argued with one another about the most basic questions — whether there is a self, whether there is a creator, whether the Veda is authoritative, whether caste is just, whether non-violence admits of exceptions — for more than two thousand years. The modern idea of a single, harmonious “Indian worldview” obscures the very thing that makes the Indian intellectual tradition remarkable.

    Encounters shaped self-understanding.

    The categories through which Indian traditions are now described were shaped by long encounters: with Persian scholarship at the Mughal court, with British and German Orientalists, with missionaries and Theosophists, with modern science (Chapter 8). Modern Hindu self-understanding, in particular, was formed by reformers who answered colonial critiques by reinterpreting the tradition, often around Advaita Vedānta (Chapters 9–11).

    The modern syntheses were positions.

    Vivekananda’s harmony of religions, Aurobindo’s evolutionary Vedānta, Gandhi’s truth and non-violence, Radhakrishnan’s religion of the spirit, Tagore’s religion of man, Iqbal’s Islamic personalism, Madanī’s composite nationalism and Ambedkar’s Navayāna Buddhism were all creative syntheses, and all were disputed — not least by one another, and by the academic philosophers who asked what freedom in ideas required (Chapters 10–15 and 17).

    India’s traditions are many.

    Islam and Indo-Persian learning, Sufism, the devotional poets, Sikhism, Christianity, Adivasi religions and the smaller communities are part of Indian intellectual history, and Buddhism and Jainism have their own modern histories; none of them is a footnote to Vedānta (Chapters 14 and 16–18).

    Global careers require honesty.

    Indian mathematics, yoga, meditation, Āyurveda, environmental movements and arts have entered the world’s common life. Their reputation is best served by accurate history and honest evidence, not by priority claims (Chapters 19–21).

    22.17 Samanvaya Reconsidered¶

    This volume began with the Ṛgvedic verse that “what is one, the inspired poets speak of in many ways” (1.164.46), and with the Brahmasūtra’s principle of samanvaya, the coherent reading of a body of texts. It is worth returning to both in closing.

    The Ṛgvedic verse, in its context, says that the poets call one divine reality by the names of several gods — Indra, Mitra, Varuṇa, Agni, Yama, Mātariśvan. It has become, in modern India, a motto of religious tolerance, cited in support of the view that all religions are paths to one truth. The verse can bear that reading, and it is a reading that has encouraged generosity. But it is a reading. The poet was speaking of the gods of the Vedic sacrifice, not of the religions of the world; and many Indians, from the Buddha to Ambedkar, from the Mīmāṃsakas to the Sikh Gurus, did not believe that the paths they rejected led to the goal they sought.

    The Brahmasūtra’s samanvaya was a discipline of interpretation: the effort to show that texts which seem to disagree have a coherent meaning. Its practitioners, from Śaṅkara to Madhva, did not agree on what that meaning was, and they said so, with arguments. Perhaps that is the most useful model for a synthesis today: not the declaration that everything is one, but the patient and argued attempt to understand how different things hold together, and where they do not.

    Aśoka’s twelfth rock edict, more than two thousand years old, asked the religious communities of his empire to restrain their speech about one another, to listen to one another’s teachings and to honour one another, arguing that whoever exalts his own sect by disparaging others damages it. It did not ask them to agree. A civilisation that can sustain such restraint and such listening, while continuing to argue, has achieved something rarer than uniformity. That, rather than the claim of a single “Indian worldview”, is the samanvaya this series has tried to describe.
