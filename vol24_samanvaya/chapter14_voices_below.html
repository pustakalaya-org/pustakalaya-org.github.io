    Chapter 14: Voices from Below: Anti-Caste Thinkers, Women Writers and Adivasi Movements¶

    na jaccā vasalo hoti, na jaccā hoti brāhmaṇo
    kammunā vasalo hoti, kammunā hoti brāhmaṇo
    Not by birth is one an outcaste, not by birth a Brahmin. By deeds one is an outcaste, by deeds a Brahmin.
    — Sutta Nipāta 1.7 (Vasala Sutta)

    14.1 Introduction¶

    The reformers and philosophers of the preceding chapters mostly argued about the Indian inheritance from within it, as heirs who wished to renew it. This chapter turns to thinkers who stood where the classical texts had placed those they excluded: the castes ranked lowest, women, and the forest and hill peoples who were not ranked at all. The chapter treats four anti-caste intellectuals — Jotirao Phule, Iyothee Thass, Narayana Guru and Periyar — and compares their counter-histories with Ambedkar’s; three women writers who asked who had made the rules they lived under; and the religious and political movements through which Adivasi communities, in central India and the North-East, answered missionaries, Hindu reformers and the state. The verse at the head of the chapter, from one of the oldest Buddhist collections, shows how old the argument against birth-rank is; its earlier history is told in Chapter 5.

    14.2 Anti-Caste Thought on Its Own Terms¶

    The reformers of the Brahmo, Prarthana and Arya Samajes (Chapter 9) argued about caste from within the Sanskrit tradition, usually by claiming that hereditary rank was a later corruption of a purer Vedic or Upaniṣadic past. A second current of modern thought, rooted in the communities that the tradition ranked lowest, argued against that tradition or from outside it. Its thinkers wrote mostly in Marathi, Tamil and Malayalam rather than in English, for audiences of cultivators, labourers and artisans, and they did not regard themselves as contributors to a Hindu synthesis. Gail Omvedt called them “anticaste intellectuals” and took the title of her study from Ravidās’s song of Begumpurā, the city without sorrow, where no one is ranked above another; she argued that, for all their differences, they shared the hope of such a society and a habit of reading the Indian past against its Brahmanical narrators (Omvedt 2008). Their predecessors, the Buddhist, Vīraśaiva and sant critics of birth-rank, are described in Chapter 5, and Ambedkar in Chapter 12. This section treats four of them as thinkers, not only as organisers of reform.

    14.2.1 Jotirao Phule: a counter-history¶

    Jotirao Phule (1827–1890) belonged to the Mālī caste of Pune, a Śūdra community of cultivators and market-gardeners. He was educated at a Scottish mission school, where he read Thomas Paine’s Rights of Man, and from 1848 he and his wife Savitribai opened the schools for girls, and then for children of untouchable castes, that are described in Volume 22 (Manas and Śikṣā). His importance for a comparative history is that he wrote one of the first systematic counter-histories of India from the standpoint of the lower castes (O’Hanlon 1985).

    Gulāmgirī (“Slavery”, 1873) is a dialogue in Marathi between Jotirao and a young questioner, Dhoṇḍībā, with an English preface; Phule dedicated it to “the good people of the United States” for their sacrifice in abolishing Black slavery, and so placed caste beside slavery as a single kind of injustice. Its argument takes up the Orientalist story of an Aryan migration (Chapter 8) and reverses its values. The Brahmins, in Phule’s account, descend from invaders who conquered the original inhabitants of the land, the ancestors of the Śūdras and Ati-Śūdras, and kept them subject by force and then by fraud. The Purāṇic avatāras become veiled memories of that conquest: Vāmana’s trick, by which the dwarf obtained three paces of land from the generous king Bali and then took the whole world, is the type of the Brahmin’s deceit, and Bali becomes the just king of the cultivators. Phule found confirmation in a Marathi saying recited at the Bali festival during Divāḷī, a wish that “troubles and sorrows go and Bali’s kingdom come”. The law-books and Purāṇas, on this reading, were composed to keep the conquered in ignorance, and the denial of education to Śūdras and women was the mechanism of their enslavement (Deshpande 2002).

    As history the racial part of this account is no better than the Aryan theories it inverts; modern scholarship does not support a conquest of Śūdras by Brahmins (see Volume 1 on the spread of Indo-Aryan languages). Its force lay elsewhere. Phule treated sacred narratives as records of power and asked who had written them and for whom, a question that the reformers who sought a purer Vedic past did not ask. He also gave the lower castes a collective identity and heroes of their own: his ballad (povāḍā) of 1869 presented Śivājī as a king of the peasants, not of Brahmins. In Śetkaryācā Asūḍ (“The Cultivator’s Whipcord”, written in 1883) he extended the analysis to the agrarian economy, describing how the peasant was squeezed by the Brahmin village accountant, the moneylender and the colonial revenue and forest departments together. His last work, Sārvajanik Satyadharma Pustak (published in 1891, after his death), sets out a universal ethical religion of a single creator, without priests or exclusive scriptures, in which men and women are equal; Phule applied the same scepticism to the revealed books of Christianity and Islam as to the Vedas.

    The Satyashodhak Samaj (“Truth-Seekers’ Society”, 1873) put these ideas into practice, conducting marriages and other rites in Marathi without Brahmin priests. Its successors formed the non-Brahmin movement of western India in the early twentieth century. Ambedkar dedicated Who Were the Shudras? (1946) to Phule’s memory, calling him the greatest Śūdra of modern India, and regarded him, with the Buddha and Kabīr, as one of his three teachers.

    14.2.2 Iyothee Thass and the Tamil Buddhist past¶

    Iyothee Thass (Ayōttitācar, 1845–1914), a practitioner of Siddha medicine and a Tamil scholar from the Paraiyar community of Madras, worked out the most elaborate counter-history of the south. Active from the 1880s in Paraiyar associations and journals, he concluded that the communities treated as untouchable in the Tamil country were not outcastes but the descendants of the land’s original Buddhists. In his account, Brahmins whom he called “false Brahmins” (vēṣa pirāmaṇar), Brahmins by dress and claim rather than by conduct, had destroyed Tamil Buddhism and degraded those who refused to abandon it; untouchability was the punishment of the faithful. He insisted that his people were “casteless Dravidians” and “original Tamils”, not a caste at the bottom of a Hindu hierarchy (Aloysius 1998).

    In 1898, with the help of the Theosophist Henry Steel Olcott, Thass and his associates received the Buddhist precepts in Ceylon and founded in Madras the Sakya Buddhist Society, which had branches among Tamil labourers as far away as the Kolar Gold Fields. From 1907 he edited a weekly, Oru Paicā Tamiḻaṉ (“The One-Paisa Tamilian”), soon renamed Tamiḻaṉ, in which he reread the Tirukkuṟaḷ, the verses attributed to Auvaiyār and popular festivals as residues of a Buddhist culture, and argued that Indra (Tamil Intiraṉ) had been in origin an epithet of the Buddha. His Buddhism, like Dharmapāla’s (Chapter 18), was rationalist and critical of ritual; unlike Dharmapāla’s, it was a Buddhism of caste memory, which G. Aloysius has called an “emancipatory identity”.

    Thass anticipated by nearly half a century Ambedkar’s thesis, in The Untouchables (1948), that the untouchables were descended from Buddhists who had refused Brahmin supremacy, and by nearly sixty years Ambedkar’s conversion; whether Ambedkar knew his work is not established. V. Geetha and S. V. Rajadurai have traced a line from Thass’s Tamil Buddhism to Periyar’s non-Brahmin radicalism (Geetha and Rajadurai 1998). After Thass’s death the society declined, and his writings were largely forgotten until Dalit scholars recovered them in the 1990s.

    14.2.3 Narayana Guru¶

    In Kerala, Sree Narayana Guru (1856–1928), born into the Ezhava community, which was regarded as polluting by the upper castes, became the leader of a movement of religious and social reform. In 1888 at Aruvippuram he consecrated an image of Śiva himself, defying the rule that only Brahmins could do so. According to the account preserved by his followers, when orthodox critics objected he replied that he had consecrated an Ezhava Śiva, not a Brahmin one. He wrote philosophical hymns in Sanskrit and Malayalam in an Advaitic idiom, but drew from them a radical conclusion summed up in his teaching of “one caste, one religion, one God for humanity”. The Sree Narayana Dharma Paripalana Yogam (1903) spread education and temple reform among Ezhavas and transformed Kerala society. Narayana Guru is an example of the Advaita tradition being used from below, against caste.

    His philosophical writing is more substantial than his reputation as a social reformer suggests. The Malayalam Ātmopadeśaśataka (“One Hundred Verses of Self-Instruction”) and the Sanskrit Darśanamālā (“Garland of Visions”) restate non-dualism in his own terms, and the short prayer Daivadaśaka (“Ten Verses to God”) is still recited in many Kerala homes and schools. In a brief Sanskrit poem on caste, the Jātinirṇaya (“Determining Kind”), he made an argument with a long Indian pedigree. In the Nyāya–Vaiśeṣika theory of universals, jāti means a natural kind, a universal shared by all members of a class. Humanness, he argued, is the jāti of human beings as cowness is of cows; since a Brahmin is born of a human being in the same way as a Paraiyar, there is only one human jāti. The argument is close to the Buddha’s in the Vāseṭṭha Sutta (Chapter 5), and it turns the technical vocabulary of the schools against the social meaning of the word.

    His later temples expressed the same thought in stone and glass. In some he installed a lamp or a tablet of moral precepts instead of an image, and at Kalavamkodam in 1927, according to the standard accounts, a mirror, so that the worshipper saw his own face where the deity should be. In 1924 he convened at his ashram at Aluva a conference of religions whose motto, inscribed at the venue, declared that the participants had met not to argue and win but to know and to make known. The movement he founded did not remain of one mind. His disciple Sahodaran K. Ayyappan organised in 1917 a shared meal of Ezhavas with Pulayas, who stood below them in the local hierarchy, and recast the Guru’s motto as “no caste, no religion, no God for humanity”; the poet Kumaran Āśān, the Yogam’s first general secretary, carried the Guru’s thought into Malayalam literature; and the Yogam itself became, as Filippo and Caroline Osella have shown, a vehicle for the social mobility of one community as much as for the abolition of caste (Osella and Osella 2000).

    14.2.4 Periyar¶

    E. V. Ramasamy (1879–1973), called Periyar, “the great one”, left the Indian National Congress in 1925 to found the Self-Respect Movement in the Tamil country. He attacked Brahmin dominance, caste, the subordination of women and religion itself as instruments of oppression, promoted “self-respect marriages” without Brahmin priests, and championed Tamil identity against what he saw as the imposition of Sanskrit and Hindi. His rationalist atheism and his critique of the epics, especially the Rāmāyaṇa, made him the most radical opponent of Brahmanical tradition in modern India, and the Dravidian parties that descend from his movement have governed Tamil Nadu since 1967.

    Periyar’s thought is best read as a theory of dignity. “Self-respect” (cuyamariyātai) named both the goal and the method: a person who accepts an inferior birth because a scripture or a priest says so has given up his self-respect, and no political freedom can restore it to him. He spread the argument through his Tamil weekly Kuṭi Aracu (from 1925) and through the Self-Respect conferences, the first of which, at Chengalpattu in 1929, called for the abolition of caste titles and for women’s rights to divorce and to property. He led the agitation against compulsory Hindi in 1937–39, became president of the Justice Party in 1938, and in 1944 turned it into the Dravidar Kazhagam; the title “Periyar” was conferred on him by a women’s conference in Madras in 1938. His essays on women, collected under the title “Why Did Woman Become a Slave?”, attacked chastity (kaṟpu) as a virtue demanded of women alone, and defended divorce, widow remarriage and contraception. Self-respect marriages, solemnised by an exchange of garlands and a declaration without Sanskrit mantras, were given legal validity in Tamil Nadu by an amendment to the Hindu Marriage Act in 1967.

    His counter-history was political rather than theological. Unlike Narayana Guru he did not seek a truer religion; unlike Phule and Thass he did not seek an original faith to restore. The opposition of Aryan and Dravidian, which M. S. S. Pandian has traced to colonial philology (beginning with Robert Caldwell’s Comparative Grammar of the Dravidian or South-Indian Family of Languages, 1856) as well as to Tamil revivalism, served him as a frame for a contemporary struggle over education, office and language (Pandian 2007). His readings of the Rāmāyaṇa, which made Rāvaṇa a Dravidian king wronged by northern invaders, belong to the long history of counter-tellings of the epic described in Volume 7 (Itihāsa–Purāṇa). Dalit critics have since pointed out that the non-Brahmin movement benefited the intermediate landowning castes more than it benefited those below them.

    14.2.5 Counter-histories compared¶

    Table 14.1 sets four of these thinkers beside Ambedkar. Three of them, like Ambedkar, answered the Brahmanical account of the Indian past with a rival history of loss: a just order or a true religion of the people that had been destroyed by fraud and force and could be recovered. Phule’s lost order was Bali’s kingdom of cultivators, Thass’s and Ambedkar’s a Buddhist past, Periyar’s a Dravidian self-respect that had never needed Sanskrit gods. Narayana Guru is the exception. He offered no history of conquest, and argued instead from the concept of jāti itself that there was only one human kind; his method was conceptual where theirs was genealogical.

    Table 14.1. Anti-caste counter-histories compared
    Thinker The excluded were originally Remedy proposed
    Jotirao Phule the cultivators of Bali’s just kingdom, conquered by Aryan Brahmins education; a priestless religion of one creator (Satyashodhak Samaj)
    Iyothee Thass the original Buddhists of the Tamil land return to Buddhism; a casteless Dravidian identity
    Narayana Guru no counter-history: there is only one human jāti temples, schools and organisation open to all; Advaita

    The genealogical histories share the weakness of their Orientalist models: they read myth as coded history and project modern categories of race and nation onto the distant past, and specialists do not accept them as accounts of what happened. They share also the strength of a hermeneutics of suspicion, the recognition that the texts which classified people were written by some of those people about others. For a volume on samanvaya they pose a direct challenge. The classical ideal of harmonising the scriptures assumed that the scriptures were the measure; these thinkers asked who had made the measure, and they found the harmony of the texts to be the harmony of a ruling voice.

    14.2.6 Dalit and Bahujan thought after Ambedkar¶

    Ambedkar’s critique has been continued and extended by later Dalit and Bahujan (“majority”, that is, non-elite) thinkers. Kancha Ilaiah’s Why I Am Not a Hindu (1996) contrasted the productive, egalitarian culture of the labouring castes of Andhra Pradesh with what he called the “spiritual fascism” of Brahmanical Hinduism, in a polemic that provoked fierce debate (Ilaiah 1996). The political scientist Gopal Guru and the philosopher Sundar Sarukkai, in The Cracked Mirror (2012), asked who has the right to theorise experience in India, and argued that the social sciences had treated Dalits as objects of study rather than as thinkers; they called for a philosophy that takes the experience of humiliation and exclusion as its starting point (Guru and Sarukkai 2012). Dalit autobiographies and literature in Marathi, Tamil, Kannada, Hindi and other languages have become one of the most important sources for Indian thought about caste. These writers do not regard themselves as contributors to a “synthesis” of Indian traditions; they regard themselves as critics of a tradition that excluded them. A volume on synthesis must give their criticism its full weight.

    14.3 Women Thinkers Answering the Śāstras¶

    The reform debates of the nineteenth century were conducted mostly by men about women. Reformers and their orthodox opponents argued over satī, widow remarriage and the age of marriage by quoting the śāstras at one another, and both sides tended to locate the ideal Indian woman in an imagined Vedic past of learned wives such as Gārgī and Maitreyī. Some women answered for themselves, and they asked a different question: not what the texts permitted, but who had written them. The legal history of these debates is told in Volume 18 (Rājanīti and Dharmaśāstra), the history of women’s schooling in Volume 22, and women’s writing in the regional literatures in Volume 8 (Kāvya Kalpalatā); this section treats three women as thinkers who compared and judged traditions. In western India the questioning began early. In 1855 Mukta Salve, a fourteen-year-old Māṅg girl at one of the Phules’ schools, wrote an essay on the sufferings of the Māṅgs and Mahārs, published in a missionary journal, which asked what religion the Māṅgs and Mahārs could call their own if the Vedas belonged to the Brahmins alone. Savitribai Phule (1831–1897), Jotirao’s wife, who taught in these schools from 1848, published two books of Marathi verse, Kāvyaphule (1854) and Bāvanakaśī Subodharatnākar (1892), and died in 1897 while nursing victims of plague.

    14.3.1 Tarabai Shinde¶

    Little is known of Tarabai Shinde (c. 1850–1910) beyond her single book. She was the daughter of Bapuji Hari Shinde, a Maratha clerk in the revenue office at Buldhana, in Berar, who was a member of Phule’s Satyashodhak Samaj and who taught her to read. Strī Puruṣ Tulanā (“A Comparison of Women and Men”, 1882) was provoked by a case reported in the Marathi press in 1881: a young Brahmin widow, Vijayalakshmi, was convicted at Surat of killing her newborn illegitimate child and sentenced to death, a sentence later commuted to transportation for life. The newspapers moralised about the wickedness of women. Tarabai asked why the woman was punished while the man who had seduced her went unnamed, and turned the case into a general comparison of the vices of the two sexes (O’Hanlon 1994).

    Her method was inversion. A long literary tradition in Sanskrit and Marathi catalogued the faults of women, their fickleness, deceit and lust; Tarabai catalogued the faults of men, from the drinking, gambling and whoring of the idle to the hypocrisy of English-educated reformers who demanded learned wives while keeping their own habits. She turned the epic exemplars of the devoted wife (pativratā) against the men who held them up, asking which husband had shown the constancy that the stories demanded of wives, and she described the condition of widows, forbidden remarriage, often tonsured and treated as a burden, as the creation of men who had written the rules for their own convenience. The Pune press received the book with hostility, and Phule defended it. Rosalind O’Hanlon, who translated it, reads it as one of the earliest sustained feminist arguments in a modern Indian language and stresses that it criticised Brahmanical patriarchy and the new reforming patriarchy together.

    14.3.2 Pandita Ramabai¶

    Pandita Ramabai (1858–1922), a Brahmin woman who mastered Sanskrit and was given the title “Pandita” by the scholars of Calcutta, criticised the treatment of women in the Dharmaśāstras in The High-Caste Hindu Woman (1887), converted to Christianity and founded the Mukti Mission for widows and destitute women near Pune. Uma Chakravarti’s study shows how her life was shaped by the conflict between her learning and the restrictions placed on women (Chakravarti 1998).

    Her father, Anant Shastri Dongre, had taught Sanskrit to his wife and daughter against the custom of his caste, and the family lived as wandering reciters of the Purāṇas until her parents and a sister died, within a short time of one another, in the famine years of the 1870s. After the recognition in Calcutta in 1878 Ramabai married a Bengali lawyer of the Kāyastha caste, a marriage across caste and region that orthodox opinion condemned, and was widowed with an infant daughter in 1882. In the same year she founded the Arya Mahila Samaj in Pune and published Strīdharmanīti (“Morals for Women”), a Marathi conduct book that urged women to educate themselves and to become self-reliant. The High-Caste Hindu Woman, written in the United States, was organised by the stages of a woman’s life, childhood, marriage and widowhood, and quoted the law-books against themselves; its argument was that the treatment of high-caste widows was not a corruption of the tradition but was prescribed by it. Its sale and the American association formed to support her paid for the Sharada Sadan, a residential school for widows opened in Bombay in 1889 and moved to Pune in the following year (Kosambi 2000).

    Ramabai’s Christianity was as independent as her Hinduism had been. Baptised in England in 1883, she disputed doctrine and obedience with the Anglican sisters who sponsored her, and wrote that having freed herself from one priestly authority she would not submit to another. Nationalist critics in Pune, among them the editors of the Kesari, accused the Sharada Sadan of converting its pupils, and in America in 1894–95 supporters of her mission were among Vivekananda’s critics, the two offering American audiences opposed pictures of Hindu womanhood (Chapter 10). After the famine of 1896–97 she built the Mukti Mission at Kedgaon, and in her last years she completed a translation of the Bible into Marathi from the Hebrew and Greek. Chakravarti argues that she was written out of the nationalist history of reform because she had converted, and that her critique of Brahmanical patriarchy was more searching than that of most male reformers.

    14.3.3 Rokeya Sakhawat Hossain¶

    Rokeya Sakhawat Hossain (1880–1932) grew up in a landed Muslim family of Rangpur in northern Bengal, where girls were kept in strict seclusion; an elder brother taught her English and Bengali secretly at night. Her husband, a deputy magistrate in Bhagalpur, encouraged her writing, and after his death in 1909 she used his bequest to found a school for Muslim girls, first at Bhagalpur and from 1911 in Calcutta. In 1916 she founded the Anjuman-e-Khawateen-e-Islam, an association of Muslim women in Bengal (Ray 2002).

    Sultana’s Dream, written in English and published in 1905 in the Indian Ladies’ Magazine of Madras, is a short utopia. The narrator dreams that she walks through Ladyland, where the men live in seclusion in the mardāna and the women govern. Women scientists in the two women’s universities have learned to draw water from the clouds and to collect the heat of the sun, and the country won its only war by turning concentrated sunlight on the invaders after the men had failed; crime has nearly disappeared, and the religion of the land is one of love and truth (Hossain 1988). The satire turns on a simple inversion of purdah, but its argument is about knowledge: seclusion is an arrangement of power, sustained by keeping women from science. Rokeya’s Bengali writing was more direct. Her essay “Our Degradation” (Āmāder abanati), first published in the Calcutta journal Nabanūr in 1904 and reprinted in revised form as “The Degradation of Women” (Strījātir abanati) in Motīcūr, argued that religious scriptures had been used by men to keep women in subjection; its most outspoken passages on religion were omitted from the reprint. Her novel Padmarāg (1924) imagines a refuge run by women in which women of every religion live and work together, and the sketches of Abarodhbāsinī (“The Secluded Ones”, 1931) record, with deadpan precision, the injuries and absurdities that purdah produced.

    14.3.4 Three strategies compared¶

    The three women wrote from different religious worlds, Tarabai from a Marathi non-Brahmin Hindu household, Ramabai from Brahmin learning that led her to Christianity, Rokeya from Bengali Muslim gentility, and they used different means: satire and inversion, scholarly exegesis and institution-building, and a scientific utopia. They agreed on one point that set them apart from most male reformers. They located women’s subordination not in a decline from a golden age but in the texts and customs that men had made and continued to make, Hindu, Muslim and modern alike. In this they stand in sharp contrast to the idealised images of ancient Indian womanhood promoted by many reformers and nationalists, and they anticipate the questions that Uma Chakravarti and other historians have since asked of the Dharmaśāstra tradition (Chakravarti 2003), discussed in Chapter 5.

    14.4 Adivasi Religion and Thought¶

    The communities now called Adivasis (“original inhabitants”, a name that came into political use in Chotanagpur in the 1930s) or, in the language of the Constitution, Scheduled Tribes, are the part of India’s population least represented in its written traditions. Their knowledge of forests, plants, animals and crafts is the subject of Volume 23 (Jana–Jāti). This section is concerned with Adivasi religion as a tradition with its own voice, and with the arguments of Adivasi leaders and their interlocutors about what their place in India should be.

    14.4.1 Sacred groves and Sarna¶

    Most Adivasi religions of central and eastern India centre on a supreme being associated with the sun or the sky, on ancestral and village spirits, and on sacred places, above all the grove. Among the Munda, Ho and Oraon of Jharkhand the grove is the sarnā, the site of the village’s festivals and sacrifices (Chapter 21); the supreme being is called Singbonga by the Munda and Dharmes by the Oraon. In the twentieth century these practices were organised into a self-conscious religion, “Sarna”, distinct from both Hinduism and Christianity, and Sarna associations have long asked that the census record it as a religion in its own right rather than counting its followers as Hindus or under “other religions”. In 2020 the Jharkhand Legislative Assembly passed a resolution supporting that demand. The question is the one that this volume meets among Sikhs, Jains, Buddhists and Ambedkarites: whether a community’s own account of its religion, or an outside classification, decides what it is.

    14.4.2 Prophets and rebellions¶

    Adivasi religious thought often found expression in movements that were at once religious reforms and revolts. The Santal rising of 1855–56, led by the brothers Sidhu and Kanhu, began with a vision in which they were commanded to free the Santals from moneylenders, landlords and officials. In the 1890s Birsa Munda (c. 1875–1900), who had been educated at a mission school, proclaimed himself a prophet among the Munda, taught the worship of one god, Singbonga, and the abandonment of sacrifice and of the fear of spirits, and called on his followers to reclaim the land from outsiders (diku); his teaching drew on Munda tradition, on Christianity and on Vaiṣṇava ideas of purity, and it ended in the armed rising (ulgulān) of 1899–1900. Birsa died in prison at Ranchi in 1900, and the Chotanagpur Tenancy Act of 1908 was in part a response to the grievances his movement had expressed. K. Suresh Singh’s study remains the standard account (Singh 1966). Among the Oraon, the Tana Bhagat movement that began in 1914 combined a reformed, sober and vegetarian discipline with the refusal of rent and labour dues, and many of its members later joined Gandhi’s campaigns. In south Gujarat in 1922–23 a goddess was believed to speak through mediums who commanded Adivasis to give up liquor and meat and to boycott the liquor-dealers and moneylenders; David Hardiman has shown how this “coming of the Devi” turned a religious movement into an assertion of economic and political independence (Hardiman 1987).

    These movements are often read either as primitive rebellions or as early stages of nationalism. They are better read, as their historians suggest, as Adivasi arguments about legitimate authority, conducted in a religious idiom: who owns the land, which god is to be served, and whether the rules of outsiders bind the community. Their prophets borrowed from Christianity and Hinduism as Indian reformers borrowed from European thought, to answer questions of their own.

    14.4.3 Elwin, Ghurye and the question of assimilation¶

    In the 1940s the place of the tribal peoples in a future India became the subject of a sharp public argument. Verrier Elwin (1902–1964), an English missionary who left the church, lived for many years among the Gond and Baiga of central India and wrote ethnographies of them, argued that tribal cultures had their own values and should be protected from exploitation by traders, moneylenders, officials and missionaries, and from the contempt of reforming Hindus; in his early writing he proposed protected areas within which tribal life would be shielded from outside pressure. The sociologist G. S. Ghurye (1893–1983) replied in The Aborigines — “So-called” — and Their Future (1943) that the tribes were “backward Hindus”, long integrated into the social and religious life of their Hindu neighbours, and that the remedy for their poverty was assimilation into the wider society, not isolation (Ghurye 1943). Ramachandra Guha’s biography of Elwin shows that the two positions were less opposed than their rhetoric suggested — Elwin abandoned the idea of isolation, and Ghurye did not deny exploitation — but the argument set the terms of the later debate between protection and integration (Guha 1999). After independence Elwin became adviser on tribal affairs for the North-East Frontier Agency, and Jawaharlal Nehru’s principles for tribal policy, which called for development along the lines of the people’s own genius and respect for tribal rights in land and forests, drew on his views.

    Adivasi leaders spoke for themselves. Jaipal Singh Munda (1903–1970), educated at Oxford and captain of the Indian hockey team that won the Olympic gold medal in 1928, led the Adivasi Mahasabha of Chotanagpur and argued in the Constituent Assembly that the Adivasis, as the oldest inhabitants of the land, had been dispossessed by the rest of India and needed safeguards rather than paternal improvement. The Constitution responded with the Fifth Schedule, which gives special responsibilities for Scheduled Areas, and the Sixth Schedule, which created autonomous district councils in the hill areas of the North-East; later laws on self-government in Scheduled Areas (1996) and on forest rights (2006; Chapter 22) extended these protections. Their uneven enforcement, and the displacement of Adivasis by dams, mines and conservation projects, remain among the gravest failures of Indian democracy.

    14.4.4 The North-East¶

    The hill peoples of the North-East met the colonial and national states later than those of central India, and their religious history took a distinctive course. In the Brahmaputra valley the Vaiṣṇava teacher Śaṅkaradeva had already, in the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries, drawn people of many communities, including tribal ones, into a devotional order of village prayer-halls and monasteries (Chapter 16). In the hills, Baptist and Presbyterian missions of the nineteenth and twentieth centuries led to large-scale conversion, so that Christians now form the majority in Nagaland, Mizoram and Meghalaya, and Christianity there became a vehicle of literacy, of written languages and of new ethnic identities. Indigenous religions answered in their own ways. In the Khasi Hills the Seng Khasi, founded at Shillong in 1899, set out to preserve the Khasi religion and customs against conversion. Among the Zeme, Liangmai and Rongmei Nagas, the movement begun by Jadonang and continued after his execution in 1931 by Gaidinliu (1915–1993) reformed the indigenous religion as Heraka and opposed both British rule and the Christian missions; Gaidinliu, imprisoned from 1932 until independence and later honoured as “Rani”, became a national figure, and her later association with Hindu organisations has made her legacy contested among Nagas. In Arunachal Pradesh, movements of the late twentieth century have organised the worship of Donyi-Polo, the sun and moon, among the Adi and neighbouring peoples as a self-conscious indigenous religion.

    These developments show that Adivasi religion is not a static substratum beneath the “great” traditions, as older scholarship often assumed, but a set of living traditions that have argued with Hinduism, Christianity and the state, borrowed from them and resisted them. For many of their adherents the most important question is not whether they share the ideas of their neighbours but whether they may keep their land, their gods and their own name for themselves.

    14.5 Assessment¶

    The anti-caste thinkers, the women writers and the Adivasi leaders of this chapter worked in different places and languages and rarely read one another. They shared a starting point: each spoke from a position that the classical texts had defined from outside — as Śūdra or outcaste, as wife or widow, as forest-dweller beyond the four varṇas — and each refused that definition. Their methods differed: counter-history, conceptual argument, satire, scholarly exegesis, prophecy, rebellion and constitutional bargaining. For a volume on synthesis they pose the most direct challenge of all, set out above in the comparison of counter-histories, and it applies to the women writers and the Adivasi leaders as much as to the anti-caste thinkers: the classical texts are evidence about a tradition, not the measure of who belongs to it.
