    Preface¶

    This is Volume 24, the last volume of the series Bhāratīya Jñāna Paramparā: Samanvaya: Comparative Perspectives and Synthesis. The earlier volumes treat particular fields: the Veda and the Upaniṣads, the philosophical schools, the Buddhist and Jain traditions, yoga and tantra, the epics and Purāṇas, literature, music, language, mathematics, astronomy, medicine, chemistry, agriculture, architecture, crafts, law and polity, trade, warfare, geography, psychology and education, and the knowledge of tribal and folk communities. This volume asks a different kind of question. What ideas did India’s traditions share, and where did they genuinely disagree? How have Indians and outsiders compared these traditions with one another and with the thought of other civilisations? And how did Indian thinkers of the last two centuries respond to colonial rule, to modern science and to one another?

    The volume is in three parts. The first (Chapters 2–7) sets Hindu, Buddhist and Jain ideas of cosmos and time, of the self and the means of knowledge, of dharma, the aims of life, karma, non-violence and truth side by side, so that their differences are as visible as their family resemblances. The second (Chapters 8–18) is a history of encounters: European Orientalism and the rise of comparative philosophy; the Hindu reform movements, Neo-Vedānta and Hindu nationalism; Vivekananda, Aurobindo, Gandhi and Ambedkar, and the political thinkers who came after them; Tagore and other poets, nationalists and independent thinkers; the anti-caste thinkers, women writers and Adivasi movements who answered the śāstras and the state from below; the academic philosophers, from K. C. Bhattacharya to Matilal and Daya Krishna, who asked what it meant to do philosophy as an Indian; the long meeting of Islamic, Indo-Persian, Sufi, Sikh and Christian traditions with those already in India, and the Singh Sabha reformers who defined modern Sikh identity; the arguments of modern Muslim reformers, revivalists and their critics, from Sayyid Ahmad Khan, Deoband and Iqbal to Mawdūdī and Hamid Dalwai; and the modern lives and thinkers of Buddhism and Jainism. The third (Chapters 19–22) examines how Indian ideas have been used in conversations about science, health, ecology, the arts and education, and closes with an assessment of the present “Indian Knowledge Systems” movement and of the arguments of its advocates and critics.

    Popular writing on these subjects is full of claims that cannot stand: invented or garbled verses, quotations attributed to modern scientists that they never wrote, statistics without sources, and repeated suggestions that ancient texts anticipated modern physics or that all religions teach the same thing. This book does not repeat them, and where they are widely current it says why they fail. Where a modern comparison is made, it is presented as an analogy drawn by a named modern writer, not as evidence that anyone knew the modern result in advance. Dates follow the series standard: the range accepted in current scholarship is given first, and traditional dates are labelled as traditional.

    A synthesis volume is especially exposed to two temptations. One is to celebrate: to present the tradition as a seamless whole whose every part confirms every other. The other is to dismiss. The guiding word of this volume, samanvaya, names a more demanding practice, that of showing how different statements can be held together without pretending that they say the same thing. Readers will find here the Buddhist and Jain rejections of Vedic authority, Ambedkar’s critique of caste and of the Hindu law-books, the doubts of K. C. Bhattacharya and Daya Krishna about the colonised mind, and the voices of Sufi, Sikh, Dalit and Adivasi thinkers, presented on their own terms rather than as footnotes to Vedānta.

    References to standard editions, translations and studies are given in the text and collected in the select bibliography; cross-references point to the volumes of this series where each subject is treated at length. A glossary and a chronology are provided at the end, and the bibliography lists the editions and translations of the primary texts quoted. Every quotation should be checked against a critical edition before scholarly use.

    Nikhil Kumar Rajput
    Ramanujan College, University of Delhi
    Guru Pūrṇimā, 2026
