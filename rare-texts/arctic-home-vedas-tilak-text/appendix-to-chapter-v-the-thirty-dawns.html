    REMARKS. It has been previously mentioned that the fifteen rses, quoted above, arc used or recited as Mantras at the of laying down certain emblematieal bricks, called Jushti-ishtakâs, or dawn-bricks, on the saerifieial altar. 1t as the Mantras, or verses, used for sacrificial purposes often taken from different Vedic hymns, these verses are ely to be regarded as unconnected with each other. The count of the thirty dawns, contained therein, however, ews that these verses must have originally formed an tire or one homogeneous hymn. Again if the Mantras ¹d been selected from different hymns, one for each wn-briek, there would naturally be 16 verses in all, 16 dawn-bricks are to be laid on the altar. The fact, that the Anuvâka contains only 15 verses (leavthe sacrificer to sclect the 16th from elsewhere ), therere, further supports the sume view. It is true that some of ese verses are found in the Atharva-Veda, either detached in connection with other subjects. But that does not event us from treating tl e passage in the Taittiriya Sar-5 hitâ, as containing a connected account of thirty dawns divided into five groups of six each. The question is not. however, very material, inasmuch as verses 5 and 6, whether they formed part of an entire hymn or not, are by themselves sufficient to prove the point at issue, viz., that the Vedic Goddess of Dawn constituted a group of thirty sisters. The Rig-Veda speaks of thirty steps" traversed by the Dawn, (VI, 59, 6), or of Dawns going round thirty mast (I, 123, 8); but both these statements have, as yet, re-maincd totally unexplained, or have been but imperfectly explained by Indian and Western scholars alike. But now that we know that the Vedic Dawns were thirty in number, both the aforesaid statements become at once casily comprehensible. The only other point necessary to be decided, so far as the subject in hand is concerned, is whether these thirty dawns were the dawns of thirty consecutive days, or whether they formed a (closely-gathered band' of thirty continuous dawns; and on reading the two aforesaid passages from the Taittirtya Sarnhitâ, the one from the Taittirtya Brâhmaṇa, II, 5, 6, 5, and other authorities cited in the foregoing chapter, I do not think, there can be any doubt that the Goddess of Dawn, worshipped by the Vedic bards, was originally a group of thirty continuous dawns. It is not contended that the ancestors of the Vedie bards were unaequainted with ordinary dawns, for, even in the circumpolar regions there are, during certain parts of the year, successions of ordinary days and nights and with them of ordinary dawns. But so far as the Vedie Goddess of morning is concerned, there is enough evidence to shew that it was no other than the continuous and revolving Dawn at the end of the long night in those regions, the Dawn that lasted for thirty periods of 24 hours each, which is possible only within a few degrees roundabout the North Pole.
