    2. Murari Murari tells us that he was the son of Crivardhamânaka ef the Maudgalya Gotra and of Tantumati; he claims to be a Mahaâkavi, and arrogates the style of Bâla-Vâlmiki. His date is uncertain; he is certainly later than Bhavabhtiti since he cites from the Uttararâmacar ta, while we have evidence from the anthologies that he was reckoned by some as superior to Bhavabhiiti, apparently his predecessor. A further suggestion as to date may be derived from the Kashmirian poet Ratnâkara,' who in his Haravi aya makes a clear reference to Murari as a dramatist, for the effort of Bhattanatha Svamin to disprove the reference must be deemed completely unsuccessful. As Ratnâkara belongs to the middle of the ninth century A.D., this gives us that period as the latest date for Muâri. Curiously enough, Professor Konow,' who accepts the disptoof of the reference to Murari in Ratnâkara, admits that the reference to Murari in Mafkha's Crikanthacarata® (c. A.D. 1135) suggests that he was regarded by that author as earlier than Râjagekhara, a fact which accords excellently with his priority to Ratnâkara, and is far more important than the fact that he is not cited by the authors on theory of the eleventh century A.D. A finther effort to place him late is that of Dr. Hultzsch, who infers from verse 3 of the K aumudsmitrâganda of Râmacandra, pupil of Hemacandra, that that891011 226 Murari dramatist was a contemporary of Murari. But the evidence is clearly inadequate ; the words used are perfectly compatible with the fact that Murai was dead, and there are grave chronological difficulties in the way of the theory. It is practically impossible that a contemporary of Râmacandra could have been cited by Mafikha at the date of the Crikaṇthacarita. Moreover Murâri seems to have been imitated by Jayadeva in the Prasannarâghava. Of his place of activity we know nothing definite. He mentions, however, Mâhigmati as the seat of the Kalacuris, and it has been suggested that this indicates that he lived under the patronage of a prince of that dynasty at Mâhigmati, now Mândhâtâ on the Narmada.

The Anargharâghava Murâriedeclares in the prologue to the solitary drama, the Anargharâghava,' which has come down to us, though quotations show that he wrote other works, that his aim is to please a public tired of terior, horror, violence, and marvels, by a com. position elevated, heroic, and marvellous throughout, not merely at the close. He defends his choice of the banal subject of Râma ; his character adds elevation and charm to the poet's work, and it would be folly to lay aside so splendid a theme. The Anargharâghava, however, does little to justify the poet's confidence in his choice of topic The theme, treated already at length by Bhavabhiti, offered no chance of success save for a great poet, and Murâri was not such a poet save in the estimate of occasional later writers who extol his depth (gambhṅratâ) without any shadow of justification. Act I shows us Dagaratha in conversation with Vâmadeva. The arrival of the sage Vigvâmitra is announced ; he exchanges with the king hyperbolic compliments of the most tedious type, but proceeds to business by demanding the aid of Râma against the Râksasas which are troubling his hermitage The king hesitates to send one so young and dear into danger. The sage insists on his obeying the call of duty, and he hands over Râma and Lakṣmaṇa to the care of the ascetic. The herald announces1213 midday, and the king mourns the loss of his son. In Act II we have first a long-drawn-out conversation between Cunahgepha and Pagumedhra, two pupils of Vigvâmitra, which serves to enlighten us on the history of Valin, Râvana, the Râksasas, Jâmbavant, Hanumant, and Tâadakâ. The entr'acte is followed by the appearance of Râma and Laksmana who describe the hermitage and the doings of its occupants, and then the heat of midday. Time, however, does not trouble the dramatist ; though there is no further action and no interruption in the dialogue, we find ourselves transported to the evening , Vigvâamitra enters and describes in converse with the boys the sunset. A cry behind the scene announces the approach of the demoness Tâdaka; Râma hesitates to slay a female, but finally departs for the necessary duty ; on his return he has to describe the rising of the moon. Vigvâamitra then suggests a visit to Janaka of Mithila, affording an opportunity for a description of the city and its ruler. In Act II only do we reach the motif which Bhavabhiiti with far greater skill made the leading idea of the drama, thus giving it effective unity, so far as the story permits. The chamberlain of Janaka in conversation with Kalahansikâ, one of Sitâ's suite, lets us know that the princess is now ripe for marriage, and Râvaṇa seeks her hand. In the scene that follows the king accompanied by Catânanda receives Râma, but hesitates to put him to the severe test involved in bending Ctva's bow. Causkala, Râvana's envoy, arrives to demand the maiden's hand, but indignantly declines the request that his master should bend the bow. He eulogizes Râvana whom Râma depireciates. Râma is at last allowed to make the trial , those who remain on the stage describe his wonderful deed in breaking the bow. He is promised Siutâ's hand, while the other sons of Dagaratha are also awarded consorts. Caugkala departs, menacing revenge. Act IV shows us Râvana's minister Mâlyavant lamenting the failure of his scheme to win Sitâ. Ctrpanakhâ arrives from Videha and tells of the union of Râma and Sita. Maâlyavant recognizes that Râvaṇa will insist on seeking to separate the pair, and he counsels Ctrpaṇakhâ to assume the disguise of Mantharâ, the maid of Kaikeyî, with the view of securing the banishment of Râma to the forest, where he will be more vulnerable to attack. 228 The Anargharâghava He is also cheered by the news given by Cirpanakhâ of the approach of Paragurâma to Mithila, whence some gain may accrue to his cause. The following scene shows us Râma and Paragurâama in verbal contest Râma is even more polite than in the Makâviracarita which is obviously imitated, while the friends of Râma carry on a vituperative dialogue behind the scene without actually appearing. Finally they resolve to fight, for Râma has annoyed his rival by reminding him that the flag of his fame won by his destruction of the Kṣatriyas is worn out and challenging him to mount a new one. The rivals then appear on excellent terms; Paragurama exchanges farewells with his former interlocutors and disappears. Then enter Janaka and Dagaratha. In Act VI Sâraṇa and Cuka, two spies of Râvaṇa's describe to Mâlyavant the building of the bridge over the ocean and the advent of Râma's army. Voices from behind announce the departure of Kumbhakarna and Meghanâda for battle ; in the same way we learn of their fall and the last exit of Râvaṇa, whom Maâlyavant decides to follow to the field. The final struggle is described with tedious and tasteless prolixity by two Vidyâdharas, Ratnactiga and Hemafgada, and with this the Act closes. In Act VII we have a determined effort to vie with the close of the Makâviracarita. Rama, Sitâ, Lakymana,Vibhigana,and Sugriva proceed in Kubera's celestial car to Ayodhyâ. But the route is diversified from the simplicity of the model, for the travellers are taken to the celestial regions to view in all its aspects the mythical mountain Sumeru and the world of the moon ; only then do they commence their journey in its terrestiial aspect by a description of Sinhala, distinguished as usual from Lafkaâ; the route then passes over the Malaya mountains, the forest, the mountain Prasravana, the Godâvari, mount Mâlyavant, Kundinipura in the Maharastra country, Kâfici, Ujjayini, Mâhigmati, the Yamunâ, the Ganges, Vârânasi, Mithila and Campa ; the car goes then west to Prayâga, and later turns east to Ayodhya, where the priest Vasistha waits with R3ma's brothers to crown him king. The demerits of the poem are obvious; there is no attempt to improve on the traditional narrative, though Vaâlin is honourably killed ; the characters are as stereotyped as ever. The author, however, delights to overload and elaborate the theme ; hyperbole marks every idea , his mythological knowledge is adequate to enable him to abound in conceits and plays on words, when he does not sink, as largely in Act III, to mere commonplace. The taste which mvents the visit to the world of the moon and Sumeru is as deplorable as that which substitutes the dull dialogue between Jâmbavant and Jatâyu for the vigorous conversation of Jatâyu and Sampaâti in the Makâviracarita. His knowledge of the lexica is obvious, while his love of the recondite in grammar has won him the fame of being used to illustrate rare forms by the author of the Siddhântakaumudi. These linguistic merits have secured him the preference shown for him by modern taste. There can be no doubt of the demerits of Râjagekhara's works; he is devoid of the power to create a character : Vidyâdharamalla is stiff and uninteresting beside his model, the gay and gallant Vatsa ; the queen is without the love or the majesty of Vâsavadatta ; Bhagurâyaṇa is a feeble Yaugandharâyaṇa, whose magician is borrowed in the Karpṅramaṅjari and spoiled in the borrowing. The heroines are without merit; the Vidugaka in the Karpâramaṅjarî is tedious, but Cârâyana in the Viddhagâlabhaṅyikâ has merits; he has plenty of sound common sense, though he is simple and capable of being taken in by others. The intrigue in both Nâtikâs is poorly managed; the confusion of exits and entiances in the Karpṅramaṅ)art is difficult to follow and probably more difficult to act, while in the Viddhagâlabhaṅjikâ the queen is induced to anange a marriage out of a puerile cident affecting the Vidigaka only. The taste of giving two brides to the king at once is deplorable, as is the farlure to explain why the king accepts the suggested mariage when ignorant of its true import. In all his dramas, however, Râjagekhara 1s merely concerned with exercises in style. The themes he frankly tells us in the prologue to the Karpâramaṅjarî are the same, the question is the expression, and the language is indifferent ; therefore Prâkrit being smooth, while Sanskrit is harsh, the language of women as opposed to men, can be used as a medium of style by one who boasts himself an expert in every kind of language. We have, therefore, elaborate descriptions in equally elaborate verses, of the dawn, midday, sunset, the pleasures of the harem, the game of ball, the swing, a favourite enjoyment of the Indian maidens, and in the Nâtakas pictures ad nauseam of battles with magic weapons, and appalling mythical geography and topography. His allusions to local practices and customs may be interesting to the antiquarian, but are not poetical.
