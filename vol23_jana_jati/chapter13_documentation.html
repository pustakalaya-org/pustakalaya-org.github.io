    Chapter 13: Documentation, Preservation and Archives¶

    13.1 Introduction: The Ethics of Recording¶

    To record knowledge that might be lost seems an obvious good. But the history of documenting Adivasi knowledge is ambiguous. Records have preserved songs, stories, plant names and languages that would otherwise have disappeared; they have also been made without consent, kept far from the communities concerned, used to classify and control them, and used commercially without benefit to them. This chapter looks at that history, at participatory and community-controlled alternatives, and at the documentation of languages.

    Principles of Ethical Documentation

    • Free, prior and informed consent of the community and of individual knowledge holders.

    • Community ownership and control of records, including decisions about access.

    • Respect for restricted knowledge: some knowledge is meant only for initiates, for one gender, or for particular occasions.

    • Benefit-sharing from any use of the records.

    • Do no harm: records should not expose communities to appropriation, ridicule or danger.

    • Support for living practice: documentation should complement, not replace, the continued practice of knowledge.

    13.2 Colonial and Early Ethnography¶

    The first large records of Adivasi life were made by colonial officials, missionaries and scholars: census reports and district gazetteers; compendia such as H. H. Risley’s The Tribes and Castes of Bengal (1891) and Edgar Thurston’s Castes and Tribes of Southern India (1909) (Risley 1891; Thurston and Rangachari 1909); and monographs by missionaries such as Stephen Hislop and P. O. Bodding and by scholars such as Sarat Chandra Roy (Hislop 1866; Bodding 1925; Roy 1912, 1915). These works preserve much that would otherwise be lost, and they are still used, including by Adivasi scholars and activists.

    They were also shaped by colonial purposes and assumptions. Risley used anthropometry to rank castes and tribes by race; the census assigned communities to fixed categories that hardened identities; ethnographers collected data and departed, without obligation to the people they studied. The categories of “tribe”, “animist” and “criminal tribe” were partly products of this knowledge-making.

    13.2.1 Old Ethnographies, New Uses¶

    Colonial ethnographies have had unexpected afterlives. Naga, Khasi and Ao readers consult Hutton, Mills and Gurdon for information about their own clans, customs and history (Hutton 1921; Mills 1926; Gurdon 1907); Munda and Oraon litigants have cited Roy’s descriptions of customary tenure in land disputes (Roy 1912); and revival movements draw on recorded myths and songs to recover forgotten rituals. Such uses show that the records belong, in a sense, to the communities they describe. They also show the danger of treating an outsider’s snapshot of one moment as the definitive statement of a community’s “true” custom.

    13.2.2 Verrier Elwin¶

    Verrier Elwin (Chapter 1) produced the largest body of writing on the Adivasis of central and north-eastern India: monographs on the Baiga, Muria and Saora, collections of myths, songs and folktales, and studies of tribal art (Elwin 1939, 1947, 1949, 1951, 1955). He worked closely with Indian collaborators, above all Shamrao Hivale, and with Adivasi informants and assistants whose role was not always acknowledged. His work is sympathetic and vivid, but also reflects his own preoccupations; Ramachandra Guha’s biography assesses it critically (Guha 1999). Elwin’s autobiography, The Tribal World of Verrier Elwin (1964), gives his own account (Elwin 1964).

    13.2.3 Photographs¶

    Photography was part of documentation from the start. Colonial photographers produced “type” portraits of tribal people for ethnological albums, often posed and sometimes partly undressed, which reinforced the image of Adivasis as exotic and primitive. Later photographers such as Sunil Janah, who travelled with Elwin in the 1940s, made more sympathetic portraits, but the images still circulated far from the people photographed. Such photographs are now valuable historical records, and some communities have used them to recover knowledge of past dress, ornament and architecture; but their use raises questions of consent and dignity that apply equally to photography today.

    13.2.4 Anthropology after Independence¶

    The Anthropological Survey of India (founded 1945) and university departments of anthropology produced many studies after independence. The Survey’s People of India project, directed by K. S. Singh between 1985 and 1992, compiled brief profiles of several thousand communities, including all the Scheduled Tribes (Singh 1994). The Indira Gandhi Rashtriya Manav Sangrahalaya (National Museum of Mankind) in Bhopal has built large collections of Adivasi art and architecture and invited Adivasi artists and builders to create works on its grounds. Increasingly, Adivasi scholars such as Virginius Xaxa and Ram Dayal Munda have written about their own communities and challenged the assumptions of earlier ethnography (Xaxa 1999).

    13.3 Participatory Documentation¶

    Participatory approaches treat communities as partners rather than sources of data. Community members are trained as researchers and work in their own language; records are made in that language first, with translations clearly marked, and audio and video capture performance and practice as well as words. The community reviews the records before they are finished and decides what is too sensitive to record at all. The most successful projects tie documentation to support for living practice, such as apprenticeships, seed exchanges and performances, so that the record does not become a substitute for the thing recorded.

    13.4 People’s Biodiversity Registers¶

    People’s Biodiversity Registers (PBRs) record the biological resources of a village or local body, their uses, and the knowledge associated with them. The methodology was developed in the 1990s by Madhav Gadgil and colleagues, who saw the registers both as a record of knowledge and as a tool for local communities to manage their resources and claim a share in benefits from their use (Gadgil et al. 2000). The Biological Diversity Act, 2002, and its rules require every local body to constitute a Biodiversity Management Committee and prepare a PBR.

    In practice many registers were prepared hastily, often by consultants or officials rather than by communities, especially after the National Green Tribunal set deadlines for their completion. Their quality varies greatly. They also raise questions of ownership and access: a register that lists a community’s knowledge of medicinal plants may, if publicly accessible, make that knowledge easier to appropriate.

    13.5 The Honey Bee Network¶

    The Honey Bee Network, started around 1988–89 by Anil K. Gupta at the Indian Institute of Management, Ahmedabad, set out to find, document and support grassroots innovations and traditional knowledge held by farmers, artisans and other people with little formal education. Its name reflects its principle: like a honey bee, it should take from the flowers without harming them and help them to pollinate. The network publishes newsletters in several Indian languages so that knowledge circulates among practitioners, insists that every entry be credited to the person who provided it, and supports innovators in seeking protection and investment. It led to the creation of the Society for Research and Initiatives for Sustainable Technologies and Institutions (SRISTI) and, in 2000, of the government-supported National Innovation Foundation. The network has compiled a very large database of innovations and practices.

    13.6 Archives and Digital Records¶

    Digital media make it possible to record performance, speech and practice in sound and image, to store large collections, and to return copies to communities. Important collections relating to Adivasi and folk traditions are held by the Archives and Research Centre for Ethnomusicology (ARCE) of the American Institute of Indian Studies in Gurugram, the Indira Gandhi National Centre for the Arts, the Sangeet Natak Akademi, the Central Institute of Indian Languages, and international language archives. Online platforms such as Sahapedia publish essays and recordings on folk traditions.

    Digital archives also raise problems. Many Adivasi villages lack reliable internet access, so communities may be the last to see their own records, while records held on distant servers can be used by anyone, including for commercial purposes. A recording of a song without its setting, meaning and rules of performance keeps only part of the knowledge. And the principle of open access can conflict with a community’s rules about who may hear or see certain things.

    Community archives, in which a community or its organisation holds and manages its own records, are one response. Another is to attach community-defined access conditions to records held in institutional archives. Adivasi communities have also used new media to speak for themselves: community radio stations in Adivasi languages; CGNet Swara, a mobile-phone service started in 2010 in central India on which people can record and listen to news reports in Gondi and Hindi; and video and social media made by young Adivasi journalists, film-makers and musicians. These are not archives in the usual sense, but they are part of how knowledge is now shared.

    At Tejgadh in eastern Gujarat, the Bhasha Research and Publication Centre founded by G. N. Devy established in 1999 an Adivasi Academy, which runs courses, a museum and a library, and publishes literature in Adivasi languages. State Tribal Research Institutes, set up in most states with large Adivasi populations, hold reports, photographs and recordings, though their records are often difficult to access.

    13.7 Museums and Return¶

    Museums in India and abroad hold large collections of Adivasi objects (weapons, ornaments, textiles, ritual objects, musical instruments) and, in some cases, human remains, collected by colonial officers, missionaries and anthropologists, often without the consent of the communities. Communities and scholars increasingly ask for these collections to be shared, interpreted with the communities, and in some cases returned. In Nagaland, a collaborative initiative begun in 2020 has worked with Naga communities on the return of Naga ancestral remains held in a British university museum, with attention to how communities wish such remains to be received.

    In India, museums such as the Indira Gandhi Rashtriya Manav Sangrahalaya in Bhopal, the state tribal museums and community museums run by Adivasi organisations have experimented with exhibitions designed and built by members of the communities represented. Such approaches change the museum from a place where communities are displayed into a place where they represent themselves.

    13.8 Contact, Protection and the Gaze¶

    The ethics of documentation are sharpest for the small communities of the Andaman Islands. The Jarawa had little peaceful contact with outsiders until the late 1990s. The Andaman Trunk Road, built through their reserve from the 1970s, brought settlers, poachers and tourists, some of whom treated the journey as a “human safari”. After a video of Jarawa women being made to dance for tourists was published in 2012, there was national outrage, and the authorities restricted tourist traffic and photography along the road. Disease, alcohol and exploitation brought by contact pose grave risks to such communities.

    The Sentinelese of North Sentinel Island have consistently rejected contact. Indian policy since the 1990s has been to protect them by leaving them alone, enforcing an exclusion zone around the island. In 2018 an American missionary who landed on the island illegally was killed by the islanders, an event that drew renewed attention to the policy of non-contact. For such communities, the most ethical form of “documentation” may be none at all: respect for their choice to remain apart.

    13.9 Genetic and Biomedical Research¶

    Adivasi communities have been studied not only by ethnographers but also by geneticists and medical researchers, for whom small, relatively isolated populations are of particular interest. Blood and DNA samples from Andaman Islanders, Munda-speaking peoples and others have contributed to major studies of human population history (Narasimhan et al. 2019). Such research can benefit communities, for example by identifying inherited conditions such as sickle cell disease and guiding health services. But it also raises questions of consent (were participants told what their samples would be used for?), of the storage and reuse of samples, of how findings about a community’s “origins” are reported and used in politics, and of benefit. Elsewhere in the world, indigenous communities have objected strongly to the use of their samples for purposes they had not agreed to. Ethical guidelines for research in India, issued by the Indian Council of Medical Research, treat tribal communities as needing special protection and call for community as well as individual consent.

    13.10 Languages¶

    Adivasi knowledge is held in Adivasi languages; their linguistic history and scripts are also discussed in Volume 10, Śabda Vidyā. The 2011 Census recorded 121 languages with ten thousand or more speakers (22 scheduled and 99 non-scheduled), grouping thousands of reported mother tongues under them (Office of the Registrar General and Census Commissioner, India 2013). Many Adivasi languages are counted as “mother tongues” under larger languages, which conceals their distinctness. The People’s Linguistic Survey of India, a volunteer project led by G. N. Devy from 2010, recorded some 780 languages and warned that many were in danger.

    The first systematic survey of the languages of India, the Linguistic Survey of India directed by George Abraham Grierson and published between 1903 and 1928, recorded specimens, grammatical sketches and vocabularies of hundreds of languages and dialects, including many Adivasi languages for which it remains an important early record. Its classifications, made largely through correspondence with officials and missionaries, were sometimes wrong, and many smaller languages were missed or merged with their neighbours. The People’s Linguistic Survey was conceived partly as a response, in which speakers themselves described their languages.

    UNESCO’s atlas of endangered languages (2009) listed nearly two hundred languages of India as vulnerable or endangered, more than for any other country, most of them Adivasi languages; its assessments, based on limited data, have been debated, but the trend they describe is not in doubt.

    Only a few Adivasi languages have official recognition: Santali and Bodo were added to the Eighth Schedule in 2003, and several states recognise tribal languages for education and administration (Sikkim, for example, recognises Lepcha and Limbu among its official languages, and Jharkhand recognises Santali, Mundari, Ho, Kharia, Kurukh and other languages as second official languages). Most Adivasi children are still taught in a language that is not their mother tongue, which contributes to high drop-out rates and to the loss of the language within a few generations.

    13.10.1 Speakers and Status¶

    Census figures show both the size of the largest Adivasi languages and the extent of language shift (Table 13.1). Bhili/Bhilodi, with about ten million speakers in 2011, was the largest language of India outside the Eighth Schedule, larger than several scheduled languages; Santali had more than seven million. Yet the number of Gondi speakers, about three million, is far smaller than the number of people who identify as Gond, many of whom now speak Hindi, Marathi or Telugu as their first language. The figures also conceal internal variety: “Bhili/Bhilodi” and “Gondi” are each umbrella labels under which the census groups many mother tongues that their speakers name separately.

    Demands for inclusion in the Eighth Schedule have been made for many of these languages, among them Bhili, Gondi, Ho, Mundari, Kurukh, Khasi, Garo and Kokborok. Inclusion brings recognition by the Sahitya Akademi, use in national examinations and a stronger claim on state support; it also, as the Santali case shows, raises the question of which script and which variety will be standard (Chapter 8, Section 8.14).

    13.10.2 Standardisation¶

    Writing a language down raises the question of which dialect becomes the standard. Gondi, for example, is spoken in many varieties across several states, some of them barely mutually intelligible, and speakers have been schooled in Hindi, Marathi, Telugu or Odia. Efforts by Gond organisations and linguists to compile a common Gondi vocabulary and standard have had to balance unity with respect for local speech. In the most sustained of these, a series of meetings held over about four years and organised by the citizen-journalism network CGNet Swara, with the Indira Gandhi National Centre for the Arts, brought together Gondi speakers from six states to agree on common words: for each word the variants of the different regions were compared and one was chosen by vote. The resulting standard vocabulary of some three thousand words, printed in Devanagari, was released in 2018; its compilers regarded it as a first step towards a full dictionary. Whether such a standard will be taken up in schools depends on state governments, since Gondi has little official status in any state. Similar debates surround the choice of script (Chapter 8). Standardisation can strengthen a language by giving it a written norm for schools and media; it can also marginalise the speech of smaller groups within the community.

    13.10.3 Documentation and Revival¶

    Language documentation projects record grammar, vocabulary and texts, often with community members as researchers. The Central Institute of Indian Languages in Mysuru (founded 1969) has worked on tribal languages and on mother-tongue-based education. Adivasi communities themselves have developed scripts (Ol Chiki for Santali, Warang Citi for Ho, Tolong Siki for Kurukh), published textbooks and literature, and set up schools teaching in their languages. Language revival is most successful when the language is used in schools, media and public life, not only recorded.

    13.10.4 Radio, Telephone and the Internet¶

    Since the 1990s the means of recording and circulating Adivasi speech have passed increasingly into Adivasi hands. Regional stations of All India Radio have long broadcast programmes and news in some Adivasi languages, but the decisions about content were made elsewhere. Community radio, licensed in India from the mid-2000s, has allowed some stations in Adivasi regions to broadcast in local languages under local management. In central India CGNet Swara, begun in 2010 by the journalist Shubhranshu Choudhary, adapted the idea of citizen journalism to places with mobile phones but little internet: callers record reports and songs by telephone in Gondi, Hindi and other languages, which are checked by moderators and made available to other callers and online. Its reports have drawn attention to unpaid wages, failures of the ration system and police abuses, and it has also served as an archive of songs and stories in Gondi.

    The encoding of Adivasi scripts in Unicode (Table 8.1) has made it possible to type them on phones and computers, and Santali has had its own edition of Wikipedia, in the Ol Chiki script, since 2018. Video-sharing platforms carry a large and largely unstudied production of songs, dance and short films in Santali, Kurukh, Mundari, Ho, Gondi and the languages of the North-East, made by Adivasi musicians and studios for Adivasi audiences in India and among migrants. Documentary film-makers such as the Oraon director Biju Toppo, working with the Ranchi collective Akhra, have recorded movements against displacement from within. These media do not by themselves protect a language or a body of knowledge; but they reverse, at least in part, the history described at the beginning of this chapter, in which recording was something done to Adivasi communities by others.

    Key Points

    • Documentation has preserved knowledge but has also been extractive; consent, community control and benefit are essential.

    • People’s Biodiversity Registers, the Honey Bee Network and digital archives are important but imperfect tools.

    • For communities such as the Sentinelese, respect for their choice of isolation outweighs any wish to document.

    • Language is the foundation of knowledge; mother-tongue education and community scripts are key to its survival.
