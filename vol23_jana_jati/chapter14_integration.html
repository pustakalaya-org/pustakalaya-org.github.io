    Chapter 14: Indigenous Knowledge in Policy and Education¶

    14.1 Introduction: Beyond the Dichotomy¶

    This volume has described the ecological, medical, literary, artistic, political, hydrological, culinary and calendrical knowledge of Adivasi and folk communities. This final chapter asks how such knowledge can be given its due place in the policies and institutions of a modern state, in ways that serve the communities who hold it.

    Integration carries risks. It can become appropriation: the selective use of indigenous knowledge without recognition or benefit to its holders. It can become distortion: the extraction of “useful” elements from living systems while ignoring their context. And it can become patronage: the display of indigenous knowledge as heritage while its holders remain poor and powerless. The approach favoured here is sometimes called epistemic pluralism.

    Epistemic Pluralism

    Epistemic pluralism holds that different knowledge systems can each be valid in their own domains, and that the most productive relation between them is dialogue rather than hierarchy. It rejects both the view that one system (usually modern science) is the only legitimate source of knowledge on every question, and the view that all claims are equally valid and cannot be compared. Where claims overlap, for example on whether a remedy works or a water structure recharges groundwater, they can and should be tested; where they concern meaning, value or identity, communities speak for themselves (Agrawal 1995; Sillitoe 1998).

    14.2 Adivasi Knowledge and “Indian Knowledge Systems”¶

    This volume belongs to a series on Bhāratīya Jñāna Paramparā, the knowledge traditions of India, a subject that has received new official attention since the National Education Policy of 2020. That framework raises a question that Adivasi scholars have put sharply: is Adivasi knowledge to be treated as part of a single Indian tradition whose centre is Sanskrit learning, or as a set of traditions in their own right?

    The evidence of this volume points to the second answer. Adivasi knowledge has interacted with Sanskritic, Islamic, Christian and modern scientific knowledge for centuries, but it was developed by peoples with their own languages, religions, histories and institutions, and it should not be described as a folk or “lost” form of the Vedic or classical traditions. Presenting it in Sanskrit categories, however respectfully, risks repeating the absorptive framing that Adivasi movements have rejected. A plural understanding of Indian knowledge, in which Adivasi, Dalit, Islamic, Christian, regional and classical traditions each have their own place, is both more accurate and more just.

    14.3 Education¶

    14.3.1 Mother Tongue and Curriculum¶

    For most Adivasi children, school begins in a language they do not speak at home. Educationists have long argued that early schooling in the mother tongue, with a gradual transition to the regional language and English, improves learning and helps languages survive. Odisha has run a multilingual education programme in several tribal languages since the mid-2000s, and other states have produced primers in Santali, Gondi, Kurukh, Mundari, Ho and other languages.

    The National Education Policy 2020 recommends that, wherever possible, the medium of instruction until at least Grade 5 should be the home language or mother tongue, and it calls for the inclusion of Indian knowledge systems, including tribal knowledge and indigenous and traditional ways of learning, in the curriculum. These are welcome commitments. Their realisation depends on teachers who speak the children’s languages, materials written with communities, and a curriculum that treats Adivasi knowledge as knowledge rather than as folklore.

    14.3.2 Residential Schooling¶

    Much Adivasi education takes place in residential schools and hostels: government āśram schools, Ekalavya Model Residential Schools (from the late 1990s), mission schools and large private institutions. They have brought education to remote areas, but they also separate children from their families, languages and the practical learning of village life. The experience of residential schooling in other countries, where it contributed to the loss of indigenous languages and to serious abuse, is a warning. Many Adivasi educators argue for schools rooted in the community, with local teachers and calendars that fit the agricultural year.

    14.3.3 Universities¶

    The Indira Gandhi National Tribal University at Amarkantak in Madhya Pradesh was established by Act of Parliament in 2007, with a mandate to promote higher education and research on tribal art, culture, traditions, languages and knowledge. Central tribal universities have since been established in Andhra Pradesh and Telangana, and universities such as North-Eastern Hill University in Shillong and Rajiv Gandhi University in Arunachal Pradesh have long had programmes on the cultures of the North-East. Ranchi University’s department of tribal and regional languages, built up by Ram Dayal Munda and others from the 1980s, was a pioneer in teaching Adivasi languages at university level.

    14.4 Biodiversity Law and Traditional Knowledge¶

    International and Indian law on biodiversity and traditional knowledge, the Convention on Biological Diversity, the Nagoya Protocol and the Biological Diversity Act, is explained in Sections 11.4 and 12.10. For the purposes of this chapter the main point is that benefit-sharing payments under these laws have so far been small and have rarely reached the communities whose knowledge was used.

    14.5 Forest Self-Government: Mendha-Lekha¶

    The village of Mendha-Lekha in Gadchiroli district of Maharashtra, a Gond village, has become a symbol of Adivasi forest self-government. From the 1980s, under the slogan māvā nāṭe māvā rāj (“our village, our rule”), its gram sabha took decisions by consensus, refused permission for outsiders to cut its forest, protected the forest itself, and set up study circles to discuss issues ranging from forest management to the rights of women. Its leaders, among them Devaji Tofa, worked with supporters from outside the village, while insisting that decisions be made by the village.

    In 2009 Mendha-Lekha was among the first villages in India to have its community forest rights recognised under the Forest Rights Act. In 2011 it became the first gram sabha to obtain a transit pass to harvest and sell bamboo from its own forest, a right long monopolised by the forest department and paper mills, and it used the income for village development and forest protection. The village later placed its land under collective ownership. Following its example, many other gram sabhas in Gadchiroli and elsewhere in Maharashtra and Odisha have claimed community forest rights and begun to manage and market forest produce, with varying success.

    The experience of Mendha-Lekha shows what PESA and the FRA can mean in practice when a community is organised and has time and support. It also shows that self-government is hard work, depends on leadership and unity, and can be undermined by conflicts within and outside the village.

    14.6 Forest Produce and Livelihoods¶

    For many Adivasi households, forest produce is the main source of cash, and the terms on which it is sold determine whether forest knowledge brings any benefit. Historically, collectors sold to traders and state agencies at low prices and were often paid in kind or in advance against debt. Several policies have tried to change this. In 2013 the central government introduced minimum support prices for a list of minor forest produce, under which state agencies are to buy produce if market prices fall below the support price; and from 2018 the Van Dhan programme has supported groups of forest-produce gatherers, many of them women, to process and market produce. Where gram sabhas hold community forest rights, as at Mendha-Lekha, they can sell produce such as bamboo and tendu leaves directly. The Forest Rights Act also requires that titles to land be issued jointly in the names of both spouses where the claimant is married, a provision that, where implemented, gives Adivasi women a recorded right in land for the first time. These measures have been unevenly implemented, but they point to the principle that the people who hold forest knowledge should share in its value.

    14.7 Tourism¶

    Tourism is increasingly promoted in Adivasi regions as a source of income: homestays in Nagaland, Meghalaya and Arunachal Pradesh, festivals such as the Hornbill Festival, visits to living root bridges and sacred forests, and craft villages. Community-run tourism, in which villages decide how many visitors to receive, set the rules and keep the income, can bring benefits and pride in local knowledge. Tourism controlled by outside operators has often done the opposite, treating communities as spectacles, as in the “human safaris” to the Jarawa reserve (Chapter 13), damaging sacred sites, and taking most of the profit. The difference lies, again, in who controls the decisions.

    14.8 Recognition of Knowledge Holders¶

    Recognition matters. In recent years national honours have been given to several holders of community knowledge: the Kani healer Lakshmikutty (Padma Shri, 2018) and Tulsi Gowda of the Halakki Vokkaliga community of Karnataka, known as an “encyclopedia of the forest” for her knowledge of tree species and her work in raising seedlings over decades (Padma Shri, 2020), among others. Artists such as Jivya Soma Mashe, Bhuri Bai and Mithila painters have received the same honours (Chapter 9). Such recognition raises the status of knowledge that was once dismissed. It is most meaningful when accompanied by support for the communities and practices from which the knowledge comes.

    14.9 Health Care¶

    India’s Ministry of AYUSH (Āyurveda, Yoga and Naturopathy, Unani, Siddha, Sowa-Rigpa and Homoeopathy) supports the classical systems of traditional medicine. Folk and tribal healers have little place in this framework. Several states and non-governmental organisations have documented local health traditions, promoted the cultivation of medicinal plants by communities, and trained village healers to recognise conditions that need referral. Accredited Social Health Activists (ASHAs), village women appointed under the National Rural Health Mission from 2005, have become a bridge between communities and health facilities in many Adivasi areas.

    The aim, from the point of view of public health, is not to make folk healers into substitutes for doctors, but to make good health care available to Adivasi communities, to respect patients’ own understanding of illness, and to evaluate traditional remedies honestly (Chapter 7). Adivasi communities have some of the worst health indicators in India, including high rates of malaria, tuberculosis, malnutrition and maternal and child mortality, and sickle cell disease is common in many central Indian communities (Government of India 2014). Respect for traditional knowledge is no substitute for the public health services to which they are entitled.

    14.10 Lessons from Elsewhere¶

    Other countries offer both warnings and examples.

    • Canada: from the late nineteenth century until the 1990s, indigenous children were sent to residential schools designed to remove them from their languages and cultures. The Truth and Reconciliation Commission, which reported in 2015, documented the abuse and loss that resulted, and called for the restoration of indigenous languages and knowledge in education.

    • New Zealand: from 1982 Māori communities established kōhanga reo (“language nests”), pre-schools in which young children are immersed in the Māori language by elders. The movement, followed by Māori-medium schools, helped to reverse the decline of the language and has been imitated around the world.

    • Norway, Sweden and Finland: the Sámi have elected parliaments with advisory and some administrative powers over language, culture and reindeer herding.

    • Latin America: the constitutions of Bolivia and Ecuador recognise indigenous peoples’ self-government and, in Ecuador’s constitution of 2008, rights of nature itself, partly inspired by indigenous ideas of the earth.

    These examples differ greatly from Indian conditions, and none is without problems. But they show that indigenous languages can be revived, that self-government can be given institutional form, and that the damage done by assimilationist schooling can be long-lasting.

    14.11 Research Partnerships¶

    Much of the knowledge in this volume reached print through research by outsiders. The principles that ought to govern future research with Adivasi communities are now widely agreed, even if often ignored. Research should begin only with the free, prior and informed consent of the community, given through its own institutions, as well as of the individuals concerned. Communities should help decide what is studied, so that research answers their questions as well as the researcher’s. Those who contribute knowledge or labour should be credited by name, as authors where appropriate, rather than reduced to anonymous “informants”. Findings should go back to the community in its own language and in a usable form; any commercial use should be governed by agreements that benefit the community, as the Biological Diversity Act requires; and a community may decline to take part or restrict what is published (Section 13.1). Adivasi universities, research institutes and organisations, and Adivasi scholars in mainstream institutions, are increasingly setting these terms themselves.

    14.12 A Dialogue of Knowledges¶

    The aim of the approaches discussed here is not to preserve Adivasi knowledge in museums, databases or policy documents, but to make it possible for communities to continue, develop and apply their knowledge, on their own land and in their own languages, in dialogue with other knowledge systems. Such a dialogue treats indigenous knowledge as knowledge, not as raw material to be validated and appropriated by others. It tests claims where they can be tested, and it expects scientists and officials to learn from communities as well as to teach them. Above all it depends on material conditions, secure rights to land and forest, self-government, livelihoods, health and education, without which knowledge cannot be practised.

    Modern Relevance: Indigenous Knowledge and Sustainability

    International bodies such as the Intergovernmental Science-Policy Platform on Biodiversity and Ecosystem Services (IPBES) and the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change now recognise the knowledge of indigenous peoples and local communities as relevant to biodiversity conservation and climate adaptation (Nakashima et al. 2012). Much of the world’s remaining biodiversity lies in lands managed by indigenous peoples. In India, as elsewhere, conservation that excludes or displaces forest communities has often been both unjust and ineffective.

    14.12.1 The Long View¶

    The knowledge described in this volume was built up by many generations of observation, experiment, argument and teaching, and it continues to change. What it calls for is respect, rather than nostalgia or the absorption of Adivasi traditions into someone else’s story: expressed in political action that secures the rights of the communities who hold this knowledge, in education that takes it seriously, and in scholarship that listens to what Adivasi people say about themselves.

    14.13 Proposals and Their Difficulties¶

    Earlier chapters have described many proposals made by Adivasi organisations, scholars and official bodies such as the High Level Committee of 2014 (Government of India 2014). They are gathered here, with the objections they meet, so that the reader can weigh them rather than take them as settled.

    On land and self-government, the proposals are to implement PESA in every Fifth Schedule state without diluting the powers of the gram sabha; to use the Governor’s powers under the Fifth Schedule to protect land; to implement the Forest Rights Act fully, above all its community forest rights; to require the free, prior and informed consent of the gram sabha for projects on Adivasi land, as in the Niyamgiri decision; and to resettle fairly those already displaced. The objections are of two kinds. State governments and industry argue that a village veto over mines, dams and roads would let a few hundred people block projects that serve millions. Some conservationists argue that individual forest titles can fragment wildlife habitat (Section 11.4.4). The second objection is partly empirical and can be tested; the first is a question of how the costs of development are to be shared, which law alone cannot settle.

    On customary institutions, the proposal is to recognise village councils and customary law while guaranteeing the rights of women and dissenters within them, and to enforce the laws against witch-hunting. The difficulty is that the two halves pull against each other: strengthening customary authority can strengthen the exclusion of women from land and councils (Section 11.7), and Adivasi women’s organisations have been among the sharpest critics of uncritical praise of custom.

    On knowledge and its use, the proposals are to protect oral traditional knowledge in law, with community control and benefit-sharing; to support documentation and archives controlled by communities, and to return copies of old records to them; to record sacred groves and community lands correctly in land records; and to test widely used remedies and weather signs with their holders as partners. Here the difficulty is practical. Knowledge that is shared across many villages, or across the line between tribe and caste, has no single owner with whom benefits can be shared, and the registers meant to protect knowledge can make it easier to copy (Section 13.4).

    On education, health and food, the proposals are mother-tongue education in the early years with curricula prepared with communities; health, nutrition and education services of the quality other citizens receive; the inclusion of millets and gathered foods in public nutrition programmes; and a census code for Sarna and other indigenous religions for those who wish to use it. These meet fewer principled objections, but they cost money and require teachers, health workers and officials who speak Adivasi languages, which most states have been slow to provide.

    None of these proposals is new, and most have been made by Adivasi organisations themselves for decades. Whether they are adopted is a political question. What this volume can contribute is an account of the knowledge at stake, and of the evidence for and against the claims made on its behalf.

    Key Points

    • Integration of indigenous knowledge risks appropriation, distortion and patronage; dialogue among knowledge systems is the goal.

    • Mother-tongue education and curricula prepared with communities can give Adivasi knowledge its place in schools.

    • Mendha-Lekha shows what PESA and the FRA can achieve when a community is organised.

    • Secure rights, fair markets and good public services are the material conditions for the survival of Adivasi knowledge.

    Further Reading¶

    • Agrawal (1995): a critique of the opposition between indigenous and scientific knowledge.

    • Brokensha et al. (1980): an early collection on indigenous knowledge and development.

    • Warren (1991): indigenous knowledge in agricultural development.

    • Gadgil (1987): the links between cultural and biological diversity.

    • Guru and Sarukkai (2012): a debate on who has the authority to theorise experience, with lessons for the study of community knowledge.
