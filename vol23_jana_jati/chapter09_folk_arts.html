    Chapter 9: Folk Arts: Painting, Craft, Dance and Visual Knowledge¶

    9.1 Introduction: Art, Ritual and Meaning¶

    The white-on-earth paintings of the Warli, the crowded and brilliant panels of Mithila, the dotted and patterned animals of Pardhan Gond painters, the ritual wall paintings of the Saura and the horse-filled Pithora murals of the Rathwa and Bhilala are among the best-known visual traditions of India. Most began as ritual art: paintings made on house walls or floors for weddings, harvests, vows and the honouring of deities and ancestors. In the twentieth century many of them moved onto paper and canvas, entered markets and museums, and produced named artists of national and international reputation.

    This chapter looks at these traditions both as ritual and as art. Crafts and textiles as a whole are treated in Volume 17, Śilpa–Kāru, and folk and tribal music, dance and theatre in Volume 9, Saṅgīta–Nāṭya. It avoids two misreadings: that the images are “mere decoration”, and that every line encodes a hidden cosmological message. Painters work within shared conventions, and those conventions carry religious and social meaning; but they are also individual artists who innovate, sign their work and earn their living by it.

    9.2 Warli Painting¶

    The Warli live in the hills of the northern Konkan and the Sahyadri in Maharashtra’s Thane and Palghar districts and in neighbouring Gujarat. Their traditional painting is done by women on the mud-plastered walls of the house, in white rice paste on a ground of earth or red ochre, using circles, triangles and lines: two triangles joined at the tip make a human figure.

    9.2.1 The Chauk¶

    The central ritual painting is the chauk, a square frame painted for a wedding. Inside it is Pālaghaṭā, the goddess of fertility; around it are scenes of village life, dancing, farming, hunting and animals. The painting creates a sacred space for the marriage and invokes the goddess’s blessing on the couple and their household.

    9.2.2 Scenes of Life¶

    Around the ritual core, Warli paintings depict the tārpā dance, in which a chain of dancers follows the player of the tārpā wind instrument; sowing, harvest and threshing; hunting and fishing; trees full of birds; and the village with its houses and animals. The images record the rhythms of the agricultural year and of community life. Yashodhara Dalmia’s study remains the fullest account of the ritual setting of these paintings (Dalmia 1988).

    9.2.3 Jivya Soma Mashe and the New Warli Painting¶

    From the early 1970s, after Warli painting was noticed by officials of the government’s handicrafts programmes, artists began to paint on paper and cloth for sale. The most celebrated was Jivya Soma Mashe (1934–2018) of Ganjad in Dahanu taluka (then in Thane, now in Palghar district), a man working in what had been a women’s tradition, who developed a dense and dynamic personal style. He was awarded the Padma Shri in 2011. Warli motifs are now used widely in textiles, advertising and decoration, often without any benefit to Warli artists. This raises questions of recognition and intellectual property, which are discussed below.

    9.3 Mithila Painting¶

    Mithila (Madhubani) painting of northern Bihar is a caste-society tradition rather than an Adivasi one, and its history as a craft (the wall paintings of the nuptial chamber, kohbar, seen by W. G. Archer after the earthquake of 1934, and the move to paper during the drought of the late 1960s) is told in Volume 17, Śilpa–Kāru. It belongs here for what it shows about caste and style. Brahmin women developed the brightly coloured bharnī (filled) style with mythological subjects, and Kayastha women the fine-line kachnī style; from the 1970s women of the Dusadh and other Dalit communities created their own styles, drawing on tattoo designs (godnā) and on the epic of their hero Raja Salhesh. The painter Jagdamba Devi received the Padma Shri in 1975, and Sita Devi, Ganga Devi, Mahasundari Devi and Baua Devi later became widely known. The Dalit painters’ achievement challenged the assumption that the tradition belonged only to upper-caste households, and shows how folk art changes with the social position of those who make it.

    9.4 Pardhan Gond Painting¶

    Modern Gond painting begins with Jangarh Singh Shyam (c. 1962–2001), a Pardhan Gond from Patangarh in Mandla (now Dindori) district of Madhya Pradesh. In the early 1980s he was discovered by a team led by the artist J. Swaminathan, who was collecting tribal and folk art for the new Bharat Bhavan arts centre in Bhopal. Jangarh moved to Bhopal and, working first with poster colours and then with acrylics on paper and canvas, created a powerful new idiom from the motifs of Gond wall painting, tattoo and the stories the Pardhans sang. His work was exhibited in India and abroad, including at the Centre Pompidou in Paris in 1989. He died in 2001 in Japan, where he had gone to work at a museum.

    Jangarh’s style, sometimes called Jangarh kalam, was taken up by relatives and others from Patangarh and neighbouring villages, among them his wife Nankusia Shyam, Bhajju Shyam, Venkat Raman Singh Shyam, Ram Singh Urveti and Durga Bai Vyam. Characteristic features are large flat forms of animals, trees and deities filled with dense patterns of dots, dashes and lines, each artist developing a personal pattern as a kind of signature.

    Pardhan Gond artists have taken their art in new directions. Bhajju Shyam’s The London Jungle Book (2004) depicts London as seen by a Gond artist on his first journey abroad (Shyam et al. 2004). Durga Bai Vyam and Subhash Vyam illustrated Bhimayana (2011), a graphic biography of B. R. Ambedkar that turned Gond painting to the depiction of caste discrimination (Natarajan and Anand 2011).

    9.5 Saura Wall Paintings¶

    The Sora (Saura) of the hills of southern Odisha paint ritual pictures called iḍital on the walls of their houses. They are made at the request of a shaman, usually to honour or placate a deity or one of the dead, and depict figures of people, horses, elephants, trees, the sun and moon, and scenes of ritual within a framed space. Elwin described them as “icons” and recorded many in his study of Saora religion (Elwin 1955, 1951); Piers Vitebsky places them in the context of the Sora’s continuing conversation with their dead (Vitebsky 1993). The paintings are addressed as much to the spirit world as to human viewers.

    9.6 Pithora and Bhil Painting¶

    The Pithora paintings of the Rathwa, Bhilala and related communities of eastern Gujarat and western Madhya Pradesh are large, brightly coloured murals painted on the inner walls of houses in fulfilment of a vow to the deity Baba Pithora, for example after recovery from illness or the birth of a child. They are painted by specialist painters (lakhārā) and consecrated by the priest (baḍvā), who reads the painting and recites the associated narrative. Horses, the mounts of the gods, dominate the composition, together with deities, animals, people and, increasingly, modern objects such as aeroplanes and motorcycles.

    Bhil painters have also developed new styles on paper and canvas. Bhuri Bai, a Bhil artist from Jhabua district who began painting at Bharat Bhavan in the 1980s, became one of the best-known Adivasi artists in India and received the Padma Shri in 2021.

    9.7 Other Wall-Painting Traditions¶

    In the Hazaribagh region of Jharkhand, women of several communities paint the mud walls of their houses with sohrāī paintings at the harvest festival and with khovar paintings, made by scratching through layers of coloured earth, for weddings. In Odisha and Chhattisgarh, house walls are decorated with relief and painting at festivals. These traditions are mostly anonymous, ephemeral (renewed each year) and made by women; they have only recently been studied and marketed.

    9.7.1 Painted Scrolls for the Santal¶

    In the Santal country of Jharkhand and West Bengal, a community of travelling scroll painters known as jādu paṭuā made painted scrolls for Santal patrons, illustrating the Santal account of creation, the origin of the clans and the fate of the soul after death. Their most striking practice, described by twentieth-century observers, was the “eye-giving” picture (cakṣudān paṭ) of a person who had recently died: the painter brought the bereaved family a picture of the dead person without pupils in the eyes and, on receiving a payment, painted them in, which was believed to allow the soul to find its way. The jādu paṭuā were not Santals, and their art shows the close exchanges between Adivasi patrons and caste specialists in eastern India.

    9.8 Metal, Clay and Body: Craft Traditions¶

    9.8.1 Iron-Smelting: Asur and Agaria¶

    Iron-working is an old craft of forest India, and two Adivasi communities of central and eastern India were known above all as smelters: the Asur of the Netarhat plateau and neighbouring hills of Jharkhand, and the Agaria of the Maikal hills and adjoining regions of central India. Elwin’s monograph on the Agaria, written when smelting was still practised, describes the craft and the myths attached to it (Elwin 1942).

    The smelters dug iron ore from surface deposits, roasted and broke it, and smelted it with charcoal in small clay furnaces, the draught supplied by bellows worked by hand or foot. The product was a spongy bloom of iron mixed with slag, which was reheated and hammered to drive out the slag before it could be forged into tools. The work required knowledge of which ores and which woods to use, how to build and line a furnace, how to control the draught and how long to fire. Smelting was household work, in which gathering ore and charcoal and working the bellows were shared among its members. The process belongs to the general family of bloomery smelting whose history in the subcontinent, and the archaeometallurgical evidence for it, are discussed in Volume 14, Rasāyana.

    The craft carried meaning as well as technique. Among the Agaria, Elwin recorded myths in which the first smelters’ furnaces scorch the earth until a deity destroys them, a story close to the Munda narrative of the Asur and Siṅboṅgā (Section 8.5); the furnace and the bellows were treated with ritual care. The smelters also had a recognised place in the village economy, exchanging iron and tools with cultivators for grain.

    Bloomery smelting declined from the late nineteenth century. Cheap imported and factory iron undercut village iron; colonial forest law restricted the cutting of trees for charcoal; and in parts of central India furnaces were taxed (Elwin 1942). By the mid-twentieth century most Agaria and Asur families had turned to cultivation, labour or blacksmithing with bought iron. The Asur are now listed as a Particularly Vulnerable Tribal Group, and their history has been taken up in Hindi fiction (Section 8.14). A technical knowledge can thus disappear within two generations once its raw materials and its market are taken away, even while the community that held it survives.

    9.8.2 Iron and Bamboo Today¶

    In Bastar, blacksmiths forge wrought-iron lamps, figures of deities, horsemen and animals, which, like Dhokra brass, are now made largely for sale. In North-East India, bamboo and cane are the basic materials of daily life: houses, floors, baskets, mats, fish traps, containers, hats and furniture are made from them, and the knowledge of selecting, splitting, weaving and treating bamboo is shared by men and women in every village.

    9.8.3 Dhokra, Terracotta and Pottery¶

    Dhokra, the lost-wax brass casting of itinerant and settled craft communities in Bastar, Odisha, Jharkhand and West Bengal, made figures of deities, animals, lamps and measuring bowls for Adivasi patrons and their shrines before it found a wider market; its technique is described in Volume 17, Śilpa–Kāru. In Bastar, potters (kumhār) make the terracotta horses, elephants and other figures that Gond, Halba and other communities offer at the shrines of village and clan deities, where large groups of such horses accumulate over the years. The hand-built black stone-and-clay pots of the Tangkhul Naga village of Longpi in Manipur are another case of a community craft that has found new buyers far from home.

    9.8.4 Weaving¶

    Textiles carry social information, and weaving traditions of Adivasi India are particularly rich in the North-East. Among the Naga peoples each community, and often each village, has its own repertoire of shawl designs, worn by men and women according to age, status and achievement; some designs were reserved for men who had distinguished themselves in war or had given the great “feasts of merit”, such as the Ao warrior’s shawl (tsungkotepsu). Weaving on the back-strap loin loom has traditionally been women’s work. Bodo women weave the dokhonā, the wrapped garment of Bodo women, on looms kept in almost every household; Mizo women weave the puan, whose patterns identify occasions and sometimes communities. In the Nilgiris, Toda women embroider the white cotton cloak (puthkuli) worn by men and women with bands of red and black geometric patterns, worked by counting threads rather than following a drawn design; the cloak is worn on ritual occasions and at funerals. In central and eastern India, most cloth worn by Adivasis was traditionally woven for them by specialist weaving castes. At Kotpad in the Koraput district of Odisha, weavers of the Mirgan community weave cotton saris and shawls dyed with the red of the āl tree (Morinda) root, worn especially by Adivasi women of the region; the cloth has received Geographical Indication registration. As with painting, weaving has moved between the household, the local market and national and international markets, with mixed results for the weavers.

    9.8.5 Tattoo¶

    Tattooing (godnā) was widespread among women of central Indian communities such as the Baiga, Gond and Korku, as well as among many castes. Tattoos were applied in stages from girlhood to marriage by specialist women, using needles and lampblack or plant pigments. Elwin recorded Baiga beliefs about tattoos, among them the widespread belief that tattoos, unlike ornaments, go with a woman after death (Elwin 1939). Among some North-East communities, facial and body tattoos marked clan, status and achievement; among the Konyak Naga, facial tattoos were associated with head-hunting and are now borne only by the oldest generation. Among the Apatani of Arunachal Pradesh, women once wore large nose plugs and facial tattoos; the practice was abandoned from the 1970s, and today only older women bear these marks. Tattooing in these forms has largely ceased, partly because of missionary and reformist disapproval and partly because younger women prefer not to be marked.

    9.9 The House and the Village¶

    Architecture is among the most visible forms of Adivasi knowledge. Houses are built from local materials (mud, wood, bamboo, thatch, stone) by the household and its neighbours, using techniques suited to the climate and to the community’s way of life.

    • Santal houses have thick mud walls, often painted in bands of white, black and red earth and sometimes decorated with relief designs, set around a clean courtyard; the walls are renewed before the major festivals.

    • Toda houses, grouped in small hamlets (mund), were half-barrel-shaped huts of bamboo and thatch, with a very small entrance through which one had to crawl, and the Toda dairy-temples had a similar form; Rivers described them in his study of 1906 (Rivers 1906).

    • Naga villages were traditionally built on hilltops for defence, around large houses and the morung or men’s house (Section 11.12).

    • Khasi houses were traditionally oval in plan, with stone plinths and thatched roofs suited to the heavy rainfall of the hills (Gurdon 1907).

    • Apatani villages are dense clusters of houses on stilts, surrounded by the terraced fields and bamboo groves of the valley (Fürer-Haimendorf 1962).

    Wood carving was highly developed among the Naga (Hutton 1921; Mills 1926). The posts, beams and façades of morungs and chiefs’ houses, especially among the Konyak, were carved with figures of hornbills, mithun heads, tigers, human figures and heads, which recorded the status, feasts and exploits of the village and its men. Log drums, house posts and grave effigies were carved by specialists. With the end of head-hunting and the spread of Christianity, much carving lost its ritual meaning, and many fine pieces were removed to museums; carving continues today for churches, public buildings and the market.

    Government housing programmes have often replaced these houses with concrete structures that are hotter in summer, colder in winter and require purchased materials. Architects and community organisations have argued for housing designs that build on local materials and knowledge, and the Indira Gandhi Rashtriya Manav Sangrahalaya in Bhopal preserves full-scale examples of Adivasi houses from many regions, built by members of the communities.

    9.10 Masks and Dance¶

    9.10.1 Chhau¶

    Chhau is a dance tradition of eastern India with three main styles: Purulia (West Bengal) and Seraikela (Jharkhand), in which dancers wear masks, and Mayurbhanj (Odisha), which is performed without masks. It combines martial movements with stories from the epics and Purāṇas, and was inscribed on UNESCO’s Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity in 2010. The masks, especially the large papier-mâché and clay masks of Purulia, are made by specialist mask-makers in villages such as Charida.

    9.10.2 Dance in Adivasi Life¶

    Dance is part of almost every festival, ritual and celebration in Adivasi life, and is usually communal rather than a performance by specialists for an audience. Some examples (see also Table 9.1):

    • Karam (Karmā): danced across Jharkhand, Chhattisgarh and Odisha by Oraon, Munda, Gond, Baiga and many others around a branch of the karam tree planted in the village during the monsoon month of Bhādo, with prayers for good crops and the welfare of brothers and sisters.

    • Gaur dance: danced by the Maria of Bastar, whom Grigson called “Bison-horn Maria” after their headdresses of gaur (Indian bison) horns, feathers and cowries (Grigson 1938).

    • Bihu: the festivals of Assam (Rongāli or Bohāg Bihu in April, Kongāli or Kāti Bihu in October, Bhogāli or Māgh Bihu in January) are shared by Assamese and many tribal communities, such as the Bodo, whose spring festival is Bwisāgu, and the Mising, whose spring agricultural festival is Ali-Āye-Ligaṅ.

    • Cheraw: the Mizo bamboo dance, in which dancers step in and out of bamboo poles clapped together in rhythm.

    9.11 Music and Instruments¶

    Music accompanies festival dance, ritual, work and courtship in Adivasi life, and its instruments and repertoires are independent of the classical saṅgīta codified in texts such as the Nāṭyaśāstra and the Saṅgītaratnākara; folk and tribal music as a whole is surveyed in Volume 9, Saṅgīta–Nāṭya. Drums are central. In Jharkhand and neighbouring regions the māndar, a double-headed clay-bodied drum, accompanies the dances of the Oraon, Munda and others; Santal music uses the large kettledrum tamak’ and the barrel drum tumdak’. In Nagaland the great log drums carved from single tree trunks, found in Konyak, Ao and other villages, were used to summon and signal as well as for music. The Warli tārpā leads a chain of dancers who follow the player’s movements, a scene often painted on Warli walls, and the Pardhans sing the Gond narratives and clan genealogies to the bānā (Chapter 8).     9.12 Music and Performance Today¶

    Adivasi music and dance are not only village traditions. In Jharkhand, songs in Nagpuri, Kurukh, Mundari and Santali circulate on cassettes, discs and now online video, and a Santali film and music industry has developed. In Nagaland, the state government’s Hornbill Festival, held each December since 2000 and since 2003 at the Naga Heritage Village at Kisama near Kohima, presents the dances, songs, games and crafts of the Naga communities to visitors. Musicians such as the Tetseo Sisters, who perform the polyphonic songs (li) of the Chakhesang Naga, and many rock, hip-hop and folk-fusion groups across the North-East combine traditional songs with new forms. Festivals of this kind have been criticised for turning culture into spectacle, but they have also given young people new reasons to learn the songs and dances of their communities.

    9.13 Artists, Markets and Rights¶

    The movement of ritual art into markets has transformed it. It has given artists, many of them women and many from communities with few other sources of cash income, a livelihood and public recognition; it has also produced new styles, subjects and individual careers. At the same time it raises difficult questions:

    • Authorship: art that was once anonymous and communal is now signed. Who owns a tradition: the community, the families who practise it, or the individual artist?

    • Copying: designs are copied by machine printing and by non-community painters and sold as “Warli” or “Gond” art, often more cheaply than the work of the artists themselves.

    • Middlemen and prices: the gap between what artists are paid and the price of their work in city galleries remains large.

    • Context: a painting made for a wedding wall and one made for a gallery are different objects, and both are genuine work of the tradition; but the ritual knowledge behind the first may not survive if only the second is valued.

    Institutions have played an important part in this history. Bharat Bhavan in Bhopal, opened in 1982, displayed Adivasi and folk art alongside modern Indian art in its museum, Roopankar, treating Jangarh Singh Shyam, Bhuri Bai and others as contemporary artists rather than as anonymous craftspeople. The Indira Gandhi Rashtriya Manav Sangrahalaya, also in Bhopal, invited Adivasi builders and artists from across India to construct houses, shrines and installations in its open-air exhibitions. Non-governmental organisations, publishers such as Tara Books in Chennai, and galleries in India and abroad have helped artists to reach new audiences, sometimes with fair terms and sometimes not.

    Geographical Indication registration, which protects the name of a product associated with a place, has been obtained for several folk arts, including Madhubani painting. It can help against misleading labelling, but it does not by itself secure fair prices or protect individual artists (Chapter 12).

    The most useful way to understand these arts is through the work and words of the artists themselves: in exhibitions, in books such as The London Jungle Book, and in the growing number of interviews and memoirs in which painters describe how they learned, what their images mean to them, and how their art has changed.

    Key Points

    • Warli, Mithila, Pardhan Gond, Saura and Pithora painting began as ritual art and became contemporary art with named artists.

    • Caste and gender shape who makes art and how it is valued, as the Dalit painters of Mithila show.

    • Craft traditions such as Dhokra casting, pottery, weaving and tattoo, and the drums and pipes of Adivasi music, carry technical and social knowledge.

    • Markets have brought income and recognition but also copying, unfair prices and loss of ritual context.
