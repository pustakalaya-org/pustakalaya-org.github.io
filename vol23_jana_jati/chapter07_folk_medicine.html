    Chapter 7: Folk Medicine and Healing Traditions¶

    7.1 Introduction: Healers and Their Knowledge¶

    In every village and forest hamlet of the subcontinent there have been people who knew how to deal with illness and injury: women who treated fevers and digestive complaints at home, bone-setters, midwives, herbalists, and diviners and priests who identified the hidden causes of misfortune. For many Adivasi communities, especially those far from hospitals, such healers have been, and often still are, the first and sometimes the only source of care.

    This chapter describes these traditions. It treats them as knowledge systems with their own logic, and it records the respect in which healers are held. It also applies the same standard to them that should be applied to any medicine. A remedy is not shown to work because it is old, because it is used by many people, or because laboratory tests on a plant extract have found some biological activity. Only careful clinical study can establish whether a treatment is safe and effective, and very few folk remedies have been studied in that way. Nothing in this chapter is a recommendation to use any remedy, and no doses are given.

    Health and Safety Note

    This chapter describes traditional practices for historical and cultural understanding only. It does not recommend any treatment. Several plants mentioned here (for example Datura, Calotropis, Euphorbia latex, Acorus calamus and some wild tubers) are toxic. Snakebite, fractures, difficult childbirth, high fever, jaundice and serious infections need prompt care at a health facility; delay in seeking such care is a major cause of avoidable death. Commercially sold herbal and mineral products have repeatedly been found to contain lead, mercury or arsenic (Saper et al. 2004, 2008).

    7.2 Folk Medicine and Āyurveda¶

    All traditional healing in India is often loosely called “Āyurveda”. This is misleading. Folk medicine, as the term is used here, is the healing knowledge of rural and Adivasi communities, learned by apprenticeship and practice rather than from texts or colleges. Classical Āyurveda, as set out in the Carakasaṃhitā, the Suśrutasaṃhitā and Vāgbhaṭa’s Aṣṭāṅgahṛdaya, is a learned, textual system with a theory of three doṣas (vāta, pitta, kapha), seven bodily tissues (dhātu) and an elaborate pharmacology (Wujastyk 2003); it is treated in Volume 13, Āyurveda. Adivasi healers generally do not think in these terms. A Santal ojhā, a Gond guniyā or a Sora shaman explains illness in other ways, and uses plants of the local forest that may not appear in any Sanskrit text.

    The two traditions have interacted for a very long time. As noted in Chapter 1, the Carakasaṃhitā acknowledges that herdsmen and forest dwellers know medicinal plants by sight and name (Sūtrasthāna 1.120–121), and many plants of the classical pharmacopoeia were surely learned from such people. In the other direction, Āyurvedic ideas such as “hot” and “cold” foods have spread widely in folk practice. Today many rural healers combine local plants, Āyurvedic preparations bought in the market, and biomedical drugs.

    7.2.1 Medical Pluralism¶

    Anthropologists use the term medical pluralism for the coexistence of several medical systems among which people choose. The collection Asian Medical Systems, edited by Charles Leslie (1976), established the comparative study of such pluralism in South Asia and elsewhere (Leslie 1976). In an Adivasi village today a family may, for the same illness, consult a household elder, a herbalist, a diviner, a government health worker, an unqualified “doctor” who gives injections, a pharmacy and a hospital, in various orders. People are pragmatic: they judge treatments by results, cost and accessibility, and often attribute different aspects of an illness (its symptoms, its cause, its meaning) to different domains. Understanding this pattern of choice is more useful than asking whether people “believe in” traditional or modern medicine.

    7.3 Ideas of Illness¶

    In many Adivasi traditions illness can have several kinds of cause, and an important part of healing is finding out which one is at work:

    • ordinary or natural causes, such as cold, heat, bad food, injury or worms, treated with plants, diet and massage;

    • the anger of deities or spirits (boṅgā among the Santal and Munda, pen among the Gond), often because of a neglected offering or broken taboo;

    • the dead, whose wishes must be heard;

    • human malice, including sorcery and the evil eye.

    Piers Vitebsky’s study of the Sora of Odisha describes how shamans, many of them women, enter trance and allow the dead to speak through them; in these “dialogues with the dead” the living and the dead negotiate over illness, grief and obligation (Vitebsky 1993). Elwin had described the Sora shamans and their wall paintings (iḍital) a generation earlier (Elwin 1955).

    Such explanations attend to the social and emotional side of illness: a patient’s quarrels, losses and fears are part of the diagnosis, which biomedical consultations often leave out. But the same frameworks can do great harm. Belief that illness or death is caused by a witch (ḍāin, ṭonhī) has led, across Jharkhand, Odisha, Chhattisgarh, Assam and elsewhere, to the accusation, expulsion, torture and murder of women, often widows or women with land. Several states have passed laws against witch-hunting, beginning with Bihar (1999) and Jharkhand (2001) (Chapter 11). An honest account of folk healing must include this.

    7.4 Healers¶

    Healers are known by many names. In eastern India the ojhā is a diviner and healer; Santals also consult the jān guru, a diviner who identifies the cause of misfortune. In Chhattisgarh and Madhya Pradesh the guniyā and the baigā (a village priest-healer, a role often filled by members of the Baiga community for their Gond neighbours) treat illness and appease local deities. Among Bhils and Oraons the bhagat has similar functions, and in Rajasthan the bhopā is a possessed medium of a deity. Some healers specialise in particular conditions such as snakebite, fractures or children’s illnesses; many are farmers or labourers who heal part-time. The main kinds are summarised in Table 7.1.

    Table 7.1. Some kinds of healers and ritual specialists (terms vary between communities)
    Term Region / communities Role
    Ojhā Eastern India; Santal, Munda, Ho and neighbours Diviner and healer; identifies spirits or sorcery behind illness
    Jān guru Santal Diviner consulted to find the cause of illness or death
    Guniyā Central India Healer using plants, charms and offerings
    Baigā Central India (often Baiga for Gond neighbours) Village priest of the land and healer
    Bhagat Oraon, Bhil and others Healer and ritual specialist; also a reformed devotee
    Bhopā Rajasthan, Gujarat Medium of a deity; also epic singer

    Dāī Throughout India Birth attendant
    Bone-setter Throughout India Treats fractures and dislocations

    Knowledge is usually learned over years from a parent, relative or teacher. The apprentice accompanies the healer to collect plants, learns their appearance in different seasons, learns preparations, and is gradually allowed to treat patients. Spells and songs (mantra in the broad sense) are often taught with the plants. Some knowledge is secret, passed on only to a chosen successor; this protects the healer’s livelihood and authority, but it also means that knowledge can die with its holder.

    Healers’ knowledge of plants can be extensive: documentation projects have recorded experienced healers who use well over a hundred species. Ethnobotanical surveys, starting with the work of S. K. Jain and of P. O. Bodding among the Santal, have collected thousands of plant–use records (Bodding 1925; Jain 1991). These records are an important cultural archive. They are not, by themselves, evidence that the remedies work.

    7.5 Regional Traditions¶

    7.5.1 Santal¶

    The Norwegian missionary and scholar P. O. Bodding, who worked among the Santal for nearly half a century, published a detailed study of Santal medicine and the beliefs connected with it, based on manuscripts written for him by Santal informants (Bodding 1925). Santal healing combined plant remedies for everyday complaints with divination and offerings to the boṅgā for illness thought to have a spiritual cause. W. G. Archer’s later collection of Santal poetry shows how illness, healing and the spirit world run through Santal song (Archer 1974).

    7.5.2 Gond and Baiga¶

    Among the Gond of central India and their Baiga neighbours, healing is closely tied to the village priesthood. Elwin’s The Baiga records Baiga charms, remedies and the reputation of Baiga priests as healers among other communities (Elwin 1939). Plants commonly reported in central Indian folk medicine include harṛā (Terminalia chebula), bahedā (T. bellirica), āṃvlā (Phyllanthus emblica), kālmegh (Andrographis paniculata) and bāibiḍaṅg (Embelia ribes).

    7.5.3 Kani of the Western Ghats¶

    In the 1980s scientists of the Tropical Botanic Garden and Research Institute (TBGRI) in Thiruvananthapuram learned from Kani guides of the Agasthyamalai hills in Kerala about a plant, ārogyappacca (Trichopus zeylanicus), which Kani men ate to keep up their energy on long walks. TBGRI developed a herbal preparation from it, licensed its manufacture to a pharmaceutical company in the mid-1990s, and agreed to share half of the licence fee and royalties with the Kani through a trust set up for the purpose. The case is often cited as an early example of benefit-sharing with a source community. It was also contested: Kani communities were divided over the arrangement, and the supply of the plant from the forest became a problem.

    Another Kani healer, Lakshmikutty of Kallar in Thiruvananthapuram district, known for her knowledge of several hundred herbal preparations and for teaching it to others, received the Padma Shri in 2018; her recognition by the state is one sign of the changing status of folk healers.

    7.5.4 North-East India¶

    The communities of North-East India, in one of the world’s richest botanical regions, have extensive plant medicine traditions. Khasi, Garo, Naga, Mizo and other healers use local plants for fevers, wounds, digestive and skin complaints. Much of the documentation is recent and scattered in regional journals, and many traditions have been reshaped by Christianity, which in some communities discouraged healing associated with older religious practice.

    7.5.5 Sowa-Rigpa in the Himalaya¶

    In Ladakh, Lahaul and Spiti, Sikkim, Arunachal Pradesh and other Himalayan regions, many of whose peoples are Scheduled Tribes, the main traditional medical system is Sowa-Rigpa (“science of healing”), the Tibetan medical tradition. Unlike the oral traditions described above, it is a learned textual system, based on the Rgyud bzhi (“Four Tantras”), which draws on Indian Āyurvedic, Chinese and other sources. Its practitioners, the amchi, were traditionally trained in families or monasteries and served their villages, often without fixed payment. The Government of India recognised Sowa-Rigpa as an official system of medicine in 2010. Amchi medicine shows that “tribal” and “textual” are not opposites: Himalayan tribal communities have their own literate medical tradition.

    7.6.1 Lessons from Drug Discovery¶

    The history of drug discovery shows both the promise and the limits of traditional medicine. Quinine, the first effective treatment for malaria, came from the bark of the South American cinchona tree, whose use was learned from Andean peoples. Artemisinin, now the basis of the most effective malaria treatments, was isolated in China in the 1970s by a team including Tu Youyou, who had searched classical Chinese medical texts for leads; she received the Nobel Prize in 2015. From India, reserpine, isolated in 1952 from Rauvolfia serpentina (sarpagandhā), a plant long used in Indian medicine for insanity and snakebite, became one of the first effective drugs for high blood pressure. In each case, however, the effective drug was a purified compound tested in clinical trials, not the traditional preparation; and the Andean and Indian communities whose knowledge pointed the way to quinine and reserpine received no share of the benefits. Most leads from traditional medicine do not produce drugs at all.

    Laboratory research on plants used in folk medicine has found many with biological activity: extracts that kill microbes in a test tube, reduce inflammation in animals, or affect cells in culture. Such findings are a starting point for research, not a confirmation of traditional claims. Many promising extracts fail when tested in people, because the active compound does not reach the right place in the body in sufficient amounts, because of toxicity, or because the original observation was mistaken. At the same time, some of the most important drugs of modern medicine began with plants used in traditional medicine elsewhere in the world, and the systematic, ethical study of folk remedies, with consent and benefit-sharing, is worthwhile.

    7.7 Bone-Setting¶

    Traditional bone-setters are found throughout rural India and are widely trusted. They reduce dislocations and fractures by traction and manipulation, immobilise limbs with bamboo or wooden splints and cloth, and apply poultices of plants such as hāḍjoṛ, turmeric or castor leaves. The skill is often hereditary. For simple injuries, and where no hospital is within reach, a skilled bone-setter may help; many patients prefer them because they are near, cheap and familiar.

    Bone-setting

    Orthopaedic surgeons in India regularly treat serious complications of traditional bone-setting: fractures that have healed in the wrong position, stiff joints, infections, and gangrene caused by splints tied too tightly, which can lead to amputation. Open fractures, injuries to the spine, head, hip or thigh, and any injury with numbness, coldness or discolouration of the limb need hospital care.

    7.8 Snakebite¶

    Snakebite is a major cause of death in rural India. A nationally representative study, the Million Death Study, estimated that about 46,000 people died of snakebite in India in 2005, most of them farmers and their families in rural areas (Mohapatra et al. 2011). Folk responses to snakebite include incantations, cutting and sucking the wound, tight tourniquets, and plants applied or given by mouth; there are specialist snakebite healers in many villages, and shrines of snake deities where victims are taken.

    Snakebite

    The only proven treatment for venomous snakebite is anti-venom given in a health facility, with supportive care. Cutting the wound, sucking, tight tourniquets and herbal remedies do not help and can cause harm. Time spent with a traditional healer before reaching hospital is one of the main reasons people die. Keep the person calm and still, immobilise the bitten limb, and go to a hospital immediately (Mohapatra et al. 2011).

    The Irula snake-catchers of Tamil Nadu (Chapter 2) are an instructive contrast: their knowledge of snakes is put to use in the production of anti-venom, not in competition with it.

    7.9 Women’s Knowledge and Midwifery¶

    Most everyday healing happens at home, and it is mostly done by women: treating children’s fevers, coughs and diarrhoea, preparing special foods after childbirth, and knowing which plants to gather for common complaints. Early documentation often ignored this knowledge because it focused on specialist (usually male) healers. Women’s knowledge is now more widely recognised, but it is also the most easily lost when families move to towns and buy medicines in the market.

    Traditional birth attendants (dāī) attended most births in rural India until recently. Many had great experience and were trusted by the women they served. They were also, in many regions, women of low caste whose work was considered polluting and poorly paid (Jeffery et al. 1989). From the 1970s the government trained large numbers of dāīs in hygienic delivery and the recognition of danger signs, and supplied them with simple delivery kits; evaluations of these programmes found limited effects on maternal deaths, which depend above all on timely access to emergency obstetric care. From the 2000s public health policy shifted strongly towards births in health facilities, supported by cash incentives under the Janani Suraksha Yojana (launched 2005), and the role of dāīs declined. Public health researchers continue to debate how their skills and trust might be used, for example in accompanying women to facilities.

    Pregnancy and childbirth

    Many plants are traditionally given to hasten labour, to expel the placenta or to restore the mother after birth. Some of these act on the uterus and can be dangerous. Pregnant women should not take herbal preparations without medical advice, and danger signs in pregnancy and labour (bleeding, convulsions, prolonged labour, fever) need immediate care at a health facility.

    7.10 Animal Healing¶

    Pastoral and farming communities have extensive veterinary knowledge. The Raika (Rebari) camel and sheep herders of Rajasthan, the Toda and their buffaloes in the Nilgiris, the Gaddi shepherds of Himachal Pradesh and the Gujjar buffalo herders of the Himalayan foothills have treatments for wounds, worms, bloat, lameness and skin diseases, as well as ways of judging the health of animals from their behaviour, dung and appetite. Non-governmental organisations working with pastoralists have documented this knowledge and argued that it complements, and is sometimes more accessible than, government veterinary services. Some of the plants reported in such practice are listed in Table 7.3.

    7.11 Disease Deities¶

    In much of rural India, epidemic diseases have been understood as the presence of a deity. The goddess Śītalā was associated with smallpox in northern and eastern India, and Māriyammaṉ with smallpox and other fevers in the south; offerings, songs and restrictions on the household were the response to an outbreak. Adivasi communities have their own deities of disease and protection, often worshipped at the village boundary. The eradication of smallpox, declared in 1977 for India and in 1980 worldwide, did not end the worship of these goddesses, whose role has widened to protection against illness and misfortune in general. Such beliefs coexist, for most people, with the use of vaccines and medicines; they show that religious and medical responses to illness are not simply alternatives.

    7.12 Ritual Healing¶

    Healing in Adivasi communities often combines plants with ritual: divination, offerings, songs, drumming, trance and the participation of family and neighbours. Anthropologists have long noted the psychological and social effects of such rituals. They give illness a meaning, mobilise the support of kin and community, relieve fear, and offer a course of action. Patients may well feel better as a result, especially with conditions in which stress and anxiety play a large part.

    These effects are real, but they should not be overstated. Ritual cannot cure tuberculosis, malaria or diabetes, and the authority of a healer can delay effective treatment. The best outcomes come where people can use both kinds of care, and where health workers understand and respect the explanations their patients bring.

    7.12.1 Possession and Mental Distress¶

    Possession by deities, spirits or the dead is a common idiom for experiences that biomedicine would classify as mental illness, as well as for religious experience that it would not. In many Adivasi and rural communities, a person who behaves strangely, falls into fits or seems overwhelmed by grief may be taken to a diviner or to a shrine known for healing, where the spirit is identified and asked what it wants. Anthropologists and psychiatrists working in India have noted that such healing can help where distress is rooted in family conflict or social pressure, and that it offers a language in which suffering can be expressed without stigma. It cannot treat serious psychiatric illness such as psychosis, and people with such illness have suffered harm when kept at shrines for long periods. Programmes that bring mental health services into rural areas have had most success when they work alongside, rather than against, local healers and the explanations people bring.

    7.12.2 Healers and the Law¶

    Indian law gives folk healers no formal recognition, but it does regulate some of what they do. The Drugs and Magic Remedies (Objectionable Advertisements) Act, 1954, prohibits advertising remedies as cures for a list of diseases, and the laws against witch-hunting (Section 7.3) punish those who accuse others of witchcraft. Maharashtra’s law against black magic and harmful superstitious practices, passed in 2013 after the murder of the rationalist campaigner Narendra Dabholkar, penalises the exploitation of sick people by those who claim supernatural powers, while leaving ordinary religious practice untouched. Such laws aim to protect patients, especially women and the mentally ill, who have sometimes been chained, beaten or starved in the name of exorcism. They also raise the question of how to distinguish harmful practices from the legitimate religious and healing traditions of communities.

    7.13 Adivasi Health Today¶

    Discussion of traditional healing must be set against the health situation of Adivasi communities. The High Level Committee on tribal communities (2014) reported that Scheduled Tribes had higher infant and child mortality, higher rates of undernutrition and anaemia, and a heavier burden of malaria and tuberculosis than other social groups, and that health services in tribal areas were thinly staffed and often far away (Government of India 2014). Forested tribal districts of Odisha, Chhattisgarh, Jharkhand and the North-East account for a large share of India’s malaria cases. Sickle cell disease, an inherited blood disorder, is common in many communities of central, western and southern India; in 2023 the central government launched a national mission to screen for and manage it.

    Some of the most effective responses have combined modern medicine with respect for local culture. In Gadchiroli, the organisation SEARCH, founded in 1986 by the physicians Abhay and Rani Bang, trained village women to care for newborn babies at home, including recognising and treating infections; a field trial published in 1999 showed a large fall in newborn deaths, and the approach was later adopted in national programmes (Bang et al. 1999). Its hospital for the Gond and other Adivasi patients of the district was built to resemble a village, with space for families to stay and for patients’ own beliefs to be respected. Other voluntary organisations in Chhattisgarh, Jharkhand, Odisha and the Nilgiris have built similar community health programmes, often employing Adivasi health workers who speak patients’ languages.

    7.14 The Trade in Medicinal Plants¶

    Most of the medicinal plants used by India’s herbal medicine industry are still collected from the wild, very largely by Adivasi and other forest-dwelling collectors, who are paid little and have no share in the value added later. Some species have been overharvested to the point of scarcity. The National Medicinal Plants Board, set up in 2000, promotes cultivation and conservation, and the Forest Rights Act and PESA give communities ownership of minor forest produce, including medicinal plants. Making that ownership effective, with fair prices and sustainable harvesting rules, would do more to sustain folk medical knowledge than documentation alone.

    7.15 Threats and Protection¶

    Folk medical knowledge is threatened by loss of forests and access to them; by the disappearance of apprenticeship as young people move away; by the low status of healers; and by the attraction of quick biomedical remedies. It is also open to appropriation: knowledge recorded by researchers may be used commercially without the knowledge holders’ consent.

    India’s Traditional Knowledge Digital Library (TKDL), established in 2001 by the Council of Scientific and Industrial Research and the government department now called the Ministry of AYUSH, translates formulations from classical texts of Āyurveda, Unani, Siddha, Sowa-Rigpa and Yoga into formats that patent examiners can search, in order to prevent patents on known knowledge. It has had notable successes. But it covers only codified, textual knowledge; oral folk and tribal knowledge is largely outside it. The legal and ethical questions this raises are discussed in Chapters 12 and 13.

    7.15.1 Quality and Contamination¶

    As folk and classical remedies are increasingly sold as packaged products, questions of quality arise that did not exist when a healer gathered fresh plants for a single patient. Two studies published in the Journal of the American Medical Association found that about one in five of the Āyurvedic products tested, bought in shops in the Boston area and over the internet, contained detectable lead, mercury or arsenic, in some cases at levels that could cause poisoning (Saper et al. 2004, 2008). Some contamination comes from the deliberate use of metals and minerals in certain preparations, some from poor manufacturing. Herbal products may also be adulterated with undeclared pharmaceutical drugs, or made from wrongly identified plants. These are problems of commercialised medicine rather than of village healing, but they show that “natural” is not the same as “safe”.

    7.15.2 What Can Be Said with Confidence¶

    Summing up the evidence reviewed in this chapter, a few statements can be made with confidence. First, folk healers hold a large body of knowledge about plants, the body and the social side of illness, and this knowledge is a significant cultural and scientific resource that is being lost. Second, very few folk remedies have been shown in clinical trials to be effective, and some are harmful; claims that particular remedies cure particular diseases should be treated with caution until they are tested. Third, healers often play an important part in the care of their communities, especially where health services are distant, and they can help or hinder access to effective treatment. Fourth, the communities who hold this knowledge have a right to decide how it is recorded and used, and to share in any benefits. Policies that take all four statements seriously are most likely to serve Adivasi health; the measures most often proposed are gathered in Section 14.13.

    Key Points

    • Folk healing combines plants, ritual and social explanation of illness; it is distinct from classical Āyurveda.

    • Very few folk remedies have been tested clinically; laboratory activity is not proof of effectiveness, and several plants are toxic.

    • Snakebite, fractures, childbirth and serious infections need prompt care at a health facility.

    • Witchcraft accusations are the darkest side of beliefs about illness, and several states have laws against witch-hunting.

    • Adivasi health indicators are poor; respect for traditional knowledge is no substitute for good public health services.
