    Chapter 10: Village and Folk Knowledge beyond the Adivasi World¶

    10.1 Folk Knowledge in a Caste Society¶

    Most of the people who farm, herd, fish and make things in rural India are not Adivasis. They live in villages organised by caste, and their knowledge, like that of Adivasi communities, is held mainly in practice and in speech. It is the “folk” half of this volume’s title (Section 1.2). Earlier chapters have already met parts of it: the water systems of caste villages (Chapter 4), the rain calendar and the proverbs of Ghāgh and Bhaḍḍarī (Chapter 6), the bone-setters, midwives and disease goddesses of village medicine (Chapter 7), the community epics of Rajasthan and the south (Chapter 8) and Mithila painting (Chapter 9). This chapter looks at this knowledge as a whole, through the people who hold it: cultivators, herders, fishers, artisans, Dalit specialists, priests of the village goddess, bards and the women who sing at the grindstone and in the fields.

    Two features set it apart from the Adivasi knowledge described elsewhere in this book. The first is caste. In a caste village, knowledge is distributed by birth: the potter’s son learns the wheel, the barber’s son the razor and the rites at which barbers serve, the drummer’s son the drum. Hereditary transmission kept skills alive across generations, but it also fenced them in. Some kinds of knowledge were reserved for some castes, and others, such as the handling of hides or the dead, were imposed on the lowest castes and despised with them. Any account of village knowledge that praises the skill and ignores the fence repeats the error that Chapter 4 found in romantic accounts of village tanks.

    The second is the closeness of village knowledge to the textual tradition. Village priests use the pañcāṅga; farmers name the rains after the nakṣatras; the epics of Rāma and the Pāṇḍavas are sung in village versions. Folklorists have shown that this closeness does not make village knowledge a copy of the texts. A. K. Ramanujan, Stuart Blackburn and their colleagues described the relation as a conversation in which oral and written traditions borrow from, answer and sometimes mock each other (Blackburn and Ramanujan 1986). The encyclopedia of South Asian folklore edited by Margaret Mills, Peter Claus and Sarah Diamond gives a sense of its range across the region (Mills et al. 2003).

    10.2 Cultivators¶

    Peasant farming is the largest body of folk knowledge in India, and its history is told in Volume 15, Kṛṣi and Jīva Vijñāna. Its main features can be summarised here. Farmers classify soils by colour, texture, depth and water-holding capacity, and match crops and varieties to each; they sow mixtures of cereals, pulses and oilseeds that spread risk and maintain fertility; they select and store their own seed; and they time their work by the rains, the stars and the flowering of trees. When the agricultural chemist J. A. Voelcker toured India for the government in 1889–91, he concluded that Indian cultivators were in many respects as skilled as British farmers, and that their main difficulties were shortages of water, manure, fuel and credit rather than ignorance (Voelcker 1893).

    Farmers’ knowledge has its own theory as well as its own practice. In a village of western Uttar Pradesh in the 1980s, the anthropologist Akhil Gupta found that farmers described soils, crops, fertilisers and weather in the language of “hot” and “cold” qualities and of balance, a language shared with ideas about food and the body. Chemical fertilisers, for example, were judged “hot” and thought to need balancing. Gupta argued that this was not a survival of ignorance but a coherent way of understanding the new technology of the Green Revolution, which farmers adopted and judged on their own terms (Gupta 1998).

    Much of this knowledge is stored in sayings. In the Hindi-speaking north they are attributed to Ghāgh and Bhaḍḍarī (Section 6.2); in Bengal to Khanā, a legendary woman astrologer; and in every region there are rhymes about which crop to sow after which rain, how to judge a bullock and what a red sky at evening means. Their reliability varies. Many encode real experience of local soils and seasons; others are matters of luck or belief. They should be read as records of what farmers observed and valued, not as forecasts.

    The Green Revolution of the 1960s and 1970s replaced many local varieties with high-yielding ones that needed irrigation, fertiliser and pesticide. It raised yields of wheat and rice greatly and ended the threat of famine in much of India; it also made farmers dependent on purchased inputs and made much local seed knowledge redundant. Movements for the revival of local seed and of millets, such as the Deccan Development Society’s work with Dalit women farmers in Telangana (Section 5.11), are attempts to recover what was lost without giving up what was gained.

    10.3 Herders¶

    Pastoralists of many castes, the Raika (Rebari) and Gujar of Rajasthan and Gujarat, the Dhangar of the Deccan, the Gaddi of Himachal Pradesh and the Kuruba of Karnataka among them, hold a knowledge of animals, grass and routes that settled farmers lack. Some are Scheduled Tribes; most are not. Their breeding of animals suited to heat, drought or mountain pasture, and their care of sick animals, are described in Sections 2.15 and 7.10.

    Arun Agrawal’s study of Raika shepherds who migrate each year with their flocks from western Rajasthan shows that their knowledge is as much social as ecological. Households travel together in camps that decide jointly on routes, on where to halt and on the division of work, and that negotiate with farmers for the right to graze harvested fields in exchange for manure. Agrawal argues that these decisions are made through a skilled combination of leadership and consultation, and that the herders’ success depends on reading markets and politics as closely as pasture (Agrawal 1999). As fields have been irrigated and commons enclosed, the routes have become harder to use, and many herding families have settled or sent their young people to other work.

    10.4 Fishers¶

    India’s coastal fishing communities, the Mukkuvar and Paravar of the far south, the Koli of the western coast, the Pattanavar of the Coromandel coast and many others, hold a knowledge of the sea that is among the most exact in rural India. It includes the location of fishing grounds and reefs, found far from land by lining up landmarks on the shore; the habits and seasons of fish; the currents, winds and swells of the monsoon seas; and the building and handling of boats suited to surf beaches without harbours. The kaṭṭumaram of the Coromandel coast, a raft of logs lashed together, gave English the word “catamaran”.

    Kalpana Ram’s study of Mukkuvar women on the coast of Kanyakumari shows the social side of this knowledge. Men go to sea; women trade fish, manage household money and credit, and hold the networks on which the family depends ashore, and a Catholic parish organisation shapes much of village life (Ram 1991). Ajantha Subramanian’s history of the same coast traces how the Mukkuvar have claimed rights over their stretch of sea, against mechanised trawlers from outside, against the state and sometimes against the church, and how they have used both custom and the language of citizenship to do so (Subramanian 2009). The conflict between small-scale fishers and trawlers, which began with the mechanisation of fishing from the 1950s and 1960s, led to strikes and to a fishworkers’ movement in Kerala and Tamil Nadu, and seasonal bans on trawling during the monsoon breeding season were introduced in Kerala from the late 1980s. Fishers’ knowledge of fish seasons was one of the arguments for those bans.

    Fishers also live with the most dangerous weather on the coast. Their own reading of wind and swell, and the local knowledge of safe places described in Section 6.6, are now combined with forecasts broadcast by radio and mobile phone, which have made going to sea less dangerous than it was a generation ago.

    10.5 Artisans and the Village Economy¶

    In the classic picture of the north Indian village drawn by the missionary William Wiser in the 1930s, potters, carpenters, blacksmiths, barbers, washermen, leatherworkers and others served the landowning families under hereditary arrangements, receiving fixed shares of grain at harvest and gifts at festivals in return for their work. Wiser called this the jajmānī system (Wiser 1936). Later scholars have questioned how far it was ever a single system, and how far it described an ideal more than a practice; Susan Wadley’s restudy of Wiser’s village over sixty years shows how money wages, migration and politics have dissolved most of these ties (Wadley 1994). But the picture captures something real: in a caste village, craft knowledge was hereditary, learned within the family from childhood, and tied to a place in a system of obligation.

    The crafts themselves, pottery, metalwork, carpentry, weaving, dyeing, leather and the rest, are described in Volume 17, Śilpa–Kāru. What matters for this chapter is the form of the knowledge. Much of it is tacit: the potter’s judgement of clay and fire, the smith’s reading of the colour of hot iron, the weaver’s sense of tension in the warp. It was learned by watching and doing, not from instruction, and it was protected by the fact that outsiders could not easily acquire it. When factory goods undercut village crafts in the nineteenth and twentieth centuries, this knowledge did not simply disappear; much of it moved into small workshops in towns, into repair trades and into the craft revival, but the hereditary link between knowledge and caste was loosened.

    10.6 Dalit Knowledge¶

    Some of the most important folk knowledge in India has been held by Dalit communities, and it has been doubly devalued: as unlettered knowledge, and as the knowledge of castes regarded as polluting. Leatherworkers knew how to flay, tan and work hides, and supplied the village with water bags, harness, sandals and the heads of drums (Volume 17). Dalit drummers played at weddings, funerals and festivals, and in Tamil Nadu the frame drum (paṟai), long a mark of the low status of those who played it, has become in recent decades a symbol of Dalit pride; Zoe Sherinian has studied how Dalit Christian musicians made it part of a theology of liberation (Sherinian 2014). In much of north India the midwives who attended births were women of low or Dalit castes, valued for their skill but regarded as polluted by the work, as Patricia Jeffery and her colleagues showed for western Uttar Pradesh (Jeffery et al. 1989). In Maharashtra the Mahars, as village servants, were watchmen, messengers and keepers of the village’s knowledge of its boundaries, called as witnesses when boundaries were disputed (Zelliot 1992).

    Dalit thinkers have argued that this knowledge deserves recognition as knowledge. Kancha Ilaiah’s Why I Am Not a Hindu (1996) contrasted the productive skills of the labouring castes, in farming, herding, leatherwork, pottery and washing, with a Brahmanical tradition that, in his account, despised labour and claimed knowledge for itself (Ilaiah 1996). His argument has been criticised for idealising the labouring castes and for its sweeping picture of Brahmanical thought, but it put a real question on the agenda. Gopal Guru and Sundar Sarukkai have pursued that question in philosophical terms, asking why Dalits have so often been treated as sources of experience to be studied rather than as theorists in their own right (Guru and Sarukkai 2012). Their question applies to this whole volume, which describes the knowledge of others largely from published scholarship.

    B. R. Ambedkar’s campaigns, among them the Mahad satyāgraha of 1927 for the right of Dalits to draw water from a public tank (Section 4.11), and the anti-caste movements that followed, also changed the relation between Dalits and inherited occupations. Many Dalits have deliberately abandoned hereditary work, such as the removal of dead animals, as a step towards dignity, even where it meant giving up a skill. That choice is part of the history of folk knowledge too: not every knowledge that disappears is a loss to those who once carried it.

    10.7 The Village Goddess and Her Specialists¶

    The religion of the village is a body of knowledge in its own right. In much of south India, as the missionary bishop Henry Whitehead described early in the twentieth century, each village had its own goddesses (grāmadevatā), worshipped not by Brahmin priests but by priests from other castes, with animal sacrifice at the great festivals and with a part for Dalit drummers and servants in the rites (Whitehead 1916). The goddess protects the village from epidemic and misfortune; her festival marks its boundaries and renews its unity, while also displaying its hierarchy. The goddesses of smallpox and fever, Śītalā in the north and Māriyammaṉ in the south, are discussed in Section 7.11.

    Village religion also has its specialists of possession and healing. In Tamil Nadu the cult of Draupadī as a village goddess, studied by Alf Hiltebeitel, combines a local retelling of the Mahābhārata, performed over many nights in street theatre, with a firewalk in which devotees cross a bed of embers; its main patrons are the Vanniyar, a large cultivating caste (Hiltebeitel 1988). Shrines of hero-gods such as Tejājī in Rajasthan (Section 8.6) and of the snake goddess Manasā in Bengal are places where snakebite victims are taken and where possessed mediums speak for the deity. Possession, as Chapter 7 explains, can offer a language for distress, and it can also delay treatment; both are part of the picture.

    10.8 Bards, Genealogists and Epics¶

    Caste society had its own keepers of memory. In Gujarat and Rajasthan, hereditary genealogists, the Bāroṭs, Bhāṭs and Cāraṇs, kept written registers of the births, marriages and deaths of their patron families and visited them periodically to bring the record up to date and to recite the family’s origin and history. A. M. Shah and R. G. Shroff showed in the 1950s that these genealogists were also mythographers, who supplied their patrons with legends of descent that supported claims to status (Shah and Shroff 1958). Their records were used to settle disputes over marriage and inheritance, and they show how oral and written memory worked together in village India.

    The community epics introduced in Chapter 8 belong mostly to this caste world. In Andhra Pradesh the epic of the heroes of Palnāḍu, about a war between related ruling houses, is sung by bards for the castes that claim descent from its heroes; Gene Roghair published a study and translation, and Velcheru Narayana Rao showed that each Telugu folk epic belongs to particular castes, whose values and place in the social order it expresses (Roghair 1982; Blackburn and Ramanujan 1986). In the Kongu region of Tamil Nadu, Brenda Beck recorded the story of the twin brothers and their sister known as the Annanmār Katai, the epic of a cultivating caste (Beck 1982). In western Uttar Pradesh and Rajasthan, Susan Wadley studied Dhola, the epic of Raja Nal, sung mostly by singers of low caste for mixed village audiences (Wadley 2004). These epics carry knowledge of local history, of land and of kinship, and they are often the form in which a caste remembers who it is.

    10.9 Women’s Songs, Proverbs and Riddles¶

    Women’s song is the largest and least recorded part of village oral literature. Women sing while grinding grain at the hand mill, while transplanting rice, at weddings, at births and at deaths. In Maharashtra the grinding songs (ovī) sung at the hand mill form an enormous body of short verses on work, family, devotion and the hardships of married life, and collectors have recorded them in very large numbers. Gloria Goodwin Raheja and Ann Grodzins Gold showed for north India that such songs do not simply repeat the ideals of kinship that men and texts describe; they comment on them, complain of them and sometimes mock them (Raheja and Gold 1994). The songs are discussed further in Section 8.9.

    Proverbs and riddles, whose working is described in Section 8.2, are as abundant in caste villages as among Adivasis. Many concern farming and weather; many others concern caste and kinship, and some of these carry the prejudices of the village, about women, about lower castes and about outsiders. Collections that print them without comment preserve the prejudice along with the wit, and a careful reader needs to notice both.

    10.10 Change and Survival¶

    Village folk knowledge has been changing for as long as it has been recorded. The forces at work are the same as for Adivasi knowledge: migration to towns, schooling in standard languages, the market in seeds, fertilisers and factory goods, the decline of hereditary occupations and new media. But the loss is not uniform. Some knowledge has moved into new settings, as when village songs are sold on cassettes and online, or a potter’s family makes goods for urban markets. Some has been abandoned by choice, as when Dalit families leave degrading work. And some has been revived and reinterpreted, as in the movements for local seed and millets or the Dalit use of the paṟai.

    What village and Adivasi knowledge have in common is that both were long treated as raw material for others, whether for the state, the scholar or the market, and both are now being claimed by their holders. What separates them is that the first grew up inside the caste order and the second, for the most part, outside it or against it. Holding both in one volume makes it easier to see that “folk” is not a single thing, and that the question of who holds a knowledge, and on what terms, matters as much as what the knowledge is.

    Key Points

    • Most rural knowledge in India is held by cultivators, herders, fishers and artisans of caste villages; caste distributes that knowledge by birth and fences it in.

    • Farmers’ knowledge has its own theory as well as practice, as Gupta’s account of “hot” and “cold” agronomy shows; herders’ and fishers’ knowledge is social and political as well as ecological.

    • Dalit communities have held important skills, from leatherwork and drumming to midwifery, that were devalued with the castes that held them; Dalit thinkers have argued for their recognition as knowledge.

    • Village goddesses, bards, genealogists and community epics keep the memory and the religious knowledge of caste communities.

    • Not every disappearing knowledge is a loss to its holders: some has been abandoned deliberately as a step towards dignity.
