    Chapter 5: Food Knowledge, Nutrition and Fermentation¶

    5.1 Introduction: Food as Knowledge¶

    What people eat rests on knowledge accumulated over generations: which plants and animals are edible and when, which are poisonous and how they can be made safe, how food can be stored through the lean months, and how it should be shared. Adivasi food systems draw on cultivated crops, wild plants, fish, game, insects and domestic animals, and on processing techniques such as drying, smoking and fermentation. This chapter describes that knowledge and the pressures on it.

    It also corrects a common romantic picture. Traditional diets are often more varied than modern ones and can be nutritionally valuable, but Adivasi communities in India today suffer high rates of undernutrition. National Family Health Surveys have repeatedly found higher levels of stunting and underweight among children of Scheduled Tribes than among other groups. The causes are loss of land and forest access, poverty, poor services and the erosion of food diversity, not the traditional foods themselves. Respecting food knowledge and addressing hunger are part of the same task (Government of India 2014).

    The point was made forcefully in the 1990s, when deaths of young children from malnutrition among the Korku of Melghat in Maharashtra drew national attention, at a time when their access to the surrounding forests had been restricted by forest and wildlife regulations and their rain-fed agriculture was precarious. Malnutrition in such cases is a result of the loss of the conditions in which food knowledge could be used. That knowledge covers the species themselves and their seasons; the processing that makes them safe and keeps them through the lean months; where and how much may be gathered; and the social rules of sharing, feasting and avoidance. The sections below take these in turn.

    5.2 Diversity on the Plate¶

    Studies of Adivasi food systems in central India, the Western Ghats and the North-East commonly record a hundred or more food species used by a single community over a year: cultivated cereals, millets and pulses; leafy greens gathered from fields and forest; tubers; fruits and flowers; mushrooms; fish, crabs and snails; small game; and insects. The FAO’s study of indigenous food systems, which included a case from India, documented this diversity and its contribution to diets (Kuhnlein et al. 2009).

    Wild foods are not just occasional supplements. In the months before the harvest, when stored grain runs low, greens, tubers, mushrooms and fruits gathered from the forest and from field margins can be a major part of the diet. Their importance is easily overlooked by surveys that count only cultivated crops.

    5.2.1 Greens, Mushrooms and Honey¶

    Leafy greens (sāg) are among the most important gathered foods. In Jharkhand and Odisha women gather dozens of kinds of greens from fields, field bunds and forest through the year, including the leaves of crops, weeds and trees, and dry some for the lean season. Mushrooms appear with the first rains; those that grow from termite mounds (Termitomyces species) and the puffball-like rugṛā that grows under sal trees are prized and sold in local and urban markets at high prices. Wild honey, gathered by specialist communities (Chapter 2), is food, medicine and a valued item of trade.

    5.2.2 Mahua¶

    The mahuā tree (Madhuca longifolia) is central to the economy and culture of central India. Its fleshy flowers, which fall in great quantities in March and April, are collected by women and children in the early morning, dried and stored. They are eaten cooked or roasted, sold, and distilled into a spirit that is important in hospitality, ritual and festivity. The seeds yield an edible fat used for cooking and lighting. Mahua is so important that the right to collect from particular trees is often a recognised family right, and the trees are protected when land is cleared (Elwin 1939).

    5.2.3 Rice and Millets¶

    Rice is the staple of eastern India and the North-East, where many communities maintain numerous local varieties chosen for particular fields, seasons and uses (Chapter 2). In central and southern India and the dry west, millets have been staples for Adivasi and other poor farmers for millennia.

    5.2.4 First Fruits¶

    Many communities mark the first eating of a new crop with a ritual: no one may eat the new rice, millet or fruit until it has been offered to the deities and ancestors. In western Odisha, Nuākhāī (“eating the new”) is celebrated by Adivasi and caste communities alike when the new rice is ready; families gather, the first grain is offered to the deity, and then shared. Santal, Munda and Oraon festivals similarly mark the first use of new flowers, fruits and grain. Such rituals express gratitude and kinship, and also regulate harvesting: fruit that may not be eaten before the ritual is not picked unripe.

    5.3 Millets¶

    Millets are hardy grains that grow on poor soils with little water. They were long neglected by agricultural policy, which favoured rice and wheat, and were associated with poverty. In recent years they have been revalued for their nutritional qualities and their resilience to drought. At India’s proposal the United Nations declared 2023 the International Year of Millets. The main millets are listed in Table 5.1.

    Adivasi farmers often grow millets mixed with pulses and oilseeds in the same field, which spreads risk and maintains soil fertility. Cooking traditions include porridges, flatbreads, gruels fermented overnight, and millet beers. The new popularity of millets in cities has raised prices, which can benefit growers, but there is a risk that the knowledge and varieties of Adivasi farmers will be used without benefit to them.

    5.4 Famine Foods and Detoxification¶

    Communities that live with uncertain harvests know which wild foods can be eaten when crops fail. Many of these require careful preparation because they contain bitter or toxic compounds. Examples include:

    • wild yams (Dioscorea species), some of which, such as D. hispida, are poisonous unless sliced, soaked in running water or ash solution and boiled repeatedly;

    • bamboo shoots, which contain cyanogenic compounds removed by boiling, soaking or fermentation;

    • the seeds of certain trees eaten in famine, prepared by roasting and soaking;

    • mushrooms, where knowledge of which species are edible is a matter of life and death.

    Wild foods

    Some wild tubers, seeds and mushrooms are poisonous unless correctly identified and prepared, and some cannot be made safe at all. Deaths from eating poisonous mushrooms and improperly prepared tubers are reported in India every year. Traditional knowledge of identification and preparation is precise and local; it should not be imitated from written descriptions.

    5.5 Fermentation¶

    Fermentation preserves food, changes its flavour and texture, can improve its digestibility and nutritional value, and produces drinks with important social and ritual roles. North-East India and the Himalaya have particularly rich fermentation traditions, which the microbiologist Jyoti Prakash Tamang and others have studied in detail (Tamang 2010). Table 5.2 lists some of these foods and drinks.

    5.5.1 Rice Beer¶

    Rice beer is made across eastern and north-eastern India: hãṛiyā (also called handiā) among the Santal, Munda, Oraon and Ho; zutho among the Angami Naga; apong among the Mising and Adi; judima among the Dimasa; and many others. Cooked rice is mixed with a dried starter cake (rānu, bākhar and other local names) made from rice flour and a mixture of plants, which carries the moulds and yeasts that convert starch to sugar and sugar to alcohol. Microbiological studies have identified the fungi, yeasts and bacteria in these starters (Tamang 2010). The knowledge of which plants to use and how to make and keep the starter is usually held by women.

    Rice beer is food, offering and hospitality. It is offered to deities and ancestors, shared at festivals, given to guests and to people who help with work. Its regulation by excise laws has long been contentious: the state has sometimes criminalised home brewing that communities regard as part of their culture, while selling commercial liquor through licensed shops.

    In the North-East, Christian churches have often discouraged or forbidden rice beer and other alcohol. Nagaland has prohibited the sale of liquor since 1989, and Mizoram has had prohibition for most of the period since the late 1990s, though both prohibitions have been widely evaded. Rice beer thus occupies a contested place, valued as heritage by some and rejected as un-Christian by others.

    5.5.2 Mahua Spirit¶

    Mahua flowers are fermented and distilled to make a spirit (mahuā dārū) which, like rice beer, is central to ritual and hospitality in central India. Colonial excise laws restricted its production in favour of licensed distilleries, a cause of resentment and of several Adivasi protests.

    Alcohol is also a cause of harm in many Adivasi communities, as elsewhere: of debt, domestic violence and illness. Adivasi women’s movements in Jharkhand, Chhattisgarh and Odisha have campaigned against liquor shops and drunkenness. An honest account must hold together both the cultural meaning of traditional drinks and these concerns. Women’s campaigns against liquor have a long history in India beyond the Adivasi regions, notably the movement of rural women in Nellore district of Andhra Pradesh in 1992 against the government-licensed sale of arrack, which led to its prohibition in the state. Adivasi women’s campaigns have usually targeted commercial liquor shops and drunkenness rather than the household brewing of rice beer for ritual and hospitality.

    5.5.3 Fermented Foods of the North-East and Himalaya¶

    • Fermented soybean: akhunī (axone) of Nagaland, kinema of the eastern Himalaya, tungrymbai of the Khasi and hawaijar of Manipur are alkaline fermentations dominated by Bacillus bacteria, comparable to Japanese nattō (Tamang 2010).

    • Fermented bamboo shoot: mesu (Sikkim and Darjeeling), soibum (Manipur) and similar products.

    • Fermented fish: ngari and hentak of Manipur and tungtap of the Khasi hills.

    • Fermented leafy vegetables: gundruk of the Nepali-speaking communities of the eastern Himalaya.

    5.6 Hunting, Fishing and the Law¶

    Hunting and fishing were once important sources of food for most forest communities, and remain so for some. Hunting knowledge includes the habits and signs of animals, the making of bows, arrows, traps, snares and nets, and rules about sharing the kill. Collective hunts were also ritual and political occasions, as the Santal regional hunt was (Section 11.2).

    The Wild Life (Protection) Act of 1972 made almost all hunting illegal. For many Adivasi communities this turned a customary practice into a crime, and forest guards have used the law to harass villagers. At the same time, the decline of many animal populations, including through commercial poaching networks that exploit local hunters, is real, and some ritual hunts now take place in landscapes where wildlife has become scarce. Conservation organisations and some Adivasi leaders have negotiated changes, such as symbolic hunts, while other communities insist on their customary rights. In Nagaland, where communities own most of the land, several villages have decided for themselves to ban or restrict hunting (Chapter 2).

    Fishing in streams, ponds and flooded fields, often using traps woven from bamboo and plant-based fish poisons that stun fish in small pools, provides protein in many Adivasi diets. Large dams and the leasing of reservoirs to contractors have often taken fishing rights away from the communities displaced by them.

    5.7 Food, Identity and Stigma¶

    Food has been a marker of difference between Adivasi communities and their caste Hindu neighbours. Many Adivasis eat beef, pork, rats, insects or other foods that upper-caste Hindu norms regard as polluting, and drink rice beer and mahua spirit. Missionaries and Hindu reformers alike tried to change these habits. Some Adivasi reform movements, such as the Tana Bhagat movement among the Oraon and the Devi movement among the Bhils (Hardiman 1987), themselves called for abstinence from meat and alcohol, in part to claim higher status and resist exploitation by liquor sellers. Food choices are thus bound up with identity, status and resistance.

    Today such questions appear in debates over the inclusion of eggs and meat in school meals and nutrition programmes, over restrictions on the slaughter of cattle, and over the place of traditional foods in Adivasi identity. A respectful approach recognises that communities themselves decide what they eat, and that nutritional policy should be built around the foods that people actually value.

    5.8 Food Rules and Taboos¶

    All societies have rules about food: what may be eaten, by whom, when, and with whom. Among Adivasi communities, many clans have totems (an animal or plant) that their members may not kill or eat; some foods are forbidden to women during pregnancy or menstruation; and some are reserved for particular rituals.

    Some writers interpret such rules as hidden ecological or nutritional regulations, for example totemic prohibitions as conservation measures. Some rules may have such effects, but many do not, and some are harmful: restrictions on what pregnant and breastfeeding women may eat, found in many communities, can deprive them of protein and other nutrients at the time they most need them. Food rules are best understood first as expressions of social and religious identity, and only then examined for their ecological or nutritional consequences.

    5.9 Preservation¶

    Before refrigeration, food had to be kept by drying, smoking, salting, fermenting or storing in cool places. Adivasi households dry greens, mushrooms, tubers, fish and meat in the sun in the season of plenty for use in the lean months. In the humid North-East, where sun-drying is difficult in the monsoon, meat and fish are smoked over the kitchen hearth on racks that are a feature of every house. Grain is stored in baskets plastered with mud and dung, or in specially built granaries, to protect it from insects and rodents.

    5.10 The Weekly Market¶

    Adivasi food systems have never been self-contained. In most Adivasi regions the weekly market (hāṭ) is the place where households sell forest produce, surplus grain, vegetables, chickens and goats, and buy salt, oil, cloth, tools and, increasingly, packaged foods. In Bastar and other parts of central India the hāṭ is also a social occasion, where people from many villages meet, arrange marriages, drink rice beer and mahua, and hear news. Historically, traders at the hāṭ often bought forest produce at low prices and sold salt and cloth dear, and moneylenders used the market to bind collectors in debt; one aim of the minimum support price for forest produce (Chapter 14) is to change these terms. The hāṭ is a reminder that food knowledge includes knowledge of prices, seasons of scarcity and the negotiation of exchange, as well as knowledge of plants and cooking.

    5.11 Threats and Revival¶

    Adivasi food knowledge is under pressure from several directions. Loss of land and of access to forests removes the sources of wild foods. Agricultural policy and the public distribution system have spread rice and wheat, which has improved food security but narrowed diets, and the high-yielding varieties of the Green Revolution, which need irrigation and purchased inputs, have replaced many local ones. Stigma has led families to give up foods associated with poverty or “backwardness”, and migration takes young people away from the fields and forests where food knowledge is learned.

    An example from outside the tribal areas shows what organised revival can achieve. In the Zaheerabad region of Telangana, the Deccan Development Society, working since the 1980s with groups of Dalit women farmers, has promoted the cultivation of millets and pulses in mixed fields, community seed banks, a women’s community radio station and an annual travelling festival of biodiversity. The FAO’s study of indigenous food systems included this case (Kuhnlein et al. 2009).

    Against this, there are signs of revival: community seed banks, millet missions in several states (notably Odisha’s millet mission, launched in 2017), the inclusion of millets in school meals and the public distribution system, and a growing interest among Adivasi youth and urban consumers in traditional foods.

    5.11.1 Food Policy and Adivasi Diets¶

    Public food programmes now shape Adivasi diets as much as forests and fields. Under the National Food Security Act, 2013, most rural households are entitled to subsidised grain through the public distribution system, and children receive meals in schools and in the aṅganvāḍī centres of the Integrated Child Development Services. These programmes have reduced hunger, but they have supplied mainly rice and wheat, which has encouraged the shift away from millets and gathered foods. Several states have begun to include millets and locally grown pulses in the distribution system and in school meals, and to buy forest produce and millets from local farmers. The forest itself remains a source of food security in hard times: during droughts and, more recently, during the disruption of the COVID-19 lockdowns in 2020, many Adivasi households fell back on gathered foods and on grain stored in the village while wage work in the cities disappeared. That experience has strengthened the argument that secure access to forests and commons is part of food policy, not separate from it. Nutrition specialists have argued that programmes designed around the foods that communities already know and value are more likely to improve nutrition than uniform rations imposed from outside. They add a caution that applies to the whole of this chapter: traditional diets alone will not end undernutrition, which also needs income, health care, sanitation and attention to the nutrition of mothers.

    Key Points

    • Adivasi diets draw on many cultivated and wild species, processed by drying, smoking and fermentation.

    • Despite this diversity, undernutrition among Adivasi children is high, because of poverty and loss of land and forest access.

    • Millets and fermented foods are being revalued, but their holders need to share in the benefits.

    • Food rules express identity; some protect resources, others, such as restrictions on pregnant women, can harm health.
