    Chapter 8: Oral Literature, Myth and Narrative Knowledge¶

    8.1 Oral Tradition as Knowledge¶

    One Sanskrit word for literature, vāṅmaya, means “made of speech”. The Vedas themselves were transmitted orally for many centuries before they were written down. Oral tradition is not a defective form of writing; it is another way of composing, storing and transmitting knowledge, with its own techniques and its own weaknesses.

    Adivasi and folk oral traditions in India include myths of the origin of the world, of people, clans and crops; epics and ballads of heroes and deities; songs for every season, task and stage of life; laments, riddles and proverbs; and the genealogies kept by bards. They carry history, law, religious knowledge and ecological observation. A. K. Ramanujan, Stuart Blackburn and other folklorists have shown that Indian folk traditions are not crude versions of the classical texts but a parallel world of literature with its own aesthetics and its own answers to the classical stories (Blackburn and Ramanujan 1986; Ramanujan 1991a).

    Oral traditions keep knowledge by several means. Metre, rhythm and melody aid memory and make changes noticeable. Formulae and set scenes allow a performer to recompose a long narrative in performance. Bards, priests and genealogists are trained over years, and audiences who know the tradition correct or challenge them. Ritual and seasonal settings give each performance its meaning. Oral traditions also change: each performance adapts the tradition to its audience, and stories travel between communities and languages.

    8.2 Genres¶

    Myths are foundational narratives: how the earth was made, where people and clans came from, how humans obtained fire, grain and iron, why there is death. They state how the world is ordered and what obligations people have to deities, ancestors, animals and one another.

    Legends and histories recount the deeds of ancestors, migrations, the founding of villages, wars and encounters with kings and colonial officials. For communities without written records, these narratives serve as chronicle and as evidence of land rights.

    Folktales (stories told for entertainment and instruction) rehearse cleverness and folly, greed and generosity, and often turn the social order upside down: the poor youngest son outwits the king, the animal outwits the hunter.

    Songs accompany dances, festivals, agricultural work, marriages and funerals. Much of the finest Adivasi poetry, such as the Santal songs translated by W. G. Archer and the Gond songs collected by Elwin and Shamrao Hivale, is lyric song (Archer 1974; Elwin and Hivale 1935).

    Proverbs and riddles compress observation and judgement into memorable form. Riddles in particular test and teach knowledge of plants, animals and household objects, and riddling contests are a common entertainment at weddings and festivals.

    8.2.1 How Proverbs and Riddles Work¶

    Proverbs are short, fixed sayings used in conversation to sum up a situation, give advice or settle an argument. Their authority comes from being traditional: a speaker who quotes a proverb appeals to the community’s accumulated judgement rather than to his or her own opinion. In village councils, a well-chosen proverb can carry an argument. Many proverbs comment on farming, weather and the behaviour of animals; others on kinship, marriage, hospitality, greed and the relations between the weak and the powerful.

    Riddles work differently. They describe something familiar in a strange way (an animal as a person, a household object as a living being) and challenge the listener to find the answer. They train attention to the shape, sound and behaviour of things, and they are a favourite form of play at evening gatherings and weddings. Collections of proverbs and riddles in many Adivasi languages have been published by missionaries, folklorists and, increasingly, by Adivasi scholars; the most reliable give the original text, a literal translation and an explanation of when the saying is used.

    8.3 The Gond Tradition: Pardhans and Lingo¶

    8.3.1 The Pardhans¶

    Among the Gond of central India, the hereditary bards are the Pardhans, a related community who sing the Gond myths, the stories of the clans and their deities, and clan genealogies to the accompaniment of the bowed fiddle bānā. In return they receive gifts from their Gond patrons. Shamrao Hivale’s study of the Pardhans of the upper Narmada valley (1946) remains the main account of this institution (Hivale 1946). The Pardhans’ role as keepers of Gond stories also explains why modern Gond painting emerged from Pardhan artists (Chapter 9).

    8.3.2 Lingo¶

    The most famous Gond narrative concerns Lingo, the culture hero. The first written record of it was made by the missionary Stephen Hislop in central India before 1863 and published posthumously (Hislop 1866); many versions have been recorded since (Elwin 1949). In the versions most often cited, the Gond gods (or the first Gond ancestors) have been imprisoned in a cave by the great god; Lingo, born miraculously, frees them, teaches them agriculture and music, and organises them into the exogamous clan groups, each group worshipping a set number of gods, that structure Gond marriage and ritual to this day. Among the Muria of Bastar, Lingo is also the founder of the ghoṭul, the youth dormitory (Elwin 1947).

    The Lingo narrative is therefore above all a charter of Gond social organisation: it explains why clans exist, how they are grouped, and whom one may marry. It has also become a focus of Gond cultural and political revival, in which Lingo is honoured as the founder of a distinct Gond religion (Koyā Punem). Comparisons with heroes of other mythologies such as Orpheus can be suggestive, but they say more about the comparer than about the Gond.

    8.3.3 Creation Narratives¶

    Gond, Baiga and other central Indian creation narratives often begin with a world of water, from which earth is brought up, sometimes by a crow or crab or earthworm, and made firm. Elwin’s collection Myths of Middle India gathers many such narratives from across the region (Elwin 1949). They frequently explain the origin of particular crops, of iron-working, of death, and of the relations between Adivasi communities and their caste Hindu neighbours.

    8.4 Santal Oral Literature¶

    The Santal have one of the richest recorded oral literatures in India. In the 1870s and 1880s the Santal guru Kolean narrated the traditions of the Santal ancestors to the Norwegian missionary L. O. Skrefsrud, who published them in Santali in 1887; P. O. Bodding later edited and translated much Santal folklore (Bodding 1925). Archer’s The Hill of Flutes presents Santal poetry in its social setting (Archer 1974).

    8.4.1 The Santal World¶

    In Santal religion the supreme being is Ṭhākur Jīu; the most important boṅgā (deities and spirits) include Maraṅ Buru (“the great mountain”) and Jāher Erā (“the lady of the sacred grove”), worshipped in the village grove (jāherthān) by the village priest, the nāeke. In the Santal account of creation, Ṭhākur Jīu creates a pair of birds, Hãs and Hãsil, from whose eggs the first human pair, Pilcu Hāṛām and Pilcu Buḍhī, are born; their descendants become the Santal clans.

    8.4.2 Festivals and Songs¶

    The great Santal festivals are Sohrāe, the harvest festival in which cattle are honoured, and Bāhā, the flower festival of spring, when the sal trees blossom. Each has its own songs and dances. Santal song is overwhelmingly about love, marriage, separation and the beauty of the landscape; it also contains a record of history, including the hul of 1855.

    8.4.3 Forms of Santal Song¶

    Santal song is organised by occasion and dance. There are songs for the great festivals, sung by men and women in separate or answering groups; songs for weddings, which include teasing exchanges between the families; songs for the dances of the young people, which change with the season; and songs for the hunt. Most are short lyrics of a few lines, sung repeatedly to a tune that fits a particular dance step, with drums and flutes. Their poetry works through images drawn from the landscape (flowering trees, rivers, birds, the moon) and through hints and allusions that listeners understand. Archer’s translations, made with Santal collaborators, give a sense of their range: love, longing, jealousy, the pain of a daughter leaving home at marriage, and occasionally the shock of encounters with outsiders (Archer 1974).

    8.4.4 A Script of Their Own¶

    The Santali language was written in the Bengali, Odia, Devanagari and Roman scripts before Pandit Raghunath Murmu devised the Ol Chiki script, usually dated to 1925. Ol Chiki became a powerful symbol of Santal identity. In 2003 the 92nd Amendment to the Constitution added Santali (together with Bodo, Dogri and Maithili) to the Eighth Schedule of recognised languages.

    8.4.5 Adivasi Scripts¶

    Santali’s Ol Chiki is one of several scripts created in the twentieth century by Adivasi reformers who wished their languages to have a script of their own rather than one borrowed from a dominant neighbour. Several have since been encoded in the international Unicode standard, which allows them to be used on computers and phones. The main ones are listed in Table 8.1.

    8.5 Munda and Ho Traditions¶

    The Munda tradition recounts how Siṅboṅgā, the supreme deity associated with the sun, created the world and the first human beings. A central episode tells of the Asur, a people of iron-smelters whose furnaces burned day and night and scorched the earth; Siṅboṅgā, coming among them in disguise, tricks and destroys them. The narrative is recited at an important Munda ritual and has been read by scholars as a memory of the coming of iron-working and of conflict over it (Roy 1912). The Asur are also a living community of Jharkhand, one of the Particularly Vulnerable Tribal Groups, whose own traditions of iron-smelting survived into the twentieth century.

    Munda tradition also preserves the memory of how the first lineages cleared the forest and founded villages. These clearing rights (khuṇṭkaṭṭi) were recognised in the Chotanagpur Tenancy Act of 1908 and remain central to Munda land claims (Roy 1912; Singh 1966).

    8.6 Epics of Western India¶

    Western India has a rich tradition of oral epics, some sung by and for Adivasi communities, others shared between tribal and caste communities.

    Bhil versions of the Mahābhārata and Rāmāyaṇa. Bhil singers in Gujarat and Rajasthan perform their own versions of the great epics, in which the familiar characters take part in very different stories, often centred on women and on the gods of the Bhil world. The Gujarati folklorist Bhagwandas Patel recorded and published several of them; the best documented, the Bhārath of the Dungri Bhils, is described in Section 8.7. These versions are not corruptions of Vālmīki or Vyāsa but independent tellings, of the kind Ramanujan described in his essay on the “three hundred Rāmāyaṇas” (Ramanujan 1991b).

    Ramanujan’s essay, which showed how the story of Rāma has been told in many different ways across languages, religions and communities, including Jain, Buddhist and folk versions, was itself the subject of controversy when it was removed from a Delhi University syllabus in 2011 after protests that it offended religious sentiment. The controversy showed that the existence of many tellings, which is a commonplace for folklorists and for the communities who sing them, can be contested in public life.

    Pābūjī. The epic of Pābūjī, a Rajput hero who died defending the cattle of a woman to whom he had given his word, is sung in Rajasthan by bhopā priest-singers, often of the Nayak community, in front of a long painted scroll (phaḍ) that serves as a portable temple. Pābūjī is especially worshipped by the Raika camel-herders, who credit him with bringing camels to Rajasthan. John D. Smith’s edition and translation is the standard study (Smith 1991).

    Devnārāyaṇ and Tejājī. The epic of Devnārāyaṇ, a hero-god of the Gujar herders, is sung by Gujar bhopās before a painted phaḍ even longer than Pābūjī’s; the scrolls of both epics are painted by Joshi families of Shahpura in Bhilwara district; Tejājī is a folk deity of the Jat cultivators of Rajasthan, worshipped above all as a protector against snakebite. Neither is a Bhil epic, but both show how western Indian epic traditions are tied to particular communities and livelihoods. Table 8.2 summarises the narrative traditions discussed in this chapter.

    8.6.1 Performing the Epic: Paṇḍvānī and Gavrī¶

    Two performance traditions of central and western India show how Adivasi performers have made the great epics their own. In Chhattisgarh, Paṇḍvānī is the solo narration, in song and speech, of episodes from the Mahābhārata centred on Bhīma, accompanied by a small group of musicians. Its best-known exponent, Teejan Bai, from the Pardhi community, broke the convention that women should perform only in the seated style and became famous for her vigorous standing style; she received the Padma Vibhushan in 2019.

    Paṇḍvānī is usually described as a Chhattisgarhi folk form, but its roots lie partly in Adivasi tradition. The Pardhans, bards of the Gond (Section 8.3), and the Devār, a community of itinerant singers, sang Pāṇḍava stories drawn from oral lore, and among many Gond communities Bhīma is not simply a hero of the Mahābhārata but a deity, Bhīmsen or Bhīmal Pen, who is invoked in many places for rain. In these tellings Bhīma, the strong eater and fighter of the Sanskrit epic, becomes a figure of Adivasi power. Performers today distinguish two styles. In the vedamatī (“according to the text”) style the narrator, usually seated, sings and expounds a written Hindi Mahābhārata, the seventeenth-century version in dohā and caupāī verse attributed to Sabal Singh Chauhan; the style is associated with Jhaduram Devangan, a weaver who learnt the text by heart and made his name as a performer from the 1940s. In the kāpālik style the narrator draws on memory and the oral tradition, improvises, and acts out the characters on a moving stage; Teejan Bai is its most famous exponent. Students of the form have pointed out that the two labels are not neutral: once the text-based style was called “vedic”, the older oral lore came to be described as merely imaginative, a small instance of the way written and Sanskritic versions have gained authority over oral and Adivasi ones. The same tension runs through the study of the Bhil epics described below.

    In southern Rajasthan, Bhil men perform the Gavrī, a ritual dance-drama in honour of the goddess, during the monsoon month of Bhādo: for about forty days troupes travel from village to village performing episodes in which deities, demons, kings, traders and tricksters appear, with the performers observing strict ritual restrictions throughout.

    8.7 The Bhil Bhārath¶

    The fullest record of an Adivasi Mahābhārata is the Bhārath of the Dungri (“hill”) Bhils, who live in the Aravalli hills on both sides of the Gujarat–Rajasthan border, around Khedbrahma and Danta in north Gujarat and in the adjoining hills of southern Rajasthan. Bhagwandas Patel (b. 1943), a schoolteacher who spent several decades recording the oral literature of north Gujarat’s Adivasis, recorded the epic between 1984 and 1987 on some four hundred audio cassettes. Most episodes were taken down during actual performances; some were recorded afterwards at the singer’s convenience; and details missing from a particular performance were added after discussion with several singers. Patel published the result in Gujarati as Bhīlonuṃ Bhārath (1997); a Hindi version followed, and an English translation by Nila Shah appeared in 2012 (Patel 2012).

    8.7.1 Editing an Oral Epic¶

    The way the text was made matters for how it can be read. It is not the transcript of a single performance, like the Siri epic Lauri Honko recorded from Gopala Naika (Section 8.8), but a composite established by the editor from several performances and singers. Reviewers of the English translation have noted this, and also that the translation does not print the Bhili text alongside it, so that readers without access to the Gujarati edition cannot check the wording. Folklorists are generally cautious about composite texts because they can give a single, fixed form to something that exists only in variable performances. Against this, Patel’s edition preserves a narrative that might otherwise not have been written down at all, and it was made in consultation with its singers. Like Smith’s edition of Pābūjī (Smith 1991), it is best read as one carefully made version of a living tradition, not as the tradition itself.

    8.7.2 Performance¶

    According to Patel and those who have written about his work, the Bhārath is sung by specialist singers in a mixture of verse and prose, with instrumental accompaniment, and its singing is surrounded by ritual: performers observe restrictions of food and conduct before and during a recital, and episodes are sung on particular festivals and ritual occasions, including weddings and the rites that follow a death. The epic is therefore not entertainment that happens to use a famous story; for its singers and listeners it is sacred narrative, and parts of it are performed as worship. In this it resembles the community epics of Rajasthan and the South discussed in Section 8.8, and the Bhil Gavrī described above.

    8.7.3 A Different Story¶

    Readers who know the Sanskrit Mahābhārata, or its many retellings in the regional languages, will recognise the names and little else. The summary that follows draws on published accounts of the epic, which differ in detail. The Bhārath opens with a long chain of earlier births and curses, through which Śantanu and Gaṅgā come to be married, and Gaṅgā’s condition that her children be thrown into the river is set within a world of gurus, sages and rebirths quite unlike the Sanskrit frame. Kuntī and Gāndhārī are not princesses born to kings but are made by sages; the Pāṇḍavas are born to Kuntī from the gods in a sequence that differs from the Sanskrit account. Some of the episodes on which the Sanskrit epic turns are absent: there is no attempt to disrobe Draupadī, and there is no Bhagavadgītā, although Kṛṣṇa is a major character. The quarrel between the Pāṇḍavas and the Kauravas is presented as a dispute over the division of land.

    New episodes take their place. The serpent king Vāsuki, who in the Sanskrit epic is a victim of Janamejaya’s snake sacrifice, is in the Bhil epic a powerful and often triumphant figure of the underworld (pātāḷ). In one well-known episode Draupadī’s golden hair, too heavy for the earth to bear, falls into the underworld onto the sleeping Vāsuki, who comes to Hastināpur in search of its owner and becomes her lover while Arjuna, tied to the bedpost, is helpless to prevent it. Arjuna himself travels to the underworld and marries Vāsuki’s daughter. Throughout, Kuntī and Draupadī hold authority in the household and the kingdom: the Pāṇḍavas defer to their decisions, and Draupadī rebukes them in council. Several Gujarati and Indian scholars have accordingly read the Bhārath as a women’s epic. The reading has force, but it should not be pressed too far: an epic in which goddesses and queens command is not a straightforward record of the position of ordinary Bhil women, any more than the Sanskrit epic is of high-caste women’s lives.

    8.7.4 What Kind of Text Is It?¶

    G. N. Devy, who founded the Bhasha Research and Publication Centre, which co-published the English translation, and its Adivasi Academy at Tejgadh in eastern Gujarat, has argued in his essays that the Bhil Bhārath is not a “vulgarisation” or folk adaptation of the Sanskrit epic, and has suggested that it may preserve an independent, even an older, line of the story; his wider case for hearing Adivasi literatures on their own terms is made in Devy (2006). The first point is well made and is the view of most folklorists: like the Bhil Rāmāyaṇa, the Gond Lingo narratives and the community epics of other regions, the Bhārath is a telling in its own right, organised around Bhil gods, Bhil ideas of kinship and land, and Bhil ritual, and it should not be measured by its distance from Vyāsa (Ramanujan 1991b). The second point is a different kind of claim. The oral Bhārath was recorded in the 1980s, and there is no means of dating its episodes; it could as well contain recent elements as ancient ones, and the absence of an episode such as the Gītā proves nothing about the age of the rest. It is safer to say that the Bhil epic shows how a story known across India has been taken over by a community and made to carry that community’s own concerns.

    8.7.5 Patel’s Wider Record¶

    The Bhārath is one of several long narratives Patel recorded from the Dungri Bhils. His Bhil Rāmāyaṇa, Rōm Sītmānī Vārtā (1995), is described as the first Adivasi version of the Rāma story to be published as a book, and he also recorded heroic narratives such as the Rāṭhoḍ Vārtā, which Nila Shah has also translated into English. Together with the Rajasthani epics of Pābūjī and Devnārāyaṇ and the Gavrī of the Mewar Bhils, they show that the Aravalli region has one of the richest concentrations of oral epic in India, shared, contested and reshaped across lines of tribe and caste. Most of this material still exists in print only in Gujarati or Hindi; translation into English and, more importantly, publication in Bhili for Bhil readers remain unfinished tasks.

    8.8 Community Epics across India¶

    Pābūjī and Devnārāyaṇ belong to a much larger family. In almost every region of India, long epics in the regional languages are sung by professional bards, often over many nights, for particular castes and communities, in honour of heroes and heroines whom those communities worship as deities. The collection Oral Epics in India, edited by Stuart Blackburn, Peter Claus, Joyce Flueckiger and Susan Wadley, established the comparative study of this tradition (Blackburn et al. 1989). Its central observation is that each epic belongs to a community: it tells that community’s own story and values, is sponsored by its members, and is often performed as worship rather than as entertainment. The editors proposed a rough grouping into martial epics, whose heroes fight and die for honour, land or cattle (Pābūjī, the Ālhā of Bundelkhand and the Telugu epic of Palnāḍu are examples); sacrificial epics, more common in the south, in which the violent death of a hero or heroine leads to deification; and romantic epics centred on love and marriage, such as the story of Lorik and Candā (the Candainī) in Chhattisgarh and the Dhola of western Uttar Pradesh. The music and staging of several of these traditions are described in Volume 9, Saṅgīta–Nāṭya.

    Two studies show how much such epics carry besides story. In the Tulu-speaking region of coastal Karnataka, the deeds of the spirit-deities (bhūta) and of the heroine Siri are sung in long oral narratives (pāḍdana). According to the epic, Siri leaves an unfaithful husband and after her death becomes a goddess; at the annual Siri festivals large numbers of women are possessed by Siri and her companions and speak with their voices. Peter Claus’s work on the Siri tradition showed how closely narrative and ritual interpret each other (Claus 1975), and the Finnish folklorist Lauri Honko later recorded and published a complete performance by a single singer, Gopala Naika, together with a study of how an oral epic becomes a written text (Honko 1998). In Chhattisgarh, Joyce Flueckiger found that the region’s genres are divided by gender as well as by caste: women’s songs such as the suā nācā (parrot-dance songs) and men’s performances of the Candainī belong to different occasions and give different views of the same social world (Flueckiger 1996).

    These epics complicate the line between “tribal” and “caste” tradition. Many are sung by herders, bards and other once-mobile or low-ranked communities; their heroes are local deities rather than the gods of the Purāṇas, although Purāṇic gods often appear in them; and, like the Bhil epics and the Lingo narratives, they have often been dismissed as “folk versions” of a classical original that they do not in fact follow.

    8.9 Song in Central India¶

    Song is the most abundant form of Adivasi oral literature. In the 1930s and 1940s Elwin and Shamrao Hivale collected thousands of songs from the Gond, Baiga, Pardhan and their neighbours in central India, published in volumes such as Songs of the Forest (1935) and Folk-Songs of Chhattisgarh (1946) (Elwin and Hivale 1935; Elwin 1946). They include songs for the Karam festival, for weddings and for the ghoṭul; the dadariyā, short songs of love and teasing exchanged between young men and women in Chhattisgarh; songs of work, sowing and harvest; and laments. Their themes are love and desire, the beauty of the forest and the hardship of poverty, quarrels with officials and moneylenders, and the comedy of village life.

    These songs complicate the image of Adivasi culture as solemn and sacred. They are frequently witty, sensual and irreverent, and they comment on social change: the arrival of the railway, the forest guard, the school and the market. Like Santal songs (Archer 1974), they are best read as literature, not merely as ethnographic data.

    8.9.1 Women’s Song¶

    Many song genres belong especially to women: lullabies; songs sung while husking or grinding grain; wedding songs, including the songs with which women of the bride’s and groom’s families tease each other; laments at a daughter’s departure after marriage and at funerals. These songs have often been collected less carefully than men’s epics, yet they contain some of the most direct expressions of women’s experience: the hardship of work, the loneliness of the married daughter in a strange village, criticism of husbands and in-laws, and humour at the expense of men. Recent collections by women folklorists and Adivasi women writers have given them more attention. The most influential study of such songs in a caste village is Gloria Goodwin Raheja and Ann Grodzins Gold’s Listen to the Heron’s Words, on women’s songs, stories and proverbs in Rajasthan and western Uttar Pradesh. The authors argue that in their songs women do not simply accept the ideals of kinship and modesty that male and textual sources describe; they comment on them, mock them and put forward other views of marriage, sexuality and the obligations of kin (Raheja and Gold 1994). Joyce Flueckiger’s study of Chhattisgarh, mentioned above, makes a similar point about the division of genres by gender (Flueckiger 1996). Women’s songs of village India more generally are discussed in Chapter 10.

    8.10 Oral Traditions of the North-East¶

    The peoples of North-East India have rich traditions of origin and migration narratives, many of which have become central to modern ethnic identity.

    • Ao Naga: Ao tradition holds that the first ancestors emerged from the earth at Lungterok (“six stones”) near Chungliyimti, from which the Ao clans descend.

    • Khasi: according to Khasi tradition, sixteen families once lived in heaven and travelled freely between heaven and earth by a golden ladder on the peak of Sohpetbneng; seven families were on earth when the link was cut, and from them descend the Hynniewtrep (“seven huts”), the Khasi–Jaintia people. The story underlies Khasi ideas of right conduct and of the covenant between humans and God.

    • Mizo: many Mizo and related Chin and Kuki communities trace their origin to Chhinlung, a place where the ancestors emerged from beneath a great rock; the name has become a symbol of the unity of these peoples.

    Christian missionaries wrote down many North-Eastern languages for the first time, in the Roman script, and the first printed literatures were largely religious. Since the mid-twentieth century, writers in these languages and in English have drawn on oral narratives to create modern literatures (Section 8.14).

    8.11 Songs of Resistance and Memory¶

    Adivasi oral traditions preserve the memory of struggles that official histories long ignored. Birsa Munda (c. 1875–1900), who led the ulgulān (great tumult) of 1899–1900 against landlords, missionaries and the colonial state, and who died in Ranchi jail, is remembered in songs across Jharkhand as Birsā Bhagavān and dhartī ābā (“father of the earth”) (Singh 1966). The Santal hul, the Bhumkal of Bastar and many local revolts are similarly remembered. These songs are not simply records of fact; they interpret events, and they have been revived and reworked in every subsequent movement for Adivasi rights.

    Song remains a medium of protest today. Movements against dams, mines and displacement in Jharkhand, Odisha, the Narmada valley and elsewhere have produced new songs in Adivasi languages and in Hindi, sung at rallies and meetings and spread by cassettes and now mobile phones. They draw on older forms and tunes while addressing new subjects such as companies, police and the law.

    8.11.1 Outlaw Heroes¶

    Some heroes of Adivasi song are remembered by the state as criminals. Tantya Bhil, who in the 1870s and 1880s raided the rich in the Nimar region of central India and was said to share his spoils with the poor, was captured and hanged at Jabalpur in 1889. The colonial press called him a dacoit; Bhil and other songs and stories in Madhya Pradesh and Maharashtra remember him as a protector of the poor, and he is now commemorated as a freedom fighter. The contrast between the official record and the oral tradition is typical, and shows why oral history matters for writing the history of communities who left few documents of their own.

    8.11.2 Memory and Commemoration¶

    Oral memory has increasingly been joined by public commemoration. The anniversary of the Santal hul, 30 June, is observed as Hul Diwas across the Santal region; Jharkhand was created as a state on 15 November 2000, the anniversary of Birsa Munda’s birth; and since 2021 the central government has marked 15 November as Janjātīya Gaurav Divas (Tribal Pride Day). Statues, museums and school textbooks now present Adivasi leaders such as Birsa Munda, Sidhu and Kanhu, Tilka Manjhi, Rani Gaidinliu and Komaram Bheem as heroes of the freedom struggle. Historians note that such official commemoration can also simplify or redirect the meaning of the struggles: Birsa Munda’s movement was directed against landlords and missionaries as well as the British, and was also a religious movement of reform (Singh 1966).

    8.12 Translation¶

    Most readers encounter Adivasi oral literature in translation, and translation raises its own problems. Early collectors often translated freely, omitting what they found obscene or obscure, adding explanations, or recasting songs in the style of English ballads. Many recorded texts were translated not directly but through an intermediary language such as Hindi. Puns, allusions, metres and the music of performance are almost impossible to carry over. Modern collections increasingly print the original-language text alongside the translation, involve speakers of the language as co-translators, and give information about the performer and the occasion, so that readers can see how a text was produced. Readers should treat older translations, including those cited in this volume, as valuable but partial representations.

    8.13 Performance, Memory and Change¶

    Oral narratives are performed in particular settings: myths at rituals, epics on festival nights, work songs in the fields, laments at funerals. In many communities certain stories may be told only at certain times of year or of day, or only by certain people. The setting is part of the meaning.

    Performers combine memory and improvisation, recomposing a known story with formulae and set scenes (Section 8.1). Stuart Blackburn’s study of the Tamil bow-song tradition showed how performance itself turns the dead into deities (Blackburn 1988).

    Scholarly Note: Walter Ong and “Orality”

    Walter Ong’s Orality and Literacy (1982) contrasted the thought and expression of “primary oral” cultures, which he described as additive, aggregative, conservative and participatory, with those of literate cultures (Ong 1982). His book was influential, but many scholars of South Asia have criticised its sharp opposition: in India oral and written transmission have coexisted and interacted for millennia, and Adivasi communities have long lived alongside writing, even when they did not use it themselves. Oral traditions are not survivals from a pre-literate stage but a living alternative.

    8.14 From Orality to Print: Adivasi Writing¶

    The twentieth and twenty-first centuries have seen a growing body of literature written by Adivasi authors, in their own languages and in Hindi, Bengali, Odia, Marathi, Malayalam, English and other languages. It draws on oral tradition and also challenges outsiders’ images of Adivasi life. Its history is not simply one of a move from speech to writing. For a long time Adivasi words reached print through missionaries, officials and ethnographers who chose what to record and how to present it; the more recent history is of Adivasi authors, editors and publishers taking those decisions themselves. Modern Indian literature in general is treated in Volume 8, Kāvya Kalpalatā, which notes these literatures and refers to this volume for them.

    8.14.1 The First Adivasi-Authored Texts¶

    The earliest extended texts composed by Adivasis in their own languages were dictated or written for, or in collaboration with, missionaries. The traditions narrated by the Santal guru Kolean (Section 8.4) were published by Skrefsrud in Santali in 1887 and translated into English by Bodding in 1942 as Traditions and Institutions of the Santals (Bodding 1942). The work gives an account of the creation of the first pair, the wanderings of the ancestors through a sequence of named countries, the formation of the clans, and the rules of village life, marriage, the hunt and the council. It is mediated twice, by the missionary who recorded it and by the translator, and Kolean was speaking to an outsider; but it is a Santal account of the Santal past, organised by Santal categories, and it has been read by Santals themselves as a foundational text.

    A few years later, in 1894, the Santal writer Ramdas Tudu “Reska” published, in the Bengali script, a book on the Santal religion and its customs usually known as the Kherwāl Bansa Dharam Puthi. It was written by a Santal for Santal and other readers, without a missionary’s frame, and it is often cited as the beginning of Santal authorship. Raghunath Murmu (1905–1982), whose Ol Chiki script is discussed above, saw the script as one part of a wider programme: he wrote primers, a grammar and plays, among them Bidu Candan, for performance in Santal villages, so that the new script would be used for a literature that people actually read and watched. Comparable work was done for Ho by Lako Bodra and for Kurukh by Narayan Oraon (Table 8.1).

    8.14.2 Santali after Recognition¶

    The inclusion of Santali in the Eighth Schedule in 2003 brought institutional support: the Sahitya Akademi, India’s national academy of letters, began to give its annual awards to books in Santali, and Santali has been taught in some schools and universities in Jharkhand, West Bengal and Odisha. Recognition also exposed an old difficulty. Santali literature is published in several scripts: Ol Chiki, which most Santal cultural organisations promote as the language’s own script, but also Bengali, Odia, Devanagari and the Roman script used by the missions. A reader schooled in one state may not be able to read a Santal writer from the next, and the choice of script for school textbooks and examinations has been a recurring political issue. Recognition of a language begins the decisions about it rather than ending them (Chapter 13, Section 13.10).

    8.14.3 Writing in Hindi and the Idea of Ādivāsī Sāhitya¶

    Much of the most widely read Adivasi writing is in Hindi, the language of schooling across central India. Ram Dayal Munda (1939–2011), scholar, musician and cultural leader from Jharkhand, worked to revive Munda music and dance and to build institutions for the study of tribal languages; he wrote songs and poems in Mundari, Nagpuri and Hindi. The Kharia writer Rose Kerketta (1940–2025) wrote stories and essays in Hindi and Kharia, many of them about women’s lives in the villages of Chotanagpur. The Santal poet Nirmala Putul, from Dumka, writes in Santali, and her poems are widely read in Hindi; her collection Nagāṛe kī Tarah Bajte Śabd (2004) addresses the place of Adivasi women both in their own communities and in the eyes of outsiders (Putul 2004). Jacinta Kerketta, an Oraon poet born in West Singhbhum in 1983, has published several collections of poems on Adivasi life, displacement, mining and resistance, beginning with Aṅgor (2016) (Kerketta 2016) and Jaḍoṁ kī Zamīn (2018); she writes in Hindi, and her work has been widely translated. The Munda poet Anuj Lugun belongs to the same generation.

    This writing has been accompanied by a critical argument about what “Adivasi literature” (ādivāsī sāhitya) is. For most of the twentieth century Adivasi life entered Hindi and Bengali literature through non-Adivasi writers, some of them deeply committed: Mahasweta Devi’s Bengali novel on Birsa Munda, Araṇyer Adhikār (1977), and Ranendra’s Hindi novel Global Gāṁv ke Devtā (2009), on the Asur iron-smelters of the Netarhat plateau (Ranendra 2009), are examples. Adivasi critics, among them the Kharia writer and editor Vandana Tete and others associated with the Ranchi-based publishing and cultural organisations of the 2000s, argued that such books, however sympathetic, write about Adivasis rather than from within Adivasi experience. They proposed that Adivasi literature should be defined not only by the author’s identity but by a worldview: a sense of the collective rather than the individual hero, of land, forest and animals as kin rather than scenery, of continuity with oral song and story, and of a history of dispossession told by those who suffered it. A statement on Adivasi literature adopted at a gathering in Ranchi in 2014 set out such principles. Critics within the movement have warned that any fixed definition can itself become a cage, excluding writers whose subjects are urban, personal or experimental; the debate parallels earlier arguments about Dalit literature.

    8.14.4 Other Languages¶

    In Marathi, an Adivasi literary movement grew from the 1980s, partly in dialogue with the Dalit literature of Maharashtra. Poets such as the Bhil activist Waharu Sonawane and Bhujang Meshram wrote of forest, migration, debt and the police, and Adivasi literary conferences (ādivāsī sāhitya sammelan) have been held regularly. In Gujarat, G. N. Devy’s Bhasha Research and Publication Centre at Vadodara (founded 1996) published a periodical in several tribal languages and set up an Adivasi Academy at Tejgadh, and Devy’s anthology Painted Words (2002) brought together translated oral and written texts from many Adivasi languages for a national readership (Devy 2002). In Malayalam, Narayan (1940–2022), of the Malayarayar community of the Idukki hills, wrote Kocharethi (1998), the first Malayalam novel by an Adivasi writer. It follows a Malayarayar family through the first half of the twentieth century, as forest land passes to settlers and moneylenders and as new religions and schooling reach the hills; it won the Kerala Sahitya Akademi award, and its English translation (2011) found a wide readership (Narayan 2011). In the North-East, where Roman-script writing in Khasi, Mizo, Ao and other languages goes back to the missions, writers such as Temsula Ao (Ao Naga), Easterine Kire (Angami) and Mamang Dai (Adi, Arunachal Pradesh) have written fiction and poetry in English rooted in the oral histories of their peoples (Ao 2006; Dai 2006) (Volume 8, Kāvya Kalpalatā).

    8.14.5 English, Publishing and Controversy¶

    Writing in English reaches the largest national and international audience, and has raised in the sharpest form the question of who may represent a community. Hansda Sowvendra Shekhar, a Santal writer and physician from Jharkhand, is the author of the novel The Mysterious Ailment of Rupi Baskey (2014), which won the Sahitya Akademi’s Yuva Puraskar for young writers, and the story collection The Adivasi Will Not Dance (2015) (Shekhar 2015). In the title story, a Santal musician refuses to perform for the President of India at the inauguration of a power plant that will displace his people. In 2017 the Jharkhand government banned the collection and suspended the author from his government post, after protests that one story portrayed Santal women in a degrading way; the ban was later withdrawn. Writers and critics, Adivasi and non-Adivasi, divided over the case: some saw a community defending its dignity against an insider’s frankness, others a state using community sentiment to silence a writer who had criticised mining and displacement.

    Adivasi writers have also sought control over publication. In 2012 the Santal writer and publisher Ruby Hembrom founded adivaani in Kolkata, an Adivasi-run press publishing in English, with the declared aim of letting Adivasis document and present their own histories, cultures and literatures; its books have included bilingual editions of Jacinta Kerketta’s poems. Such ventures are small, but they mark a change from the period, described earlier in this chapter, when almost every printed record of Adivasi words was edited by someone else.

    8.15 Endangered Traditions¶

    Oral traditions depend on languages and on people willing to learn them. Among the most endangered are those of the Great Andamanese, whose languages are now remembered by only a few people; the last fluent speaker of the Bo language, Boa Sr., died in 2010. The Nihali language isolate of central India has only a small number of speakers. Many Adivasi languages with large numbers of speakers are also losing ground as children are schooled in the regional language or English and as families migrate (Chapter 13).

    The loss of a tradition is not always simply decline. Communities also abandon stories and rituals they no longer believe in, or that they find demeaning, and create new ones. Documentation should respect communities’ own choices about what to keep, and for oral traditions it should record the performance, music and setting as well as the words; the general principles are set out in Chapter 13.

    Key Points

    • Adivasi oral traditions include myth, epic, song, proverb, riddle and genealogy, performed by specialists and communities.

    • The Gond Lingo narrative is above all a charter of clan organisation, recorded first by Hislop and sung by Pardhan bards.

    • Santal, Munda, Bhil and North-Eastern traditions show the variety of Adivasi literature and its links to land and history.

    • Regional epics such as Pābūjī, Siri and the Candainī belong to particular communities, which sponsor them and often worship their heroes.

    • Adivasi writers and new scripts such as Ol Chiki have carried oral traditions into print and digital media; Adivasi critics now debate what makes a literature Adivasi, and Adivasi-run presses publish their own authors.
