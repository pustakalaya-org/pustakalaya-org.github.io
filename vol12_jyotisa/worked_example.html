    Appendix A: A Worked Computation with the Sūryasiddhānta¶

    This appendix follows the computation of the Sun, the Moon and three elements of the almanac for a single moment, using the parameters of the Sūryasiddhānta (Chapters 6, 7 and 13), and compares the results with approximate modern values. The arithmetic has been done by computer; a traditional astronomer would have done it by hand, in sexagesimal fractions, with the help of a handbook. The simplifications made are noted at each step.

    A.1 The Moment and the Day-Count¶

    The moment chosen is local midnight at Ujjayinī at the beginning of 1 January 2000 (Gregorian calendar). The epoch of the Sūryasiddhānta’s Kali age is midnight at Ujjayinī at the beginning of 18 February 3102 BCE (Julian calendar). The number of civil days between these two midnights, the ahargaṇa, can be found from the astronomers’ own calendar rules (as in Example 12.1, Chapter 12) or, more simply today, from the Julian day numbers of the two dates. It is

    \[A = 1{,}863{,}079 \text{ days}.\]

    As a check: in the siddhānta reckoning the Kali epoch fell on a Friday. Since \(1{,}863{,}079 = 7 \times 266{,}154 + 1\), the chosen day falls one weekday after a Friday, on a Saturday, and 1 January 2000 was indeed a Saturday.

    A.2 Mean Positions¶

    In the Sūryasiddhānta the mean Sun, Moon and planets are all at the beginning of Aśvinī at the Kali epoch; the Moon’s apogee is then \(90^\circ\) ahead of them and its node at \(180^\circ\). The mean longitude of each body is its number of revolutions in the mahāyuga multiplied by \(A\) and divided by the 1,577,917,828 civil days of the mahāyuga, keeping only the fraction of a revolution (Chapter 6). The Sun’s apogee moves only 387 times in a kalpa; its position is found from the time elapsed since the planets began to move, after the period of creation with which the Sūryasiddhānta begins the kalpa.

    Table A.1. Mean positions at the chosen moment, from the parameters of the Sūryasiddhānta
    Quantity Longitude
    Mean Sun \(255.61^\circ\)
    Sun’s apogee \(77.29^\circ\)
    Mean Moon \(184.98^\circ\)
    Moon’s apogee \(245.16^\circ\)
    Moon’s ascending node \(105.11^\circ\)

    A.3 True Positions¶

    The manda correction (Chapter 7) is applied with the variable epicycles of the Sūryasiddhānta: for the Sun \(14^\circ\) at the ends of the even quadrants and \(13^\circ 40'\) at the ends of the odd quadrants, for the Moon \(32^\circ\) and \(31^\circ 40'\), the value at intermediate points being found by proportion to the Sine of the anomaly.

    • Sun. The anomaly is \(255.61^\circ - 77.29^\circ = 178.32^\circ\), close to the perigee, so the correction is very small: about \(0.07^\circ\), subtracted. The true Sun is at about \(255.54^\circ\), that is, \(15.5^\circ\) in Dhanus (Sagittarius).

    • Moon. The anomaly is \(184.98^\circ - 245.16^\circ = -60.18^\circ\); the epicycle at this anomaly is about \(31.71^\circ\), and the correction is about \(4.38^\circ\), added. The true Moon is at about \(189.36^\circ\), that is, \(9.4^\circ\) in Tulā (Libra).

    A full computation would also apply the correction for the observer’s terrestrial longitude (zero for Ujjayinī), the correction for the equation of time, and, for the exact times of the almanac elements, the Moon’s true daily motion.

    A.4 Elements of the Almanac¶

    From the true positions:

    • the elongation of the Moon from the Sun is \(189.36^\circ - 255.54^\circ + 360^\circ = 293.82^\circ\); divided by \(12^\circ\) this gives 24.5, so the current tithi is the 25th of the month, the tenth of the dark fortnight (kṛṣṇa daśamī), half completed;

    • the Moon’s longitude divided by \(13^\circ 20'\) gives 14.2, so the Moon is in the fifteenth nakṣatra, Svāti;

    • the sum of the longitudes of the Sun and Moon, reduced below \(360^\circ\), is \(84.90^\circ\), which falls in the seventh yoga, Sukarman.

    A.5 Comparison with Modern Values¶

    For comparison, the positions of the Sun and Moon at the same moment (18:57 Universal Time on 31 December 1999) can be computed from modern theory. Using the standard abridged formulae of modern positional astronomy, accurate to a few hundredths of a degree, the Sun’s apparent tropical longitude is about \(279.65^\circ\) and the Moon’s about \(214.74^\circ\). Subtracting the official Indian ayanāṃśa of about \(23.85^\circ\) for the beginning of 2000 gives sidereal longitudes of about \(255.80^\circ\) for the Sun and \(190.89^\circ\) for the Moon.

    Table A.2. The computation compared with approximate modern values (sidereal longitudes, Citrā-pakṣa ayanāṃśa)
    Quantity Sūryasiddhānta Modern (approx.) Difference
    True Sun \(255.54^\circ\) \(255.80^\circ\) \(-0.26^\circ\)
    True Moon \(189.36^\circ\) \(190.89^\circ\) \(-1.53^\circ\)
    Elongation \(293.82^\circ\) \(295.10^\circ\) \(-1.28^\circ\)
    Tithi dark 10 dark 10 —
    Nakṣatra Svāti Svāti —

    Two lessons can be drawn. First, a text whose parameters were fixed about a thousand years ago still gives the tithi and nakṣatra correctly for this moment; but the computed Moon lies about one and a half degrees behind the real one and the elongation is out by more than a degree, which shifts the computed ending times of tithis by two hours or more. This is why dṛk almanacs, computed from modern data, and traditional almanacs, computed from the Sūryasiddhānta, can disagree about the day of a festival (Chapter 13). Most of the error lies in the Moon, whose theory in the Sūryasiddhānta has only the equation of centre (Chapter 7). Second, the good agreement for the Sun depends on the choice of ayanāṃśa, which is a convention: with a different definition of the sidereal zero point the apparent agreement would be better or worse. Comparisons of this kind between siddhāntic and modern positions are meaningful only when the frame of reference is stated.
