    Preface¶

    This is Volume 12 of the series Bhāratīya Jñāna Paramparā: Khagola Jyotiṣa: Astronomy. It is a history of astronomy in the Indian subcontinent, from the star lists and ritual calendar of the Vedic texts and the Vedāṅga Jyotiṣa, through the mathematical astronomy of the siddhāntas (Āryabhaṭa, Varāhamihira, Brahmagupta, Bhāskara II), the school of Mādhava and Nīlakaṇṭha in Kerala, and the meeting with Islamic astronomy, to the observatories of Sawai Jai Singh and the reform of the national calendar in 1957.

    The Sanskrit word jyotiṣa covers three different things: mathematical astronomy (gaṇita or siddhānta), horoscopic astrology (horā) and the interpretation of omens (saṃhitā). This volume keeps them apart. Its main subject is mathematical astronomy and the calendar. Astrology and omen literature are described in their own chapter, as part of cultural and intellectual history: the volume reports what the texts claimed without endorsing their predictions, which controlled tests have not supported.

    The place of Mesopotamian, Greek and Islamic astronomy in the Indian tradition is described as current scholarship understands it, including where scholars disagree. Claims that the ancient texts anticipated modern physics (heliocentrism, gravitation, the speed of light, the age of the universe) are examined as modern readings at the points where the texts give rise to them, and collected in the last chapter. Dates are given as the range accepted in current scholarship, with traditional dates labelled as such.

    The volume rests on the standard editions, translations and studies, above all those of David Pingree, Kim Plofker, K. V. Sarma, K. S. Shukla, Yukio Ōhashi and S. R. Sarma, which are collected in the select bibliography; Sanskrit passages are quoted from the editions cited at each point, and the translations follow them. Mathematical topics that arose from astronomy are treated briefly here and more fully in Volume 11, Gaṇita; Purāṇic cosmography and geography are treated in Volume 21, Bhūgola, and the sky-lore of tribal and rural communities in Volume 23, Jana–Jāti.

    The sixteen chapters begin with the naked-eye sky, for readers new to astronomy. They give a place to the Jain and Buddhist calendrical texts beside the Vedic, and to the handbooks, tables and families of the working astronomers beside the great treatises. Several chapters include worked computations, which a reader with a little arithmetic can follow step by step. The book ends with the transmission of Indian astronomy, its encounter with European astronomy in the colonial period, its place among the world’s astronomies and its modern readings. Appendices give a worked computation with the Sūryasiddhānta, a table of the principal texts with their editions, a guide to further reading, a glossary and a chronology.

    Nikhil Kumar Rajput
    Ramanujan College, University of Delhi
