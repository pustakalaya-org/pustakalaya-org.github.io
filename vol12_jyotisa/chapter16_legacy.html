    Chapter 16: Legacy: Transmission, the Modern Period and Modern Readings¶

    16.1 Introduction¶

    Indian astronomy influenced, and was influenced by, the astronomies of its neighbours throughout its history. This final chapter follows three threads: the transmission of Indian astronomy to the Islamic world and through it to Europe; the colonial encounter and the modern study and practice of astronomy in India; and some modern readings of the Indian tradition that claim too much for it. It then places Indian astronomy briefly among the other astronomical traditions of the pre-modern world and ends with an assessment.

    16.2 Indian Astronomy in Baghdad¶

    16.2.1 The first translations¶

    When the Abbasid caliphs founded Baghdad (762) and began to sponsor the translation of foreign learning, Indian astronomy was among the first sciences to arrive. According to later Arabic sources, an Indian astronomer came to the court of the caliph al-Manṣūr with an embassy from Sind, around 771 or 773, bringing a Sanskrit astronomical work, and the caliph ordered it translated. The result was the Zīj al-Sindhind (“Sindhind” representing Sanskrit siddhānta), associated with the astronomers al-Fazārī and Yaʿqūb ibn Ṭāriq. The source seems to have been a work of the Brāhma school, related to Brahmagupta’s Brāhma­sphuṭa­siddhānta (Pingree 1978, 1963). Other Indian-based tables were known in Arabic under names derived from Sanskrit: the Zīj al-Arkand (from ahargaṇa, “day-count”), based on Brahmagupta’s Khaṇḍakhādyaka, and the Zīj al-Harqan, whose name is usually explained as another Arabic form of the same word. The basis of the Zīj al-Harqan is less certain than that of the al-Arkand; it has been connected with Āryabhaṭa’s midnight (Ārdharātrika) system, which the Khaṇḍakhādyaka also followed.

    16.2.2 Al-Khwārizmī¶

    Muḥammad ibn Mūsā al-Khwārizmī, in the early ninth century, compiled a Zīj al-Sindhind that combined Indian methods and parameters with Persian and Greek elements. It survives in a Latin translation made in 1126 by Adelard of Bath from a revision by the Andalusian astronomer Maslama al-Majrīṭī, and it was one of the channels by which Indian astronomical methods, including tables of sines, reached Latin Europe. Al-Khwārizmī also wrote a book on calculation with the Indian numerals, known only from Latin versions; the Latin form of his name, Algoritmi, gave us the word “algorithm”. The history of the numerals is told in Volume 11.

    Within a few decades, however, Islamic astronomy turned decisively to Ptolemy’s Almagest, and the “Sindhind” tradition became a minority one, preserved especially in al-Andalus. The lasting Indian contributions to Islamic and later European astronomy were the sine and its trigonometry, some methods of computation, and the decimal place-value numerals.

    16.2.3 Numerals and astronomy¶

    The decimal place-value numerals travelled west in the company of astronomy. They were needed to handle the large numbers of the Indian period relations and day-counts, and they appear in the earliest Arabic works based on Indian tables. Outside India, the Syrian bishop Severus Sebokht in 662 praised the Indians’ skill in astronomy and their method of calculation with nine signs, the earliest foreign mention of the numerals. In India itself, a zero written as a small circle in a dated inscription at Gwalior (876) is often cited as an early securely dated example; the Bakhshālī manuscript, with a dot for zero, has been the subject of radiocarbon dating whose interpretation is disputed. The history of the numerals belongs to Volume 11; here it is enough to note that they reached the Islamic world and Europe largely through astronomical and computational texts.

    16.2.4 The word “sine”¶

    The Sanskrit word for the half-chord, jīvā or jyā (“bowstring”), was transliterated into Arabic as jīb, a word with no meaning in Arabic. Since Arabic was usually written without vowels, jyb was later read as the ordinary Arabic word jayb, “pocket” or “fold”. Twelfth-century Latin translators rendered this by the Latin word with the same meaning, sinus, from which the European words for “sine” derive (Plofker 2009).

    16.2.5 Al-Bīrūnī¶

    The most remarkable Islamic student of Indian astronomy was Abū Rayḥān al-Bīrūnī (973–after 1050), who came to north-western India with the armies of Maḥmūd of Ghazna, learned Sanskrit, and in about 1030 wrote his great book on India, the Taḥqīq mā li-l-Hind (al-Bīrūnī 1888). More than a third of it concerns astronomy, astrology and the calendar. Al-Bīrūnī describes the siddhāntas of Brahmagupta, Pauliśa and others, the yugas and eras, the nakṣatras and the methods of the almanac-makers, quotes Indian authors at length, and does not hesitate to criticise them, as in the case of Brahmagupta’s concession on Rāhu (Chapter 8). He also reports that he translated works from Arabic into Sanskrit for Indian scholars. His book remains one of the most valuable sources for Indian astronomy in the eleventh century.

    16.3 East and Southeast Asia¶

    Indian astronomy also travelled east. Buddhist translations carried nakṣatra lore and astrology to China from the third century, beginning with the Śārdūlakarṇāvadāna (Section 3.7). In the Tang dynasty, astronomers of Indian origin served at the imperial court. One of them, Gautama Siddhārtha (Qutan Xida), produced in 718 a calendar on Indian principles, the Jiuzhi li (“Navagraha calendar”), which used the Indian Sine and Indian methods for eclipses, and he compiled a great compendium of astral omens. The calendar was not adopted officially, and Chinese astronomy continued on its own lines; but Indian astrology, in particular the system of the nakṣatras and the cult of the nine “planets” including Rāhu and Ketu, entered Chinese Buddhism. Through the esoteric Buddhist “sūtra of the lunar mansions and planets” translated by Amoghavajra in the eighth century, it reached Japan, where it was practised as sukuyōdō (Pingree 1981).

    In Southeast Asia the Indian calendar took deeper root. Inscriptions in Cambodia, Champa and Java are dated in the Śaka era, with Indian months, tithis, nakṣatras and weekdays, and later Burmese, Thai, Lao and Khmer calendars, though using their own eras (such as the Burmese era of 638 CE), are luni-solar calendars of the Indian type, computed by methods derived from Indian handbooks. Some remain in use, in modified forms, for religious purposes and festivals.

    16.4 The Colonial Encounter¶

    16.4.1 European interpretations: the antiquity debate¶

    From the late eighteenth century Indian astronomy became a subject of European controversy. Jean-Sylvain Bailly argued in 1787 that Indian tables preserved observations made in 3102 BCE (Bailly 1787), a conclusion that fitted the thesis of his earlier writings that astronomy had begun with a lost ancient people of Asia. William Jones, in Calcutta, argued that the Indian zodiac had not been borrowed from the Greeks. Others, notably John Bentley, argued that the Indian texts were much later than they claimed and that their epochs had been computed backwards, a view that, in its essentials, later scholarship confirmed. These debates were entangled with European arguments about biblical chronology and with colonial attitudes to Indian learning. They are worth following in some detail, because their technical question, how old the parameters of a text are, is still a central question of the field, and because the methods used on both sides are still in use.

    Bailly and Playfair.

    Bailly’s Traité de l’astronomie indienne et orientale worked not from Sanskrit texts, which he could not read, but from sets of tables that had reached Paris: one brought from Siam in the late seventeenth century, one that Le Gentil had brought back from the Tamil country (Chapter 13), and others sent by missionaries. Only the Tamil tables counted directly from the Kali epoch of 3102 BCE; the others started from later epochs (the Siamese from 638 CE), but all could be traced back to the same system, which placed the mean planets close together at the beginning of the zodiac at the Kali epoch. Bailly argued that the tables’ mean positions at that epoch agreed with what modern theory gave for 3102 BCE, and he concluded that the epoch rested on observation. John Playfair, the Edinburgh mathematician, endorsed and extended the argument in his “Remarks on the Astronomy of the Brahmins” (Playfair 1790), examining among other things the tables’ values for the obliquity and for the inequalities of the Sun and Moon. The argument had a fatal weakness. A set of parameters computed backwards from later observations will agree with the sky at an early epoch to the extent that its mean motions are accurate, so agreement at 3102 BCE proves nothing about who observed what then. Laplace, in his Exposition du système du monde (1796), made this point, arguing that the tables had been computed backwards; and indeed the general conjunction of the planets that the tables assume at the Kali epoch did not occur.

    Davis and Jones.

    The first account of an Indian astronomical text based on its Sanskrit was Samuel Davis’s essay “On the Astronomical Computations of the Hindus”, published in Calcutta in the second volume of Asiatick Researches (Davis 1790). Davis, a Company official at Bhagalpur, had read the Sūryasiddhānta with the help of pandits, and he explained its day-count, its period relations and its rules for eclipses accurately enough for European astronomers to follow them. Jones’s paper “On the Antiquity of the Indian Zodiac” in the same volume argued, from the Sanskrit names and from the nakṣatras, that the Indian signs were not borrowed from the Greeks. Attempts were also made to date the text from its obliquity of \(24^\circ\); but the obliquity changes so slowly, and the text’s value is so rounded, that no such argument could succeed. Jones, for his part, assumed a great antiquity for the Indian system but fitted it into a chronology that would not contradict the Bible.

    Bentley.

    John Bentley (c. 1756–1823), who lived for many years in Calcutta, turned the method round. In a series of papers in Asiatick Researches from the late 1790s and in his Historical View of the Hindu Astronomy (Bentley 1825), he asked for which date the mean positions computed from a text’s period relations agree best with the sky, and concluded that the parameters of the Sūryasiddhānta fitted the late eleventh century. The principle is sound: it is the same principle that Billard later applied, with statistical care, to the whole corpus (Billard 1971). But Bentley applied it without allowing for the fact that texts were revised and corrected (Chapter 12), so that the date of a set of parameters is not necessarily the date of the treatise or of its author. He went much too far, placing Varāhamihira and other authors centuries too late, accusing Brahmin astronomers of forgery, and arguing partly from the needs of biblical chronology. Henry Thomas Colebrooke, who read the texts themselves, opposed him. Colebrooke’s essays and his translation of the mathematical chapters of Brahmagupta and Bhāskara II (Colebrooke 1817) dated the principal authors from their own statements, such as Āryabhaṭa’s statement of his age (Chapter 9) and Bhāskara II’s of his birth year, and so put the chronology on the documentary footing that it still has.

    The outcome.

    Neither side was right. The Kali epoch is a computed epoch, first used, as far as is known, by Āryabhaṭa, and the extant siddhāntas date from the fifth century CE onward, as Laplace and Bentley maintained; but the tradition is not a late forgery, and its parameters were not all invented at one time. The methods that the debate introduced, dating parameters by comparison with modern theory and dating authors by their own statements, became the two standard tools of the field. The debate also left a legacy of exaggerated claims in both directions, which set the pattern of claims and counter-claims about antiquity and originality that persists today (Section 16.6).

    16.4.2 European astronomers in India¶

    European observational astronomy reached India before the colonial period, with the Jesuits. At Pondicherry in 1689 the Jesuit Jean Richaud, observing a comet with a telescope, noticed that the bright southern star Alpha Centauri is a double star, one of the first such discoveries. Jesuit astronomers made observations of longitude and latitude in several Indian cities in the late seventeenth and eighteenth centuries, and some of them were in contact with Jai Singh’s circle (Chapter 11). Later the observations of the transit of Venus (1761 and 1769), the needs of navigation and the trigonometrical survey of India brought European astronomers and instruments to India in larger numbers, and led to the foundation of the Madras Observatory.

    16.4.3 The Madras Observatory and its Indian assistants¶

    The Madras Observatory began as the private observatory of William Petrie, an East India Company official, at his house in Egmore in 1786. Michael Topping, the Company’s marine surveyor, persuaded him to hand it over, and it became the Company’s observatory in a new building at Nungambakkam in 1792. John Goldingham, Topping’s assistant from 1788 and the first official astronomer, directed it, with an interruption, until 1830; the local time of its meridian, about 5 hours 21 minutes ahead of Greenwich, became “Madras time”, later used by the railways and a precursor of Indian Standard Time. Under T. G. Taylor (director 1830–1848) the observatory produced the Madras catalogue of more than 11,000 stars, and under N. R. Pogson (1861–1891), known for the logarithmic scale of stellar magnitudes, it discovered minor planets and variable stars and sent expeditions to the total solar eclipses of 1868 and 1871. Its astronomical work was moved to Kodaikanal from 1899 (Section 16.5).

    Much of the observing and almost all of the computing was done by Indian assistants, whose names appear in the observatory’s reports. Sanevasa Chairy, head assistant under John Warren (below), was the first at Madras to notice the great comet of 1811 (Kapoor 2019). The best known is Chinthamani Ragoonatha Chary (Raghunātha Cāri, died 1880), who seems to have come from a family of almanac-makers, joined the observatory as a young man under Taylor, and rose to be first assistant from 1864 to 1880 (Kameswara Rao et al. 2009). His routine work was the observation and reduction of star positions with the meridian circle for the Madras catalogues. In January 1867 he established the variability of a southern star now known as R Reticuli, which had been recorded as an ordinary star of magnitude \(8\tfrac{1}{2}\) in 1864 and had then disappeared below the reach of the telescope; it is a long-period variable with a period of about 278 days, and his is usually counted the first astronomical discovery by an Indian in modern astronomy. A second variable star credited to him in his obituaries cannot now be identified with confidence. In 1868 he published in the Monthly Notices of the Royal Astronomical Society the occultations of stars to be expected along the path of that year’s eclipse, and in 1872 he became the first Indian Fellow of the Society.

    Ragoonatha Chary also tried to carry modern astronomy into the traditional world from which he came. For the total eclipse of 1871 he wrote a pamphlet, issued in four Indian languages, explaining the eclipse and arguing from the Purāṇas themselves that the popular fears were unfounded; for the transit of Venus of 1874 he wrote a dialogue in English and versions in Sanskrit, Tamil, Telugu, Kannada, Malayalam, Marathi and Urdu, observing that the siddhāntas, especially the Siddhāntaśiromaṇi, mention the class of phenomena to which the transit belongs, but that their practitioners could not predict it. He issued for several years an almanac computed from modern tables, a dṛggaṇita almanac in the sense of Chapter 13, planned a Sanskrit manual that would combine the corrections of European astronomy “with what is proper to retain from our own works”, and appealed in a public lecture of 1874 for an Indian observatory to train Indian students in practical astronomy (Kameswara Rao et al. 2009). His career shows both the opportunities and the limits of the colonial observatory: it trained him and published his work, but it was an institution of the state, whose directors could describe the traditional calculations as worthless, and the bridge he hoped to build between the two astronomies was left unfinished at his death.

    16.4.4 Calendars for the colonial state¶

    Colonial administration needed Indian astronomy for a practical reason: land grants, revenue accounts, contracts and court records were dated in Indian calendars, and officials had to convert them. The first systematic answer was Kala Sankalita (Madras, 1825) by John Warren (1769–1830), a French émigré who had served the East India Company as a soldier, had worked on Lambton’s survey of Mysore, and was acting astronomer at the Madras Observatory from 1805 to 1811 (Warren 1825). Its subtitle describes it as a collection of memoirs on the various modes according to which the nations of the southern parts of India divide time. From inquiries among local calendar-makers Warren assembled the rules of the south Indian solar and luni-solar calendars and eras, with tables for converting their dates into European ones, and he recorded a Tamil calendar-maker’s computation of a lunar eclipse (Section 13.8). The book was intended for the Company’s college at Fort St. George and for officials of the revenue and judicial departments. It is the ancestor of the conversion tables of Sewell and Dīkṣit (Sewell and Dikshit 1896), which serve the same need for historians today. As acting astronomer, Warren also observed the great comets of 1807 and 1811; his accounts remained largely unknown until they were recently edited from his papers (Kapoor 2019).

    16.4.5 Editing and translation¶

    The nineteenth century brought the first critical study of the texts. H. T. Colebrooke translated the mathematical chapters of Brahmagupta and Bhāskara II (1817); Charles Whish reported the Kerala series to the Royal Asiatic Society (Whish 1834); Ebenezer Burgess published his Sūryasiddhānta (Burgess 1860); Lancelot Wilkinson and Bāpū Deva Śāstrī translated Bhāskara’s Golādhyāya (Bhāskara II 1861); George Thibaut and Sudhākara Dvivedī edited the Pañcasiddhāntikā and Thibaut wrote the first general survey (Thibaut 1899); and Śaṅkara Bālakṛṣṇa Dīkṣit wrote his history of Indian astronomy in Marathi (1896) and, with Robert Sewell, the standard handbook of the Indian calendar (Dikshit 1969–1981; Sewell and Dikshit 1896).

    The last two names stand for something new: Indian scholars trained in the tradition writing its history by the critical methods of the age. Sudhākara Dvivedī (1855–1910), who taught mathematics and astronomy at the Sanskrit College in Banaras, edited Brahmagupta’s Brāhmasphuṭasiddhānta (1902) with his own Sanskrit commentary, collaborated with Thibaut on the Pañcasiddhāntikā, and wrote in Sanskrit the Gaṇakataraṅgiṇī (1892), a series of biographical notices of Indian astronomers and mathematicians that gathered, from the colophons and the commentaries, the dates and places that the authors give of themselves. Dīkṣit (1853–1898), a schoolteacher in western India, wrote his Bhāratīya Jyotiḥśāstra as a full history, from the Vedic texts to the almanacs of his own day; it weighs the European arguments about antiquity and borrowing against the texts, rejects the extreme positions on both sides, and remains, in its English translation, a useful survey. Dīkṣit also took part in the debates over almanac reform in western India. Their work answered Bentley’s charge of forgery in the most effective way, by showing that the tradition itself preserved the evidence for its own chronology.

    16.4.6 Traditional astronomers in the modern age¶

    Indian scholars trained in the siddhāntas responded in different ways to European astronomy. Bāpū Deva Śāstrī (1821–1900), professor at the Sanskrit College in Vārāṇasī, wrote in Sanskrit on modern astronomy and mathematics and taught both traditions. In Odisha, Sāmanta Candraśekhara (1835–1904), working with the instruments of the siddhāntas and his own, made naked-eye observations and wrote the Siddhāntadarpaṇa, revising the traditional parameters and adding new corrections to the lunar theory. In western India the debate over dṛk almanacs (Chapter 13) engaged astronomers, Sanskrit scholars and religious authorities.

    Sāmanta Candraśekhara and the Siddhāntadarpaṇa.

    Candraśekhara’s work is the last major attempt to improve the siddhānta tradition from within, by its own methods, and it deserves a fuller account. He was born in 1835 into the ruling family of Khaṇḍapāḍā, a small princely state in the present Nayagarh district of Odisha, and learned Sanskrit and the siddhāntas in the traditional way; he knew no English and had no access to European astronomy. Noticing that the positions computed from the texts did not agree with what he saw, he began to observe for himself, with instruments of bamboo and wood of his own design, among them a measuring instrument he called mānayantra, a gnomon and a water clock in place of a sidereal clock. Over many years he derived new values for the periods and other parameters of the Sun, Moon and planets, and set out his results in a Sanskrit treatise in verse, the Siddhāntadarpaṇa (“Mirror of the siddhāntas”), written in the Odia script. Biographies date its completion to about 1869. It is not to be confused with a short work of the same title by the Kerala astronomer Nīlakaṇṭha Somayājī (Chapter 10).

    The work became known outside Odisha through Jogesh Chandra Ray, a science teacher at Cuttack, who published it in Devanāgarī at Calcutta at the end of the 1890s with an English introduction comparing its values with modern ones. A review in Nature in 1899, under the title “A Modern Tycho”, praised the closeness of many of his constants to the modern values. The comparison with Tycho Brahe is apt in a second sense: Candraśekhara kept the earth at rest, but made the planets revolve around the Sun while the Sun revolves around the earth, an arrangement like Tycho’s and like the one that has been read into Nīlakaṇṭha’s model. He also added further corrections to the motion of the Moon, beyond the single correction of the classical siddhāntas and the second added by Muñjāla and Bhāskara II (Chapter 7), which later writers have compared with the variation and the annual equation of European lunar theory; how far they agree with those terms in form and size has still to be established from a modern edition. The Gajapati king of Puri and the British government honoured him, the latter with the title mahāmahopādhyāya in 1893. He died in 1904.

    His achievement and its limits are both instructive. With naked-eye instruments and the methods of the siddhāntas he obtained parameters better than those of the texts he had inherited, which shows how much the tradition could still do when its practitioners observed. But by the time his work was published, the questions it answered had been settled by telescopic astronomy and Newtonian theory, and his almanac reforms had to compete with dṛk almanacs computed from European ephemerides. He belongs, with Ragoonatha Chary in Madras and Bāpū Deva in Banaras, to the generation that tried in different ways to make the two astronomies meet.

    16.4.7 The history of Indian astronomy as a modern discipline¶

    In the twentieth century the history of Indian astronomy became a field of research in its own right, in India and abroad. P. C. Sengupta translated Brahmagupta’s Khaṇḍakhādyaka (1934). The Indian National Science Academy founded the Indian Journal of History of Science in 1966 and sponsored editions and translations, among them the Āryabhaṭīya of 1976 (Āryabhaṭa 1976), the Vedāṅga Jyotiṣa of 1985 (Sastry 1985) and the collective History of Astronomy in India (Sen and Shukla 1985). K. S. Shukla at Lucknow edited the works of Bhāskara I and the Āryabhaṭa school; K. V. Sarma, working from Hoshiarpur and Madras, edited dozens of Kerala texts and wrote their history (Sarma 1972). Outside India, Otto Neugebauer and David Pingree at Brown University made the Sanskrit sources part of the history of the exact sciences in antiquity, and Pingree’s Census (Pingree 1970–1994) catalogued the authors and manuscripts. Roger Billard in France analysed the parameters statistically (Billard 1971). A younger generation (Yukio Ōhashi, Takao Hayashi and Michio Yano in Japan; Kim Plofker, Christopher Minkowski and Clemency Montelle; S. R. Sarma on instruments; K. Ramasubramanian, M. D. Srinivas and M. S. Sriram on the Kerala texts) has continued and widened this work. Much remains to be done: most astronomical manuscripts have never been edited, and many texts discussed in this volume are available only in old editions without translation.

    16.4.8 Teaching jyotiṣa in the modern age¶

    Traditional astronomy continued to be taught throughout the colonial period in Sanskrit schools (pāṭhaśālās) and in families of astrologers, and it entered the new institutions founded under colonial rule. The Sanskrit College at Vārāṇasī, founded in 1791, taught jyotiṣa, and its teachers, among them Bāpū Deva Śāstrī, taught both traditional and European astronomy. After independence, Sanskrit universities, such as the Sampurnanand Sanskrit University in Vārāṇasī and the Rashtriya Sanskrit Vidyapeetha (now a central university) at Tirupati, continued to offer degrees in jyotiṣa, combining the study of the siddhāntas with that of astrology and the almanac. In these institutions the old unity of the three branches of the science (Chapter 1) has largely been preserved, whereas in the universities the history of Indian astronomy is studied as part of the history of science.

    16.5 Modern Astronomy in India¶

    Modern observational astronomy in India began with the Madras Observatory (Section 16.4.3) and continued with the solar observatory at Kodaikanal, opened in 1899, where John Evershed discovered in 1909 the radial flow of gas in sunspots that bears his name. In the twentieth century Indian physicists made fundamental contributions to astrophysics: Meghnad Saha’s ionisation equation (1920) explained the spectral sequence of the stars, and Subrahmanyan Chandrasekhar’s work on white dwarfs (from 1930) established the maximum mass of a white dwarf star and later won him the Nobel Prize in Physics (1983). Saha also chaired the Calendar Reform Committee (Chapter 13).

    The turn to solar physics that led to Kodaikanal began with an Indian eclipse. The total solar eclipse of 18 August 1868 crossed the peninsula, and European governments and the Madras Observatory sent observers along its track: the French astronomer Jules Janssen and a British party under J. F. Tennant to Guntur, John Herschel (son of the astronomer of the same name) to Jamkhandi, and Madras observers to other stations, among them Ragoonatha Chary, who was clouded out (Section 16.4.3). For the first time spectroscopes were turned on the prominences of the eclipsed Sun. They showed bright lines, and so proved the prominences to be glowing gas; one of them, a yellow line close to but distinct from the lines of sodium, matched no known element. Janssen found that the lines were bright enough to be seen without an eclipse and observed the prominences again on the following days; Norman Lockyer in England found the same method independently two months later. Lockyer, working with the chemist Edward Frankland, attributed the yellow line to an unknown element, which he named helium, from the Greek for the Sun; it was found on earth only in 1895. The eclipse of 1868, and the further eclipses observed in India in 1871 and later, made the subcontinent a regular destination for solar expeditions, and the decision to build a solar observatory in the clear air of the Palni hills at Kodaikanal owed much to this experience.

    After independence, research institutions in astronomy and astrophysics were founded or expanded: the Indian Institute of Astrophysics in Bengaluru (successor to the Kodaikanal and Madras observatories), the Physical Research Laboratory in Ahmedabad, the observatory at Nainital, the Tata Institute of Fundamental Research and its radio telescopes at Ooty (1970) and near Pune (the Giant Metrewave Radio Telescope), the Raman Research Institute, and the Inter-University Centre for Astronomy and Astrophysics in Pune (Kochhar and Narlikar 1995). India’s space programme launched its first satellite, named Aryabhata, in 1975, and has since sent missions to the Moon (Chandrayaan-1, 2008; Chandrayaan-3, which landed in the Moon’s south polar region in August 2023) and to Mars (2013–14), and launched the AstroSat observatory (2015) and the solar mission Aditya-L1 (2023). The names chosen for these missions (Aryabhata, Bhaskara, Chandrayaan, Aditya) express a sense of continuity with the classical tradition, but modern Indian astronomy is part of international science, and its methods descend from the European scientific revolution, not from the siddhāntas.

    16.6 Modern Misreadings¶

    The real achievements of Indian astronomy are often obscured by claims that it anticipated modern science. Such claims usually rest on mistranslation, on reading modern ideas into old texts, or on numerical coincidences. Some of the commonest are examined here.

    “Āryabhaṭa was a heliocentrist.”
    Āryabhaṭa taught that the earth rotates daily on its axis (Section 9.2), but not that it goes around the Sun. The heliocentric ratios hidden in the śīghra epicycles are no evidence either way, since every accurate geocentric model contains them (Section 7.3.3).
    “Nīlakaṇṭha anticipated Copernicus.”
    Nīlakaṇṭha’s revised model of 1500 has been read as a geo-heliocentric arrangement like Tycho Brahe’s, with the earth at rest; no connection with Copernicus or Tycho is known (Section 10.6).
    “Indian astronomers discovered gravitation before Newton.”
    The earth’s “attraction” in Brahmagupta and Bhāskara II explains why heavy bodies fall; it is not a law of force and is not applied to the heavens (Sections 6.7 and 9.6). A verse often quoted as Sūryasiddhānta 12.32, saying that the earth “holds the Moon by its force of attraction”, is not the text of that verse, which says that the earth is held in space by the power of Brahman.
    “Sāyaṇa knew the speed of light.”
    Sāyaṇa’s fourteenth-century commentary on Ṛgveda 1.50.4 quotes a verse praising the Sun, which traverses 2202 yojanas in half a nimeṣa. The verse is about the Sun’s daily motion, not about light, and the figure that results depends entirely on the lengths assumed for the yojana and the nimeṣa, which vary widely in the sources. Values close to the speed of light are obtained only by choosing them to fit.
    “Vedic astronomy proves the Vedas are much older.”
    Astronomical statements in Vedic texts have been used to argue for dates of 4000 BCE or earlier. The statements are imprecise and open to other interpretations, and they cannot outweigh the linguistic, textual and archaeological evidence (Chapter 3).
    “The kalpa gives the age of the universe.”
    The kalpa of 4.32 billion years is a multiple of a schematic age, and its closeness to the age of the solar system is a coincidence (Chapter 14).
    “The number 108 encodes the distances of the Sun and Moon.”
    The distances of the Sun and Moon are each roughly 108 to 110 times their diameters, and 108 is the number of nakṣatra quarters; nothing in the texts connects the two.
    “The Kerala school invented calculus two centuries before Newton.”
    What the Kerala astronomers found, and how far it resembles the calculus, is set out in Section 10.5; the transmission of their results to Europe is unproven (Section 10.8).
    “Indian texts record the returns of periodic comets.”
    The comet chapters of the omen literature describe types of comet, with their colours, shapes and omens, but give no dates, positions or observed durations; matching their names or the numbers in them with Halley’s or another periodic comet ignores what the texts are and what an identification requires. The first Indian comet records that can be dated are those of the Mughal court and the Jesuits (Chapter 15). In the same way, Ketu as the ninth astrological “planet” is the Moon’s descending node, a computed point, not a comet.
    “Ancient monuments are aligned on the stars.”
    Claims of astronomical alignment are made for Harappan sites, for megalithic stone rows in the Deccan and the south, and for many temples. Some are well founded: many temples are oriented within a degree or two of the cardinal directions, an accuracy that the gnomon method of finding the east–west line makes easy (Section 11.1), and archaeoastronomical surveys have argued for intended solar alignments at a number of sites (Malville and Gujral 2000). Others rest on choosing, after the event, one star or sunrise among the many that a line might point to, without a test of whether the agreement could be chance; for the Harappan sites there are, in addition, no readable texts to say what the builders intended. Each claim has to be judged by measurement and by its probability, site by site; temple orientation is discussed in Volume 16, Vāstu–Śilpa.
    “Indian astronomy owed nothing to anyone.”
    Indian astronomy absorbed Mesopotamian, Greek and Islamic ideas at different periods, as every major tradition did, and transformed them (Chapter 5).

    16.7 Indian Astronomy among the World’s Astronomies¶

    Many claims about Indian astronomy are comparative: that it was “older”, “more accurate” or “more advanced” than that of other civilisations, or, in the opposite direction, that it was “derivative”. Such claims can be judged only against an accurate picture of the other traditions. Comparative questions across the Indian knowledge systems are taken up in Volume 24, Samanvaya; here a brief sketch must suffice (Neugebauer 1975; Hunger and Pingree 1999).

    Other traditions.

    The astronomers of Mesopotamia collected celestial omens from the second millennium BCE, compiled the compendium MUL.APIN in the early first millennium, kept night-by-night “Astronomical Diaries” that survive from the seventh to the first century BCE, predicted lunar eclipses from numerical cycles, and in the last centuries BCE created a purely arithmetical mathematical astronomy that passed into Greek and, through Greek intermediaries, into early Indian systems (Chapter 5). Egypt contributed the 365-day civil year used by Hellenistic astronomers and the thirty-six “decans”, which became the drekkāṇa of Indian astrology. Greek astronomy contributed geometry: Hipparchus combined geometrical models with Babylonian observations, discovered precession and, it seems, made the first chord table; Ptolemy’s Almagest (c. 150 CE) derived its models and parameters step by step from stated observations. Chinese astronomy was an affair of state, with an imperial bureau that recorded eclipses, comets and novae for more than two thousand years and reformed the calendar scores of times; the Shoushi calendar of 1281 used a tropical year of 365.2425 days. Islamic astronomy began by absorbing Indian, Persian and Greek work and then developed on a Ptolemaic basis, with hundreds of zījes, the observatories of Marāgha and Samarqand, major advances in the trigonometry of the sine, and a sustained critique of Ptolemy’s equant. The Maya of Mesoamerica, wholly independent of the Old World, kept interlocking cycles of 260 and 365 days and a long count of days, and predicted the cycles of Venus and of eclipses with purely arithmetical tables.

    Tradition Methods of prediction Characteristic strengths Main social setting
    Meso­potamian arithmetical schemes and period relations long series of observations; eclipse cycles temple scholars; omens for the king
    Greek geometrical models derived from observation rigorous derivation; precession; chord table philosophers and mathematicians; astrology

    Chinese arithmetical and algebraic calendar methods continuous official records; calendar precision imperial bureau; portents for the state

    What the comparison shows.

    No tradition was isolated: Mesopotamian methods reached Greece and India, Greek models reached India and the Islamic world, Indian methods reached Iran, Baghdad and China, and Islamic astronomy reached India, Europe and China. Different traditions excelled at different things (Table 16.1): the Babylonians at long series of observations and arithmetical prediction, the Greeks at geometrical modelling and its explicit justification, the Chinese at continuous records and calendar reform, the Islamic astronomers at observation, instruments and trigonometry, the Indians at efficient computation, the trigonometry of the sine and, in Kerala, infinite series. In every tradition the accuracy of predictions depended less on the form of the model than on how often its parameters were checked against the sky. Everywhere, too, astronomy was intertwined with divination; the separation of astronomy from astrology was completed in Europe only in the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries, so the union of the two in Indian jyotiṣa is the normal condition of pre-modern astral science, not an Indian peculiarity. The telescope and Newtonian mechanics were a break for all these traditions alike. Indian astronomy is unusual in one respect: parts of it are still in daily use, since almanacs computed from the Sūryasiddhānta, the Grahalāghava or the vākyas still fix the festivals of hundreds of millions of people.

    Comparing fairly.

    Three errors commonly distort such comparisons: setting the best work of one tradition beside the ordinary work of another (the Kerala series beside a village almanac, or the Almagest beside a medieval European calendar); judging past work by its resemblance to modern science; and confusing priority with influence, since an idea that appeared earlier in one place need not have been transmitted to another. The surviving evidence also differs. Babylonian astronomy is known from thousands of tablets with dated observations, Greek astronomy from treatises that state their observations, Chinese astronomy from official histories; Indian astronomy survives mostly in normative texts that give rules and parameters but rarely the observations behind them, so their silence about observation is not proof that none were made.

    16.8 Assessment¶

    Freed from these exaggerations, the history of Indian astronomy remains a major chapter in the history of science. Over more than two thousand years Indian astronomers built and maintained a luni-solar calendar of great sophistication; created a trigonometry based on the sine, which, through the Arabic world, became the trigonometry of the world; developed a powerful system of computation with integer period relations and day-counts; produced planetary and eclipse models that they repeatedly tested and revised against observation; in Kerala, found infinite series for the circle and trigonometric functions and revised the theory of the planets; and, in the age of Jai Singh, combined Indian, Islamic and European astronomy in a single project. They did this within a social world that limited who could learn, and alongside an astrological practice that the same practitioners served. A sober history that records both is a better tribute to them than a list of claimed “firsts”.
