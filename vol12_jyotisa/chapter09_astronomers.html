    Chapter 9: Astronomers and Schools from Āryabhaṭa to Gaṇeśa Daivajña¶

    9.1 Introduction¶

    This chapter follows the principal astronomers of the classical tradition outside Kerala, from Āryabhaṭa (499) to the handbook writers of the sixteenth century. Their mathematical work is treated more fully in Volume 11; here the emphasis is on their astronomy, their parameters, and the schools (pakṣas) that grew up around their texts. The Kerala school is the subject of Chapter 10, and the astronomers who engaged with Islamic astronomy appear in Chapter 11. Dates are those established from the texts’ own statements or from their epochs; for many authors only the century is known.

    9.2 Āryabhaṭa¶

    9.2.1 Life and work¶

    Āryabhaṭa is the earliest Indian astronomer whose work survives complete and whose date is certain. He gives it himself (Āryabhaṭīya, Kālakriyāpāda 10):

    Āryabhaṭīya, Kālakriyāpāda 10

    ṣaṣṭyabdānāṃ ṣaṣṭir yadā vyatītās trayaś ca yugapādāḥ
    tryadhikā viṃśatir abdās tadeha mama janmano ’tītāḥ

    “When sixty times sixty years and three quarter-yugas had passed, twenty-three years had then elapsed since my birth.”

    Three thousand six hundred years of the Kali age were complete in 499 CE, so he was born in 476. He says that he set out his knowledge at Kusumapura, usually identified with Pāṭaliputra (Patna). The Āryabhaṭīya has 121 verses in four parts: the Gītikāpāda (13 verses), which gives the parameters in Āryabhaṭa’s alphabetic notation; the Gaṇitapāda (33), on mathematics; the Kālakriyāpāda (25), on time and planetary motion; and the Golapāda (50), on the sphere and eclipses (Āryabhaṭa 1976). A second work by Āryabhaṭa, using an epoch at midnight rather than sunrise, is lost but is known from Varāhamihira, Brahmagupta’s Khaṇḍakhādyaka and Bhāskara I; it became the basis of the Ārdharātrika (“midnight”) school.

    9.2.2 The rotation of the earth¶

    Āryabhaṭa is the only classical Indian astronomer who taught that the earth rotates on its axis. His parameters include 1,582,237,500 rotations of the earth in a yuga, and he illustrates the idea with an image (Āryabhaṭīya, Golapāda 9):

    Āryabhaṭīya, Golapāda 9

    anulomagatir nausthaḥ paśyaty acalaṃ vilomagaṃ yadvat
    acalāni bhāni tadvat samapaścimagāni laṅkāyām

    “Just as a man in a boat moving forward sees stationary objects moving backward, so at Laṅkā the stationary stars are seen moving exactly westward.”

    The next verse describes the daily rotation of the sky in the conventional way, as if driven by a cosmic wind, and later commentators debated whether Āryabhaṭa really meant the earth to move. The doctrine was rejected by most of his successors, including Varāhamihira and Brahmagupta, who raised physical objections (for example, that objects not attached to the earth would be left behind), and some later followers of Āryabhaṭa reinterpreted the verse (Plofker 2009). Āryabhaṭa’s rotating earth is a genuine and remarkable idea. The earth nevertheless stays at the centre of his universe, with the Sun and planets moving around it, so that his system is geocentric (Section 16.6).

    9.2.3 Other contributions¶

    The Sine table and its rule.

    Āryabhaṭa gives the 24 Sine differences in a single verse and a rule for generating them (Chapter 6).

    The circumference of the circle.

    Gaṇitapāda 10 gives the ratio of circumference to diameter:

    Āryabhaṭīya, Gaṇitapāda 10

    caturadhikaṃ śatam aṣṭaguṇaṃ dvāṣaṣṭis tathā sahasrāṇām
    ayutadvayaviṣkambhasyāsanno vṛttapariṇāhaḥ

    “One hundred plus four, multiplied by eight, and sixty-two thousand: this is the approximate circumference of a circle whose diameter is twenty thousand.”

    That is, \(\pi \approx 62{,}832/20{,}000 = 3.1416\). Āryabhaṭa calls the value “approximate” (āsanna). The Kerala astronomer Nīlakaṇṭha, a thousand years later, explained the word by saying that the exact ratio cannot be expressed; this is Nīlakaṇṭha’s interpretation, and it should not be read back into Āryabhaṭa as a proof of irrationality.

    The pulveriser.

    The last verses of the Gaṇitapāda give the kuṭṭaka (“pulveriser”), a method for solving linear indeterminate equations in integers, used in astronomy to find, for example, the number of days elapsed from given remainders of planetary revolutions (Volume 11).

    Parameters.

    Āryabhaṭa’s sidereal year, 1,577,917,500 days divided by 4,320,000 years, is 365 days 6 hours 12 minutes 30 seconds; the modern sidereal year is 365 days 6 hours 9 minutes 10 seconds. His revolution numbers (Table 6.1) were fitted to observations of his own time (Billard 1971). He divided the mahāyuga into four equal quarters and made his kalpa 1008 yugas, departing from the Purāṇic scheme, for which Brahmagupta attacked him (Chapter 14).

    9.2.4 Bhāskara I and the Āryabhaṭa school¶

    Bhāskara I (early seventh century) wrote the most important early commentary on the Āryabhaṭīya, completed in 629, and two works on astronomy in the Āryabhaṭa tradition, the Mahābhāskarīya and Laghubhāskarīya (Shukla 1976). His commentary explains Āryabhaṭa’s terse verses with many worked examples and is a rich source for the mathematics of the period. His rational approximation to the Sine has been given in Chapter 6. The Ārya school remained strong in south India, especially in Kerala, where the Āryabhaṭīya was studied and commented on until the eighteenth century.

    9.3 Varāhamihira¶

    Varāhamihira, of the region of Ujjayinī, was active in the sixth century; a late tradition places his death in 587. His astronomical compendium, the Pañcasiddhāntikā (epoch 505), was described in Chapter 5. He is better known as the great systematiser of astrology and divination: the Bṛhajjātaka and Laghujātaka on nativities, the Yogayātrā on military expeditions, the Vivāhapaṭala on marriage, and the encyclopaedic Bṛhatsaṃhitā on omens and many related subjects (Chapter 15) (Varāhamihira 1981–1982). Varāhamihira’s works became the classical texts of Indian astrology, commented on by Bhaṭṭotpala in Kashmir in the tenth century. The Bṛhatsaṃhitā includes a nice combinatorial calculation: from sixteen perfume ingredients taken four at a time, 1820 different perfumes can be made.

    9.4 Brahmagupta¶

    Brahmagupta was born in 598. He tells us that he composed the Brāhma­sphuṭa­siddhānta (“Corrected Treatise of Brahmā”) in 628, at the age of thirty, during the reign of King Vyāghramukha of the Cāpa dynasty, and he is associated with Bhillamāla (Bhinmal, in southern Rajasthan) (Sharma 1966; Plofker 2009). In 665 he wrote a handbook, the Khaṇḍakhādyaka, based on Āryabhaṭa’s midnight system rather than on his own.

    The Brāhma­sphuṭa­siddhānta, in twenty-four chapters, presents itself as a corrected version of an older Paitāmaha (“Brahmā”) siddhānta. Besides the full range of astronomical topics, it contains two chapters on mathematics, including the first systematic rules for computation with zero and with negative quantities, rules for quadratic and indeterminate equations, the composition rule (bhāvanā) for equations of the form \(Nx^2 + k = y^2\), and the formula for the area of a cyclic quadrilateral (Volume 11). Not every rule is correct by modern standards: Brahmagupta sets zero divided by zero equal to zero.

    Brahmagupta was a sharp critic. A whole chapter (chapter 11) examines the errors of earlier systems, above all Āryabhaṭa’s, attacking his equal quarter-yugas, his rotating earth and several of his parameters. He defended the Purāṇic yuga scheme and, as noted in Chapter 8, made concessions to the scriptural account of Rāhu that al-Bīrūnī later criticised. Brahmagupta’s work was the foundation of the Brāhma school, dominant in north-western and western India and in Kashmir, and it was among the Indian sources of early Islamic astronomy (Chapter 16). The commentator Pṛthūdakasvāmin (864) wrote on both his main works.

    9.4.1 The controversy with the Āryabhaṭa school¶

    Brahmagupta’s attacks on Āryabhaṭa set the terms of a long debate between the Brāhma and Ārya schools. His objections were of three kinds. Some concerned tradition: Āryabhaṭa’s equal quarter-yugas, his kalpa of 1008 yugas and his beginning of the Kali age contradicted the smṛtis. Some concerned physics: if the earth rotated, he argued, objects such as birds and clouds would be left behind. Some concerned accuracy: Brahmagupta claimed that Āryabhaṭa’s parameters gave wrong positions and wrong eclipses. The first kind of objection shows how the authority of scripture could be invoked in technical disputes; the third was the one that mattered for practice. Āryabhaṭa’s followers, notably Bhāskara I, defended him, and in the south the Ārya school prevailed. The disagreement was never simply settled; each school kept its parameters, corrected by bījas, for centuries (Pingree 1978; Plofker 2009).

    9.5 From Lalla to Śrīpati¶

    The centuries between Brahmagupta and Bhāskara II produced many siddhāntas and handbooks, most of which await full study (Pingree 1981, 1978).

    • Lalla (eighth century, the date disputed) wrote the Śiṣya­dhī­vṛddhida­tantra (“Treatise for increasing the understanding of students”), in the Ārya tradition, with a well-known chapter on “false knowledge” (mithyājñāna) refuting popular and Purāṇic beliefs about the earth and the heavens that conflict with the spherical astronomy of the siddhāntas.

    • Vaṭeśvara wrote the large Vaṭeśvarasiddhānta in 904, notable for its very detailed computational procedures.

    • Muñjāla wrote the short Laghumānasa (epoch 932). He is credited with an additional lunar correction and, according to Bhāskara II, with a steady rate of precession (Chapter 6).

    • Śrīpati wrote the Siddhāntaśekhara and a handbook, the Dhīkoṭidakaraṇa (1039), as well as astrological works. He worked in the Brāhma tradition.

    • Śatānanda of Puri wrote a much-used handbook, the Bhāsvatī (epoch 1099), with decimal rather than sexagesimal fractions.

    9.6 Bhāskara II¶

    9.6.1 Life and works¶

    Bhāskara (called Bhāskara II or Bhāskarācārya to distinguish him from Bhāskara I) was born in 1114 and completed his Siddhāntaśiromaṇi (“Crest-jewel of Siddhāntas”) in 1150, at the age of thirty-six; both dates he gives himself. He came from a family of scholars associated with a place called Vijjaḍaviḍa, whose location is uncertain; his grandson Caṅgadeva was court astrologer to the Yādava king Siṅghaṇa and founded a college for the study of his grandfather’s works, recorded in an inscription of 1207 at Patna in Khandesh (Pingree 1978).

    Bhāskara’s works became the standard textbooks of mathematics and astronomy in much of India:

    • Līlāvatī, on arithmetic and mensuration, and Bījagaṇita, on algebra, often treated as the first two parts of the Siddhāntaśiromaṇi;

    • Grahagaṇita, on the computation of the planets, and Golādhyāya, on the sphere, the two astronomical parts;

    • the Vāsanābhāṣya, his own commentary on these, with demonstrations;

    • the Karaṇakutūhala (1183), a handbook.

    9.6.2 Astronomy¶

    Bhāskara’s astronomy follows the Brāhma school, refined in many details (Bhāskara II 1861). He explains the procedures of planetary and spherical astronomy with demonstrations; discusses the instantaneous motion (tātkālika gati) of a planet, using in effect the rate of change of the Sine, and the condition that the equation of centre is greatest where its rate of change vanishes; adds the udayāntara correction to time (Chapter 6); reports Muñjāla’s steady precession alongside the oscillation of the Sūryasiddhānta; and describes instruments, including his own simple “board” instrument (phalakayantra). In the Golādhyāya he says that the earth has a power of attraction by which heavy objects in the air are drawn to it and appear to fall, and he uses the sphere’s lack of support to refute Purāṇic views that the earth rests on something. He also criticised the Purāṇic cosmology of a flat earth with Mount Meru at its centre, while reinterpreting parts of it to fit the sphere (Minkowski 2004).

    The Golādhyāya is also a work of literature. It opens its account of the sphere with a defence of the study of astronomy, includes a poetic description of the seasons, and ends with a set of problems (praśnas) for the student, many of them requiring real ingenuity in spherical trigonometry. Bhāskara’s style, clear and elegant, explains much of the lasting popularity of his works, which were taught across India until the nineteenth century and translated into Persian at the Mughal court.

    Bhāskara’s “attraction” is a qualitative explanation of why heavy things fall towards the earth. It states no relation between attraction and mass or distance and is not applied to the planets, whose motions in his system are still those of the epicycles; the modern claim that it amounts to a theory of gravitation is discussed in Section 16.6. His rules for instantaneous motion belong with the computation of planetary stations (Section 7.5).

    9.6.3 The legend of Līlāvatī¶

    A well-known story makes Līlāvatī, whose name is the title of Bhāskara’s arithmetic, his daughter. Her horoscope, the story goes, showed that she would be widowed if she did not marry at a precise moment; her father set up a water clock to measure it, but a pearl from her ornaments fell into the bowl and blocked the hole, the moment passed unnoticed, and she was widowed soon after. To console her Bhāskara named his book after her. The story is told in the preface to Fayḍī’s Persian translation of 1587 (Section 11.3) and became popular in modern retellings. It has no support in Bhāskara’s own works, in which “Līlāvatī” is the name of the book and, in its examples, an addressee (“O beautiful one”), and it should be treated as legend. It is nevertheless revealing: it combines the water clock, the horoscope and the dangers of a woman’s “ill-starred” marriage, the very themes discussed in this volume.

    9.7 After Bhāskara¶

    After the twelfth century the most influential astronomical works in northern and western India were handbooks and tables rather than full siddhāntas: the tables of Mahādeva (early fourteenth century) and the Makaranda (Vārāṇasī, 1478), the Grahakautuka (1496) of Keśava of Nandigrāma and the Grahalāghava (1520) of his son Gaṇeśa Daivajña. How these worked, and who used them, is the subject of Chapter 12. Rāma Daivajña’s Muhūrtacintāmaṇi (1600), on electing auspicious times, became the most influential astrological handbook of its kind (Chapter 15).

    Full siddhāntas continued to be written. The best documented of the early sixteenth century is the Siddhāntasundara of Jñānarāja, completed in 1503. Jñānarāja belonged to a family of astronomers at Pārthapura on the Godāvarī, in Maharashtra, and his son Sūryadāsa wrote commentaries on Bhāskara II’s Līlāvatī and Bījagaṇita. The Siddhāntasundara is written in ornate verse, and it is notable for the length at which it treats cosmology: Jñānarāja set out to reconcile the Purāṇic account of the earth, with Mount Meru at its centre, with the spherical earth of the siddhāntas, a problem that occupied astronomers for the next two centuries (Section 14.5). The work has been translated in part, with a detailed study, by Knudsen (2014).

    9.8 Jain and Buddhist Astronomers¶

    Jain scholars contributed to astronomy both within their own cosmological tradition and in the common siddhānta tradition. The canonical astronomical texts and Malayagiri’s twelfth-century commentaries on them have been described in Section 3.6. Jain monks and laymen wrote in the siddhānta tradition: the mathematician Mahāvīra (ninth century), whose Gaṇitasārasaṅgraha served astronomers among others; Mahendra Sūri, author of the first Sanskrit treatise on the astrolabe (1370), and his pupil Malayendu Sūri; and a number of authors of handbooks and astrological works in western India. Jain libraries in Gujarat and Rajasthan are among the richest repositories of astronomical manuscripts (Pingree 1981).

    Buddhist authors are rarer in the siddhānta tradition, for the monastic rules discouraged monks from making a living by astrology (Chapter 15), and the early Buddhist sources are calendrical rather than mathematical (Section 3.7). The great exception is the Kālacakratantra (“Wheel of Time”), composed in the early eleventh century, whose first chapter, with its commentary the Vimalaprabhā, contains a complete calendrical astronomy; the tantra as a whole is described in Volume 4, Buddha–Jina Prabhā. Its methods are those of a handbook (Chapter 12): mean motions counted from a recent epoch with small integer ratios, short tables for the equations of the Sun and Moon, the five elements of the almanac, the true planets and eclipses, together with the sixty-year cycle. The tantra was taken to Tibet, where the first sixty-year cycle of the Tibetan calendar is counted from 1027, and Tibetan almanacs are still computed by rules derived from it (Henning 2007). The Kālacakra thus preserves, in a Buddhist setting and outside India, a form of Indian handbook astronomy that has been in continuous use for nearly a thousand years.

    9.9 The Commentators¶

    Much of the history of Indian astronomy after the major authors is a history of commentary. Commentators explained the root texts, supplied worked examples and demonstrations, compared them with other systems, and sometimes corrected them. Some of the most important are listed in Table 9.1.

    Āryabhaṭīya Nīlakaṇṭha Somayājī c. 1500; extensive, with new theory (Chapter 10)

    9.10 Astronomers and Their Patrons¶

    The texts let us glimpse the social world of the astronomers. Authors name their fathers, teachers and places, and sometimes their kings: Brahmagupta the Cāpa king Vyāghramukha, Bhāskara II’s descendants the Yādava court of Devagiri, Mahendra Sūri the Delhi sultan, Nityānanda the Mughal emperor. Inscriptions record land granted to astrologers and the endowment of a school for the study of Bhāskara’s works. A family of astronomers could serve the same court for generations, and some families, like that of Gaṇeśa Daivajña or the astronomers of Vārāṇasī in the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries, produced a whole series of authors over several generations (Pingree 1981; Minkowski 2004). The astronomer’s main source of income, then as now, was usually not research but the almanac, the casting of horoscopes and the choice of auspicious times; the most famous mathematical astronomers also wrote on astrology.

    9.11 The Schools¶

    By the end of the first millennium Indian mathematical astronomy had settled into a small number of schools, each defined by its basic parameters and associated with particular regions (Pingree 1978, 1981).

    Table 9.2. The principal schools (pakṣas) of Indian mathematical astronomy
    School Basis Main region
    Brāhma Brahmagupta’s Brāhma­sphuṭa­siddhānta (628); later Bhāskara II north-west and west; Kashmir; Rajasthan and Gujarat
    Ārya Āryabhaṭa’s Āryabhaṭīya (499), sunrise epoch south India, especially Kerala
    Ārdharātrika Āryabhaṭa’s lost midnight system; Brahmagupta’s Khaṇḍakhādyaka (665) north India in the earlier period
    Saura the Sūryasiddhānta north and east India; widely used later
    Gaṇeśa Gaṇeśa Daivajña’s Grahalāghava (1520) Maharashtra, Gujarat and beyond

    The schools were not sects: astronomers read each other’s works, criticised and borrowed from them, and the boundaries shifted. But the differences in parameters were real and could produce different dates for festivals and different predictions of eclipses, which made the choice of school a matter of practical consequence.
