    Chapter 3: Vedic Astronomy and the Vedāṅga Jyotiṣa¶

    Vedāṅga Jyotiṣa, Ārca recension 36 (Yājuṣa 3)

    vedā hi yajñārtham abhipravṛttāḥ kālānupūrvā vihitāś ca yajñāḥ
    tasmād idaṃ kālavidhānaśāstraṃ yo jyotiṣaṃ veda sa veda yajñān

    “The Vedas came forth for the sake of the sacrifices, and the sacrifices are prescribed in the order of times. Therefore whoever knows this science of the ordering of time, jyotiṣa, knows the sacrifices.”

    3.1 Astronomy in a Ritual Culture¶

    The earliest Indian astronomy is found in texts whose purpose was religious. The Vedic hymns and the prose texts that explain the ritual (the Brāhmaṇas) and regulate it (the Śrautasūtras) needed a calendar: the new- and full-moon offerings (darśapūrṇamāsa) were performed every fortnight; the seasonal offerings (cāturmāsya) every four months; the great year-long sacrificial sessions (sattras such as the gavām ayana, “course of the cows”) were organised around the solstices and the middle day of the year (viṣuvat). The fire was to be installed under particular nakṣatras. Knowing the times was therefore part of knowing the ritual, as the Vedāṅga Jyotiṣa says in the verse quoted above.

    This chapter surveys what the Vedic texts tell us about the sky, the five-year calendar of the Vedāṅga Jyotiṣa, the related astronomy of the Jain canon, and the long debate about how far astronomical statements in these texts can be used to date them. The nakṣatras themselves are treated in Chapter 4. A fuller account of the Vedic corpus and its chronology is given in Volume 1 of this series. Here, following the series standard, the core of the Ṛgveda is dated c. 1500–1200 BCE and the later Vedic texts c. 1200–500 BCE.

    3.2 The Sky in the Ṛgveda¶

    The Ṛgveda is not an astronomical text, but its poets watched the sky. The Sun (Sūrya, Savitṛ) and the Dawn (Uṣas) are among its most frequently praised deities; the Moon is identified, in later hymns, with Soma. Some passages are of direct calendrical interest:

    • A year of twelve months and 360 days. The riddle hymn RV 1.164 speaks of a wheel with twelve spokes on which stand 720 sons in pairs (v. 11) and of a wheel with twelve fellies on which are fixed 360 pegs (v. 48). The usual reading is that the poet is speaking of the year of twelve months and 360 days and nights. A 360-day “civil” year, with the excess days handled by intercalation, remained the ritual norm in the later Vedic texts.

    • An intercalary month? RV 1.25.8 says that Varuṇa knows the twelve months and the one that is born in addition. This has often been read as a reference to an intercalary thirteenth month, which the later Vedic texts certainly know (under names such as saṃsarpa and aṃhaspati).

    • An eclipse. RV 5.40.5–9 tells how the demon Svarbhānu pierced the Sun with darkness and how the seer Atri found the hidden Sun again. This is the earliest Indian description of a solar eclipse. Attempts to identify the eclipse and so date the hymn have produced widely different results and are not reliable.

    • Asterisms. The wedding hymn of Sūryā (RV 10.85.13) mentions the asterisms Aghā and Arjunī, which correspond to the later Maghā and Phalgunī. The word nakṣatra occurs in the Ṛgveda, but the full list of asterisms appears only in later texts.

    3.3 The Later Vedic Calendar¶

    The Yajurveda Saṃhitās and the Brāhmaṇas are far richer in calendrical detail (Ōhashi 1993). The Taittirīya Saṃhitā (4.4.10) lists twenty-seven nakṣatras with their presiding deities, beginning with Kṛttikā (the Pleiades). The Atharvaveda (19.7) gives twenty-eight, including Abhijit. The Taittirīya Saṃhitā (4.4.11) also gives the old names of the twelve months, grouped in pairs into the six seasons (ṛtus): Madhu and Mādhava (spring), Śukra and Śuci (summer), Nabhas and Nabhasya (rains), Iṣa and Ūrja (autumn), Sahas and Sahasya (early winter), Tapas and Tapasya (late winter). The later month names (Caitra, Vaiśākha and the rest), taken from the nakṣatra in which the full moon falls, came into use in the late Vedic period and are standard in classical texts.

    Table 3.1. The six seasons and the old month names of the Taittirīya Saṃhitā, with the later lunar months to which they roughly correspond
    Season (ṛtu) Old month names Later months
    Vasanta (spring) Madhu, Mādhava Caitra, Vaiśākha
    Grīṣma (summer) Śukra, Śuci Jyaiṣṭha, Āṣāḍha
    Varṣā (rains) Nabhas, Nabhasya Śrāvaṇa, Bhādrapada
    Śarad (autumn) Iṣa, Ūrja Āśvina, Kārttika
    Hemanta (early winter) Sahas, Sahasya Mārgaśīrṣa, Pauṣa
    Śiśira (late winter) Tapas, Tapasya Māgha, Phālguna

    The texts know the two halves of the year in which the Sun moves northward and southward along the horizon (later called uttarāyaṇa and dakṣiṇāyana), the six seasons, the bright and dark fortnights, and the days of new and full moon. Several passages discuss which full moon is the “mouth” (beginning) of the year, and give different answers. They also show some observational practice. The Śulbasūtras (c. 800–200 BCE for the corpus as a whole, Baudhāyana’s being the earliest), manuals of altar construction attached to the Śrautasūtras, prescribe how to find the east–west line with a gnomon: mark the points where the tip of its shadow touches a circle drawn around it in the morning and in the afternoon, and join them (Ōhashi 1993). The geometry of these manuals, important for the history of mathematics, is discussed in Volume 11.

    3.4 The Vedāṅga Jyotiṣa¶

    3.4.1 The text¶

    The Vedāṅga Jyotiṣa is the oldest surviving Indian text devoted to astronomy. It survives in two recensions: the Ārca Jyotiṣa, attached to the Ṛgveda, in 36 verses, and the Yājuṣa Jyotiṣa, attached to the Yajurveda, in 43 or 44 verses, most of them shared with the Ārca. (The Ātharvaṇa Jyotiṣa is a different and later work on auspicious times.) The text says that it sets out the knowledge of time of Lagadha, who is otherwise unknown. Its verses are very compressed, the text is corrupt in places, and several verses are still not securely understood. The standard edition, with the translation and notes of T. S. Kuppanna Sastry, is that of K. V. Sarma (Sastry 1985).

    A well-known verse (Ārca 35; Yājuṣa 4) compares the science to the crest of the peacock and the jewel of the serpent: yathā śikhā mayūrāṇāṃ nāgānāṃ maṇayo yathā, “as the crests of peacocks, as the jewels of serpents”, so this science stands at the head of the Vedāṅgas. The Ārca recension names jyotiṣa as the subject of the second line, the Yājuṣa gaṇita (“calculation”). The verse is often quoted as praise of mathematics in general; in its context it praises the calendrical science.

    The text was not much used once the siddhāntas had replaced its calendar, and its transmission suffered. A commentary by Somākara on the Yājuṣa recension survives and is of help, but it is late and often uncertain. Modern study began in the nineteenth century, with the work of Albrecht Weber, George Thibaut and, in India, Bāl Gaṅgādhar Tilak and Śaṅkara Bālakṛṣṇa Dīkṣit, and many verses were explained only in the twentieth century. Sastry’s translation and notes (Sastry 1985) represent the results of this long effort, but some verses remain obscure, and interpretations of the text’s rules still differ. Readers should therefore treat any precise statement about the text’s content, including the numbers quoted in this chapter, as resting on interpretation. The broad structure of the calendar (the five-year cycle, the 366-day year, the 62 synodic and 67 sidereal months, the tithi, the solstices in Śraviṣṭhā and Āśleṣā) is, however, not in doubt, and it is confirmed by the closely similar scheme of the Jain canonical texts discussed below.

    3.4.2 The five-year yuga¶

    The calendar of the Vedāṅga Jyotiṣa is built on a cycle of five years, called a yuga. (This has nothing to do with the immense yugas of the Purāṇas and siddhāntas, discussed in Chapter 14.) The five years have the names saṃvatsara, parivatsara, idāvatsara, anuvatsara and idvatsara. The cycle begins when the Sun and the Moon are together at the beginning of the asterism Śraviṣṭhā (later called Dhaniṣṭhā) at the winter solstice, at the start of the bright half of the month Māgha. Within the cycle, the text assumes:

    \[\begin{align*} 1 \text{ yuga} &= 5 \text{ years} = 1830 \text{ civil days} \\ &= 62 \text{ synodic months} = 1860 \text{ tithis} \\ &= 67 \text{ sidereal months (revolutions of the Moon through the asterisms).} \end{align*}\]

    The year therefore has 366 days, and two intercalary months are needed in each cycle to keep the 62 lunar months in step with the five solar years. The tithi, one-thirtieth of a synodic month, appears here for the first time; it became the basic unit of the later Indian calendar (Chapter 13).

    Table 3.2. Periods implied by the five-year yuga of the Vedāṅga Jyotiṣa, compared with modern mean values.
    Period Vedāṅga Jyotiṣa Modern value
    Year (days) 366 365.2422 (tropical); 365.2564 (sidereal)
    Synodic month (days) \(1830/62 = 29.516\) 29.5306
    Sidereal month (days) \(1830/67 = 27.313\) 27.3217
    Days per tithi \(1830/1860 = 0.984\) 0.9843

    The month lengths are reasonable, but the year of 366 days is about three-quarters of a day too long. Over a single five-year cycle the calendar would drift by nearly four days against the Sun, and over a few cycles by a fortnight or more. The calendar can only have worked if it was periodically reset by observation of the Sun and Moon, and the text does not tell us how this was done. The Paitāmaha system summarised by Varāhamihira in the sixth century CE still uses this scheme (Chapter 5), but the siddhāntas replaced it.

    3.4.3 Other contents¶

    Besides the cycle itself, the Vedāṅga Jyotiṣa gives rules for finding the nakṣatra of the Moon and the Sun at any new or full moon of the cycle, for finding the tithi, for converting between units, and for the variation of the length of daylight. It states that daylight increases by six muhūrtas between the winter and the summer solstices, so that the longest day is 18 muhūrtas and the shortest 12 (a muhūrta is one-thirtieth of a day, 48 minutes). This gives a ratio of 3:2 between the longest and shortest day. It also describes a water clock that measures the nāḍikā, one-sixtieth of the day (24 minutes). The text places the summer solstice at the middle of the asterism Āśleṣā, opposite the winter solstice at the beginning of Śraviṣṭhā.

    3.4.4 A worked example: the length of daylight¶

    The rule for daylight shows how the Vedāṅga Jyotiṣa treats a continuously varying quantity. Daylight grows by 6 muhūrtas in the half-year of 183 days from the winter to the summer solstice, so it grows uniformly by \(6/183 = 2/61\) of a muhūrta each day. If \(n\) days have passed since the winter solstice (with \(n \le 183\)), the length of daylight is

    \[D(n) = 12 + \frac{2n}{61} \ \text{muhūrtas},\tag{3.4}\]

    and in the other half of the year it decreases at the same rate. Thus 61 days after the winter solstice daylight is 14 muhūrtas (11 hours 12 minutes), and at the equinox, about 91 days after the solstice, it is 15 muhūrtas, exactly half the day. The real variation is not linear but follows a curve close to a sine; a linear (“zigzag”) scheme of this kind is characteristic of early Babylonian astronomy as well, and its presence in the Vedāṅga Jyotiṣa is one of the parallels discussed below. It is nevertheless a reasonable practical rule, and the error it makes in the length of the day is never more than a fraction of a muhūrta.

    3.4.5 The yuga in practice¶

    On the usual interpretation, the two intercalary months of the cycle were placed at regular intervals, one in the middle of the cycle and one at its end. Each month began at the new or full moon computed from the cycle, and the nakṣatra of the Moon at each of these moments could be read from the rules of the text. For the ritualist the result was a table of the nakṣatras and tithis at which the new- and full-moon offerings and the seasonal rites would fall over five years. The scheme was easy to remember and to use, and its main weakness, the 366-day year, would have been corrected in practice by watching the Sun’s turning points at the solstices and the full moons of the principal months.

    3.5 The Date of the Vedāṅga Jyotiṣa¶

    3.5.1 The argument from the solstice¶

    The position of the solstices among the stars changes slowly because of the precession of the equinoxes, by about one degree in seventy-two years. If the statement that the winter solstice lay at the beginning of Śraviṣṭhā was an observation, it can in principle be dated.

    The calculation runs as follows. In the later system of twenty-seven equal nakṣatras counted from Aśvinī, Śraviṣṭhā begins at sidereal longitude \(293^\circ 20'\). Today the winter solstice (tropical longitude \(270^\circ\)) lies near sidereal longitude \(246^\circ\), using the official Indian ayanāṃśa of about \(24^\circ\). The solstice has thus moved back by about \(47^\circ\) since the time of the observation, which at the modern rate of precession (\(50.3”\) a year) corresponds to about 3,400 years, that is, to a date around 1400 BCE. Variants of the calculation, using the junction star of Śraviṣṭhā (usually identified as \(\beta\) Delphini) instead of the equal division, or other assumptions, give dates between about 1400 and 1150 BCE. Sastry and Sarma took this as the date of the text (Sastry 1985). It is important to keep two dates apart: the astronomical epoch implied by the solstice statement (c. 1400–1200 BCE on these calculations) and the date of composition of the text as we have it, which most historians of astronomy now place much later, around 400 BCE or later (Pingree 1973; Plofker 2009).

    3.5.2 Why the date is disputed¶

    Three difficulties make this result much less secure than it looks (Plofker 2009).

    1. Precision. “At the beginning of Śraviṣṭhā” is a coarse statement. It may refer to a junction star or to the edge of an equal division of the ecliptic that the text does not define, and an uncertainty of a few degrees in the position corresponds to centuries. An error of one whole nakṣatra (\(13^\circ 20'\)) would shift the date by nearly a thousand years.

    2. Tradition. A solstice position, once stated, could be repeated for centuries after it ceased to be true. Indian astronomers did exactly this with other parameters. An old observation does not date the text that preserves it.

    3. Other evidence. The language and style of the verses look later than those of the late Vedic prose texts, and the text has features with close parallels in Mesopotamian astronomy (below). On these grounds Pingree argued that the text, whatever the age of its solstice statement, was composed in the second half of the first millennium BCE (around the fifth or fourth century BCE, or later), perhaps under Achaemenid influence (Pingree 1973).

    Scholarly Note: Mesopotamian parallels

    Pingree pointed out that the 3:2 ratio of longest to shortest daylight, the use of a water clock to measure it, a linear (“zigzag”) variation of day length through the year, and the schematic 360-day year are all found in the Mesopotamian astronomical compendium MUL.APIN (early first millennium BCE) (Pingree 1973; Hunger and Pingree 1999). A 3:2 ratio is correct for a latitude of about \(35^\circ\) north, which fits Mesopotamia or the far north-west of the subcontinent, not the Gangetic plain. He concluded that these elements reached India from Mesopotamia, probably while the north-west was part of the Achaemenid empire (late sixth to fourth centuries BCE). Others have replied that a 3:2 ratio could be a simple schematic value reached independently, that north-western India is a possible place of composition, and that the core of the five-year cycle is native to the Vedic ritual calendar. Many historians now accept some Mesopotamian input while recognising that the cycle and its Vedic setting are Indian (Plofker 2009). The question remains open.

    3.5.3 Earlier dates from Vedic astronomy¶

    Since the late nineteenth century some writers have used astronomical statements in Vedic texts to argue for much earlier dates. B. G. Tilak argued that passages linking the year’s beginning to Mṛgaśiras pointed to a vernal equinox in that asterism around 4000 BCE (Tilak 1893); others have used the statement of the Śatapatha Brāhmaṇa (2.1.2.3) that the Kṛttikās “do not swerve from the eastern direction”, and the order of nakṣatra lists beginning with Kṛttikā, to argue that the Pleiades lay at the equinox, around the third millennium BCE. More recently Kak (1995) has argued that the dimensions of the Vedic fire altars and the structure of the Vedic texts encode detailed astronomical knowledge.

    These arguments rest on interpretations that are uncertain: whether a statement records an observation at all, whether it is precise enough to date, whether the asterism meant is the one assumed, and whether ritual statements about the “beginning of the year” refer to the equinox. They also conflict with the linguistic, textual and archaeological evidence on which the accepted chronology of the Vedic corpus rests. They are not accepted by most historians of astronomy or by Vedic philologists. The statement about the Kṛttikās, for instance, may only mean that they rise roughly in the east, which was true for a long period. Such dates are best treated as hypotheses about particular passages, not as a chronology of the texts.

    3.6 Jain Astronomy¶

    3.6.1 Texts and framework¶

    The canonical texts of the Śvetāmbara Jains include works devoted to the heavens: the Sūryaprajñapti (Prakrit Sūriyapaṇṇatti) and Candraprajñapti (“Exposition of the Sun”, “of the Moon”), and the Jambūdvīpaprajñapti, together with the later Jyotiṣkaraṇḍaka. Their contents are older than their final redaction, which belongs with the fixing of the canon at Valabhī in the fifth century CE, but how much older is uncertain (Pingree 1981; Plofker 2009). George Thibaut’s study of the Sūryaprajñapti (Thibaut 1880) first drew attention to its close agreement with the Vedāṅga Jyotiṣa.

    Jain astronomy is set within Jain cosmology. The earth is a flat disc of concentric continents and oceans; at the centre of the innermost continent, Jambūdvīpa, stands Mount Meru, around which the heavenly bodies circle at a fixed height. Night falls when the Sun passes behind Meru. Since a single sun circling Meru could not produce the observed alternation of day and night over the whole continent, the texts assume two suns and two moons, which cross the sky on alternate days, and accordingly two sets of twenty-eight nakṣatras. The changing seasons are explained by the Sun’s moving between inner and outer circular paths around Meru. The cosmography as a whole is described in Volume 24, Samanvaya (Chapter 3), and Volume 4, Buddha–Jina Prabhā; here the concern is the astronomy it carries.

    3.6.2 The paths of the Sun and the length of daylight¶

    The texts describe the Sun’s seasonal movement with arithmetical precision. Each sun moves on one of 184 circular paths (maṇḍalas) around Meru, keeping to one path for each day. Moving outward from the innermost path to the outermost takes 183 days, and moving back another 183, so the year has 366 days, as in the Vedāṅga Jyotiṣa. Jambūdvīpa is 100,000 yojanas across, and the innermost path lies 180 yojanas inside its rim, so that the diameter of that path is \(100{,}000 - 2 \times 180 = 99{,}640\) yojanas. According to the texts the zone of the paths is \(510\tfrac{48}{61}\) yojanas wide, the fraction being the breadth of the Sun’s disc, so that the outermost path has a diameter of \(99{,}640 + 2 \times 510 = 100{,}660\) yojanas.

    On the innermost path, at the summer solstice, the day lasts 18 muhūrtas and the night 12; on the outermost path the proportions are reversed. From one path to the next the day changes by \(\tfrac{2}{61}\) of a muhūrta, and the 183 steps between the extremes make up exactly the six muhūrtas of difference (\(183 \times \tfrac{2}{61} = 6\)). The circumferences of the paths are computed with \(\pi = \sqrt{10}\), the value the Jain texts use throughout (Volume 4, Chapter 10, and Volume 11): the innermost path comes to \(99{,}640 \times \sqrt{10} \approx 315{,}089\) yojanas. Each sun needs two days, or 60 muhūrtas, to go once around Meru, so on that path it moves \(315{,}089 / 60 = 5251\tfrac{29}{60}\) yojanas in a muhūrta. Multiplying this speed by half the length of the longest day, 9 muhūrtas, gives \(47{,}263\tfrac{21}{60}\) yojanas, the distance at which, on that day, the rising Sun first becomes visible to an observer in Bharata, the southern region of Jambūdvīpa in which India lies. The geometry is not that of the siddhāntas, but every number follows by arithmetic from a few stated assumptions. That internal consistency, rather than agreement with measurement, is what the Jain treatment of the heavens aims at.

    3.6.3 Months, years and the five-year yuga¶

    Within this framework the calendar is close to that of the Vedāṅga Jyotiṣa: a five-year yuga of 1830 days, with 62 synodic and 67 sidereal months, the same 3:2 ratio of longest to shortest day, and similar rules for the nakṣatras of the Moon. The Jain texts are more systematic than the Vedāṅga Jyotiṣa in naming the units they use. They distinguish five kinds of month, each a fraction of the yuga:

    • the sidereal month (nakṣatra), \(1830/67 = 27\tfrac{21}{67}\) days;

    • the synodic month (cāndra), \(1830/62 = 29\tfrac{32}{62}\) days;

    • the civil or “seasonal” month (ṛtu, also karma), 30 days;

    • the solar month (āditya or sūrya), \(1830/60 = 30\tfrac{1}{2}\) days;

    • the “increased” month (abhivardhita), \(31\tfrac{121}{124}\) days, one-twelfth of a year that contains thirteen synodic months.

    Twelve of each make a year of \(327\tfrac{51}{67}\), \(354\tfrac{12}{62}\), 360, 366 and \(383\tfrac{44}{62}\) days respectively. The yuga consists of three ordinary lunar years and two “increased” years, the third and the fifth, and the arithmetic closes exactly: \(3 \times 354\tfrac{12}{62} + 2 \times 383\tfrac{44}{62} = 1830\) days, which is also five solar years of 366 days, sixty-one civil months and sixty solar months. The texts begin the year and the yuga with the month Śrāvaṇa, near the summer solstice, where the Vedāṅga Jyotiṣa begins with Māgha at the winter solstice, and they begin their list of nakṣatras with Abhijit rather than Kṛttikā.

    3.6.4 The Moon among the nakṣatras¶

    The Jain texts do not treat the nakṣatras as equal. They measure the time for which the Moon is “joined” to each and assign 15 muhūrtas to six of them, 45 to six others, 30 to the remaining fifteen, and \(9\tfrac{27}{67}\) to Abhijit (Table 3.3). The total is \(6 \times 15 + 15 \times 30 + 6 \times 45 + 9\tfrac{27}{67} = 819\tfrac{27}{67}\) muhūrtas, which is exactly the sidereal month of \(27\tfrac{21}{67}\) days. The Sun’s stay with each nakṣatra is obtained by multiplying by \(\tfrac{67}{5}\), the ratio of the Sun’s year of 366 days to the Moon’s sidereal month, and the Sun’s stays then add up to 366 days. The same unequal division is found in other early jyotiṣa texts and survives in some later Brahmanical works; in the Jain canon it serves the computation of the calendar, not astrology alone.

    3.6.5 Commentaries and later use¶

    The canonical texts were explained in learned commentaries, above all those of Malayagiri in the twelfth century, which work through their computations (Chapter 9). They were not, however, used to compute almanacs once the siddhāntas had spread. Jain communities, like their neighbours, followed the regional luni-solar almanacs, and Jain scholars who wrote on astronomy wrote mostly in the siddhānta tradition. The Jain religious year is accordingly a luni-solar one of the common Indian type: Mahāvīra’s birth is celebrated on the thirteenth tithi of the bright half of Caitra, the festival of Paryuṣaṇa falls in Bhādrapada, and Dīpāvalī commemorates Mahāvīra’s liberation. The Vīra Nirvāṇa era, whose year begins on the day after Dīpāvalī, counts from the traditional date of that event, 527 BCE; most historians place Mahāvīra later, as an older contemporary of the Buddha.

    3.7 Buddhist Sources¶

    3.7.1 The Śārdūlakarṇāvadāna¶

    The fullest early Buddhist source for astronomy is the Śārdūlakarṇāvadāna, one of the stories collected in the Divyāvadāna. It was translated into Chinese in the third century CE, and two early Chinese versions survive, so it existed in some form by then (Mukhopadhyaya 1954). Its frame story is about caste. Ānanda accepts water at a well from Prakṛti, a girl of the outcaste Mātaṅga community, who falls in love with him. To explain her attachment, the Buddha tells of a former life in which Triśaṅku, king of the Mātaṅgas, asked the proud brahmin Puṣkarasārin to give his daughter in marriage to Triśaṅku’s son Śārdūlakarṇa. When the brahmin refuses with contempt, Triśaṅku shows that he knows the Veda and the brahmins’ own sciences better than Puṣkarasārin, and argues that birth creates no natural distinctions among human beings. Most of the astronomy is placed in Triśaṅku’s mouth as part of that demonstration. A Buddhist text thus uses mastery of jyotiṣa to undermine the claim that such knowledge was the birthright of one community.

    The astronomical part lists the twenty-eight nakṣatras beginning with Kṛttikā, with the number of their stars, their shapes, their presiding deities and clans (gotra), and the foods and undertakings suited to each. It describes the lengths of day and night through the year in muhūrtas and the shadow of a gnomon; defines measures of length, weight and time; and gives prognostications from the Moon’s position among the nakṣatras, from earthquakes, and for persons born under each asterism. Its calendar is of the same pre-siddhānta type as that of the Vedāṅga Jyotiṣa and the Jain texts, and Pingree used it as one of the witnesses for Mesopotamian elements in early Indian astronomy (Pingree 1973). The text matters for two reasons. It dates material of the jyotiṣa type independently of the Brahmanical tradition, and it shows that knowledge of the nakṣatras was a shared possession of Brahmins, Buddhists and others, which could be claimed and contested. Through its Chinese translations it also began the eastward travel of Indian astral lore (Chapter 16).

    3.7.2 The monastic calendar¶

    Buddhist monastic life was regulated by the lunar calendar. The community assembled for the uposatha (Sanskrit upavasatha) at new and full moon to recite the monastic rules, and lay followers kept the uposatha days, including the eighth day of each fortnight, by taking additional precepts. Because a fortnight could have fourteen or fifteen days, depending on where a tithi was omitted, the monks had to know which. The Pali Vinaya relates that lay followers asked monks which day of the fortnight it was and that the monks were then permitted to learn the reckoning of the fortnight. The Theravāda tradition later fixed a simple rule: in each season of four months the third and seventh fortnights have fourteen days and the others fifteen, giving a season of 118 days and a year of 354 days, close to twelve synodic months (354.4 days).

    The rains retreat (Pali vassa, Sanskrit varṣā) of three months begins on the day after the full moon of Āṣāḍha (Pali Āsāḷha) and ends with the pavāraṇā ceremony, after which the kaṭhina robes are given. The Theravāda calendars of Sri Lanka, Myanmar, Thailand, Laos and Cambodia remain luni-solar calendars of the Indian type: when an intercalary month is needed it is usually a second Āsāḷha, so that the retreat stays in the rainy season (Chapter 16). Their Buddhist era counts from a traditional date of the Buddha’s final passing, 544/543 BCE, about a century and a half earlier than the date most historians now prefer (c. 400 BCE).

    3.8 Other Early Sources¶

    Several other texts bridge the gap between the Vedāṅga and the siddhāntas. The Gārgīya Jyotiṣa (or Vṛddhagārgīyasaṃhitā), probably from around the beginning of the Common Era, is a large compilation of omens, nakṣatra lore and calendrical rules, whose contents and date have been re-examined by Geslani et al. (2017). The Pariśiṣṭas (supplements) of the Atharvaveda preserve omen and nakṣatra texts. The Arthaśāstra uses a calendar of the same general type (Section 3.9). With the Jain and Buddhist texts just described, they show the five-year calendar and its associated lore spread across Brahmanical, Buddhist and Jain literature before the arrival of Greek astronomy (Pingree 1981).

    3.9 Time-Reckoning in the Early State¶

    The Arthaśāstra, the treatise on statecraft attributed by tradition to Kauṭilya (its present form is composite, c. 50 BCE–300 CE), shows the calendar in administrative use. Its chapter on the measurement of space and time defines units of time from the smallest (tuṭa, lava, nimeṣa) up to the nālikā and muhūrta, describes a water clock, and gives the lengths of a gnomon’s shadow at different times of day. It describes a year of twelve months with intercalation and speaks of different kinds of month (solar, lunar, sidereal and others) used for different purposes, such as the payment of servants and the reckoning of revenue. The calendar is of the same pre-siddhānta type as that of the Vedāṅga Jyotiṣa, adapted to the needs of an administration. Like the Vedic texts, the Arthaśāstra also expects the king to employ astrologers and to heed omens, while warning, in a well-known verse, against excessive reliance on the stars (Chapter 15).

    3.10 Evidence of Observation¶

    How much observation lay behind the Vedic and Vedāṅga calendar? The texts do not record observations in the way that the Babylonian Diaries do, but they presuppose them. The ritual required knowing when the Sun reached its northern and southern turning points, which could be found by watching the sunrise point on the horizon or the noon shadow of a gnomon; the full and new moons and the asterisms near the Moon were watched night by night; and the length of daylight was measured with water clocks. Ōhashi (1993) collected the textual evidence for these practices and concluded that systematic, if simple, observation was part of Vedic ritual life; its precision, however, was limited.

    3.11 Assessment¶

    The Vedic and Vedāṅga period produced a functioning luni-solar ritual calendar based on the Sun’s annual course and the Moon’s course among the nakṣatras; the concept of the tithi; a scheme of intercalation; a schematic account of the variation of daylight; the use of the gnomon and the water clock; and a set of named asterisms that remained the frame of Indian lunar astronomy. It did not yet have a theory of the planets, a geometrical model of the sky, or a way of predicting eclipses. Those came with the siddhāntas, after contact with Greek astronomy, as the next chapters describe.

    Further Reading¶
