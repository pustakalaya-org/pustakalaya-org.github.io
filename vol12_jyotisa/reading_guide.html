    Appendix C: A Guide to Further Reading¶

    This guide suggests where a reader might go next. All the works mentioned are listed in the select bibliography.

    General introductions¶

    Kim Plofker’s Mathematics in India (Plofker 2009) is the best modern introduction to Indian mathematical astronomy as well as mathematics; it explains the technical content of the texts clearly and places them in their historical setting. Michio Yano’s short chapter on calendar, astrology and astronomy (Yano 2003) is an excellent first orientation. The older survey edited by Bose et al. (1971) and the volume edited by Sen and Shukla (1985) are useful for their range, though some of their judgements have been revised. Subbarayappa and Sarma (1985) give a large selection of translated passages arranged by topic, and are the best way to read the texts themselves without Sanskrit.

    Reference works¶

    David Pingree’s Jyotiḥśāstra (Pingree 1981) is the standard survey of the literature, author by author and genre by genre, and his long article in the Dictionary of Scientific Biography (Pingree 1978a) gives the parameters and models of the schools. His Census (Pingree 1970–1994) lists authors and manuscripts. For the calendar and the conversion of dates, Sewell and Dikshit (1896) remains indispensable. Dīkṣit’s history (Dikshit 1969–1981) is still worth reading for its knowledge of the traditional literature.

    The texts in translation¶

    The Vedāṅga Jyotiṣa (Sastry 1985), the Āryabhaṭīya (Āryabhaṭa 1976), the Pañcasiddhāntikā (Neugebauer and Pingree 1970–1971), the Sūryasiddhānta (Burgess 1860), Bhāskara II’s Golādhyāya (Bhāskara II 1861), the Tantrasaṅgraha (Ramasubramanian and Sriram 2011) and the Yuktibhāṣā (Sarma et al. 2008) are available in English translations with commentary. The Āryabhaṭīya also has the older translation of Āryabhaṭa (1930), and the Pañcasiddhāntikā that of Varāhamihira (1993). K. S. Shukla edited and translated Bhāskara I’s Mahābhāskarīya and Laghubhāskarīya (Bhāskara I 1960, 1963) and the Vaṭeśvarasiddhānta (Vaṭeśvara 1985–1986); Bina Chatterjee did the same for Lalla’s Śiṣyadhīvṛddhida (Lalla 1981). For the later tradition there are K. V. Sarma’s edition of the Tantrasaṅgraha with its Sanskrit commentaries (Nīlakaṇṭha Somayājī 1977), the Karaṇapaddhati of Pai et al. (2018) and Knudsen’s study of Jñānarāja’s Siddhāntasundara (Knudsen 2014). Varāhamihira’s Bṛhatsaṃhitā is translated by Varāhamihira (1981–1982), and the Yavanajātaka by Pingree (1978b). Al-Bīrūnī’s account of Indian astronomy is in al-Bīrūnī (1888).

    Special topics¶

    On the origins of siddhānta astronomy and the Greek and Mesopotamian connections, see Pingree (1973b), Pingree (1976), Toomer (1973) and, for a re-examination of the Yavanajātaka, Mak (2013); on Garga and the earliest astral literature, Geslani et al. (2017). On the observational basis of the parameters, Billard (1971). On eclipses, Montelle (2011); on tables, Montelle and Plofker (2018), with Pingree’s catalogues (Pingree 1968, 1973a); on the handbooks, Sengupta’s Khaṇḍakhādyaka (Sengupta 1934). For Jain astronomy the classic study is Thibaut (1880); for the Buddhist Śārdūlakarṇāvadāna, the edition of Mukhopadhyaya (1954); for the Kālacakra calendar and its Tibetan afterlife, Henning (2007). On instruments, Ōhashi (1994) and Sarma (2008); on Jai Singh, Sharma (1995). On the Kerala school, Sarma (1972), Ramasubramanian et al. (1994) and Divakaran (2018). On cosmology and the reasons astronomers gave for their methods, Minkowski (2002, 2004). On astrology and omens, Pingree (1997). On the modern period, Kochhar and Narlikar (1995).

    Wider contexts¶

    For Babylonian and Greek astronomy, Neugebauer (1975) and Hunger and Pingree (1999); for a broad and sympathetic account of non-European mathematics, Joseph (2011), to be read alongside the more cautious judgements of Plofker (2009) on questions of transmission.
