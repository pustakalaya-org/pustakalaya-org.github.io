    Chapter 15: Advanced Tantric Practices¶

    15.1 Introduction to Advanced Sadhana¶

    The advanced practices of Tantra represent the culmination of years of preparatory discipline, initiation, and progressive mastery. These practices are traditionally transmitted only from guru to qualified disciple, as they involve powerful transformative energies that can be dangerous without proper guidance. This chapter provides an academic overview of what the traditions describe. It is not a set of instructions: several of the practices named below can injure the body or the mind, and none should be attempted from a book.

    Prerequisites for advanced practice:

    • Stable meditation practice (minimum several years)

    • Formal diksha (initiation) from a qualified guru

    • Mastery of foundational practices (mantra, pranayama, asana)

    • Ethical stability and mental equilibrium

    • Strong physical health and balanced nervous system

    • Surrender to the guru-shishya relationship

    15.2 Shava Sadhana (Cremation Ground Practice)¶

    15.2.1 The Shmashana as Sacred Space¶

    The cremation ground (shmashana) holds a unique position in tantric cosmology. Where most traditions shun the cremation ground as impure, Tantra recognizes it as the ultimate place of transformation — where the illusion of permanence is most vividly revealed.

    15.2.2 Traditional Description¶

    • Accounts describe the practitioner sitting at or near a cremation site, typically at midnight on amavasya (new moon)

    • The space is said to be consecrated with mantras and protective circles

    • Meditation focuses on the transience of the body, the illusory nature of form

    • In the most extreme forms described in the texts and in legend, the practitioner sits upon a corpse (shava); this is unlawful and dangerous today, and in most living lineages it is understood symbolically

    • The stated purpose is to transcend the deepest fears — fear of death, decay, and the unknown

    • The practice is associated primarily with the Aghora tradition and Kali worship

    15.2.3 Symbolism¶

    The cremation ground represents the dissolution phase of consciousness where all constructs of identity burn away, revealing the pure awareness that remains when everything else is consumed.

    15.3 Advanced Kundalini Practices¶

    15.3.1 Preparation¶

    The texts say that, before any Kundalini practice, the practitioner should:

    • Purify the nadis through extensive Nadi Shodhana pranayama

    • Strengthen the physical body through asana and diet

    • Stabilize the mind through meditation and mantra

    • Receive specific Kundalini diksha from the guru

    15.3.2 Methods Described in the Texts¶

    1. Pranayama-based: Bhastrika with bandhas, Moorchha Pranayama, specific breath retention patterns

    2. Mantra-based: Intensive repetition of Kundalini-specific bija mantras at each chakra

    3. Visualization-based: Detailed inner visualization of serpent energy ascending

    4. Guru-based (Shaktipat): Direct energy transmission from master to disciple

    5. Combined: Integration of all methods in systematic practice

    15.3.3 The Three Granthis¶

    The rising Kundalini must pierce three “knots” (granthis):

    • Brahma Granthi (at Muladhara): Knot of attachment to physical existence, material security, and bodily identity

    • Vishnu Granthi (at Anahata): Knot of emotional attachment, personal relationships, and compassion that binds

    • Rudra Granthi (at Ajna): Knot of attachment to psychic powers, visions, and the subtle ego of spiritual attainment

    15.3.4 Dangers and Safeguards¶

    • Texts and modern accounts report physical symptoms: intense heat, pain, involuntary movements

    • Psychological disruption: anxiety, depression, psychotic-like episodes

    • “Kundalini syndrome”: When energy rises without proper channel preparation

    • Safeguards: Guru guidance, gradual practice, grounding techniques, proper diet, physical exercise, and community support; qualified medical or mental-health care for any distress. Forceful breathing and long retention of breath are unsafe for people with heart disease, high blood pressure, epilepsy, eye disease or pregnancy, and for anyone who has fainted or felt unwell during them

    15.4 Advanced Mudras¶

    15.4.1 Khechari Mudra¶

    A radical mudra described in the Hatha Yoga Pradipika and related texts. This book reports what the texts say and does not describe the method:

    • The texts describe a gradual lengthening of the tongue, with cutting of the frenulum, over many months, so that the tongue can be turned back into the nasal passage

    • The tongue is then said to reach the “nectar point” (bindu visarga) at the soft palate

    • The texts claim that a flow of amrita (nectar) follows, transforming the practitioner’s physiology and enabling very long breath retention; there is no clinical evidence for these claims

    • Cutting the tongue carries the risk of bleeding, infection and damage to speech and swallowing; physicians and responsible teachers do not recommend it

    • Many teachers describe a modified form without cutting, in which the tongue is simply turned back as far as is comfortable

    15.4.2 Shambhavi Mudra¶

    • Gazing at the point between the eyebrows with eyes half-open

    • Awareness simultaneously internal and external

    • Creates the state of unmani (beyond-mind)

    • Gateway to natural samadhi

    • Praised in the Hatha Yoga Pradipika and other texts

    15.4.3 Yoni Mudra (Shanmukhi Mudra)¶

    • Closing the sense doors with the fingers, as the texts describe it

    • Creates complete sensory withdrawal (pratyahara)

    • Facilitates hearing of Anahata Nada (unstruck sound)

    • Progression through 10 stages of inner sound

    15.5 Advanced Pranayama¶

    15.5.1 Bhastrika (Bellows Breath)¶

    A rapid, forceful breathing described in the texts, combined with bandhas (locks):

    • The texts say it generates intense internal heat (tapas), purifies the nadis rapidly and activates the Manipura chakra and digestive fire

    • Rapid breathing can cause hyperventilation, dizziness and fainting; it is unsafe in heart disease, high blood pressure, epilepsy, pregnancy and some other conditions

    • Modern teachers introduce such practices only gradually and under supervision

    15.5.2 Kapalabhati (Skull-Shining Breath)¶

    Rapid exhalations with passive inhalations:

    • In the texts it is a cleansing practice (one of the shatkarma) of the head region; the claim that it “cleanses the brain” has no medical basis

    • It is described as preparatory to advanced pranayama

    • The cautions given above for forceful breathing apply

    15.5.3 Moorchha Pranayama (Swooning Breath)¶

    • The texts describe extended retention with the chin lock (Jalandhara Bandha), producing a state close to fainting, in which the mind is said to enter a void (shunya) state

    • Deliberately inducing near-unconsciousness is risky; this book does not describe the method

    15.5.4 Kevala Kumbhaka (Spontaneous Retention)¶

    • The ultimate pranayama — breath stops naturally without effort

    • Indicates advanced meditative absorption

    • Cannot be practiced — only attained through maturation of practice

    • Associated with Samadhi and Kundalini rising through Sushumna

    15.6 Nada Yoga (Yoga of Sound)¶

    15.6.1 Anahata Nada (Unstruck Sound)¶

    • Sound that arises without any physical cause

    • Heard in deep meditation, especially with Shanmukhi Mudra

    • Progresses through 10 stages: buzzing, flute, thunder, drum, bell, vina, etc.

    • Final stage: pure silence beyond all sound

    15.6.2 Traditional Description¶

    1. Begin with external sound meditation (singing bowl, mantra)

    2. Progress to listening for inner sounds

    3. Focus on the most subtle sound heard

    4. Allow awareness to merge with the sound

    5. Sound dissolves into silence — the practitioner rests in pure awareness

    15.7 Tattwa Shuddhi (Element Purification)¶

    An advanced inner practice systematically dissolving the five elements:

    1. Earth dissolves into Water: Visualize the body becoming fluid, mantra LAM dissolves into VAM

    2. Water dissolves into Fire: Fluid body evaporates, VAM into RAM

    3. Fire dissolves into Air: Flames dissipate into wind, RAM into YAM

    4. Air dissolves into Space: Wind dissolves into emptiness, YAM into HAM

    5. Space dissolves into Mind: Emptiness collapses into consciousness, HAM into OM

    6. Reconstruction: Elements rebuilt in pure form, body reconstituted as divine

    This practice forms the foundation of Tantric Bhuta Shuddhi and is performed before all major tantric rituals.

    15.8 Trataka on Yantra¶

    15.8.1 Traditional Description¶

    • Steady, unblinking gaze at a yantra (typically Sri Yantra); modern teachers advise short sessions, and caution against it for people with eye disease

    • Traditional accounts continue the external gazing until tears flow

    • Close eyes and visualize the yantra internally

    • Progress to holding the complete yantra in mind’s eye

    • Advanced: Enter the yantra, moving through its layers from Bhupura to Bindu

    • Ultimate: Rest in the Bindu point — pure consciousness

    15.9 Ethical Boundaries and the Role of the Guru¶

    15.9.1 Why the Guru is Essential¶

    • Advanced practices manipulate powerful subtle energies

    • Without guidance, practitioners may develop physical or psychological disorders

    • The guru monitors progress, adjusts practices, and provides corrections

    • Transmission of living energy (Shaktipat) is crucial for authentic progress

    • The guru lineage (parampara) ensures continuity of authentic knowledge

    15.9.2 Ethical Framework¶

    • All practices serve liberation (moksha), not ego aggrandizement

    • Siddhis (supernatural powers) that arise must be surrendered, not exploited

    • Harm to others through tantric powers creates severe karmic consequences

    • The practitioner must maintain humility, compassion, and truthfulness

    • “Power without wisdom is dangerous; wisdom without compassion is incomplete”
