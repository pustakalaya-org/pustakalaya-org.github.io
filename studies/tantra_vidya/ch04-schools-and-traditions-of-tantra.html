    Chapter 4: Schools and Traditions of Tantra¶

    As rivers flowing from different mountains ultimately merge in the ocean, so the various Tantric paths, though differing in method, converge upon the single ocean of supreme consciousness.

    The Tantric tradition is not a monolithic entity but a rich tapestry of diverse schools, lineages, and approaches, each with its own distinctive philosophical emphasis, ritual methodology, and lineage of transmission. While united by common foundational principles—the centrality of Shakti, the use of mantra and ritual, the importance of initiation—these schools have developed remarkably varied expressions of the Tantric impulse. This chapter surveys the major schools and traditions across Hindu, Buddhist, and Jain contexts.

    4.1 Kashmir Shaivism (Trika)¶

    4.1.1 Overview and History¶

    The Trika system of Kashmir Shaivism represents the philosophical pinnacle of the Tantric tradition. The name “Trika” (“threefold”) refers to the three fundamental realities: Shiva, Shakti, and Nara (the individual soul)—or alternatively to the three goddesses Para, Parapara, and Apara who represent the supreme, intermediate, and lower aspects of Shakti.

    Flourishing primarily between the 8th and 12th centuries CE in the Kashmir valley, this tradition produced some of the most sophisticated philosophical and spiritual literature in the history of world thought.

    4.1.2 Key Texts¶

    • Shiva Sutras: 77 aphorisms revealed to Vasugupta, considered the foundational scripture

    • Spanda Karikas: Exposition of the vibration doctrine

    • Pratyabhijnahridayam: “Heart of Recognition” by Kshemaraja

    • Tantraloka: Abhinavagupta’s encyclopedic masterwork in 37 chapters

    • Vijnana Bhairava Tantra: 112 meditation techniques

    • Netra Tantra: Key ritual text focused on Amriteshvara

    • Svacchanda Tantra: Major text on the worship of Svacchanda Bhairava

    4.1.3 Key Masters¶

    Vasugupta (9th–early 10th century CE):
    Receiver of the Shiva Sutras; founder of the tradition
    Somananda (fl. c. 900–950 CE):
    Author of Shiva Dristi; founder of Pratyabhijna school
    Utpaladeva (fl. c. 925–975 CE):
    Philosopher and devotional poet
    Abhinavagupta (fl. c. 975–1025 CE):
    The supreme master; synthesizer of all streams
    Kshemaraja (fl. 11th century CE):
    Abhinavagupta’s chief disciple; gifted expositor

    4.1.4 Distinctive Practices¶

    1. Shambhavopaya: The supreme means—direct recognition through the Guru’s grace or spontaneous insight, requiring no technique

    2. Shaktopaya: The energetic means—using contemplation, mantra, and meditation on the nature of consciousness

    3. Anavopaya: The individual means—employing breath control, concentration on specific points, and ritual

    4. The 112 Dharanas: The meditation techniques of the Vijnana Bhairava Tantra

    4.2 Kaula Tradition¶

    4.2.1 Origins and Philosophy¶

    The Kaula (or Kula) tradition is one of the most important and influential streams within Tantra. The word Kula means “family” or “totality” and refers to the “family” of Shakti—the unified field of consciousness and energy that constitutes reality.

    The Kaula tradition traces its origins to Matsyendranath (Macchendranath), who is said to have received the Kaula teachings directly from Shiva. An early Kaula text is the Kaulajnananirnaya, attributed to Matsyendranath.

    4.2.2 Core Principles¶

    • The body as the primary locus of practice: Kaula emphasizes the body and its energies as the supreme instrument of spiritual transformation

    • Transcendence of duality: The Kaula path seeks to transcend the distinction between pure and impure, sacred and profane

    • The centrality of Shakti: In Kaula theology, Shakti is not merely the consort of Shiva but the supreme reality itself

    • The clan (Kula): Practitioners form a spiritual family bound by initiation and shared practice

    • Integration of all experience: Every experience—including those considered “impure” by conventional standards—can become a means of awakening

    4.2.3 The Kaula Lineage¶

    The tradition recognizes a succession of teachers:

    1. Shiva as the original revealer

    2. Matsyendranath as the first human master

    3. Gorakshanath and other Nath Siddhas

    4. Various regional lineages that developed specific Kaula practices

    4.3 Shakta Tantra¶

    Shakta Tantra encompasses those traditions that place the Goddess (Shakti, Devi) at the center of their theology and practice. Three major sub-traditions deserve special attention.

    4.3.1 Sri Vidya¶

    Sri Vidya (“auspicious knowledge”) is the Tantric tradition centered on the worship of Lalita Tripurasundari (the Beautiful Goddess of the Three Cities), also known as Shodashi or Rajarajeshvari.

    Central Mantra:
    The Panchadashi (15-syllable) or Shodashi (16-syllable) mantra
    Central Yantra:
    The Sri Yantra (Sri Chakra)—the supreme geometric representation of the Goddess
    Key Texts:
    Lalita Sahasranama, Saundaryalahari (attributed to Shankaracharya), Nityashodasikarnava
    Two Sub-lineages:
    The Hayagriva-Agastya lineage (Samayachara) and the Dakshinamurti lineage (Kaulachara)
    Geographical Spread:
    Particularly strong in South India, but with practitioners throughout the subcontinent

    4.3.2 Kali Kula¶

    The Kali Kula (“family of Kali”) tradition centers on the worship of Kali and her associated forms.

    • Dominant in Bengal, Assam, and parts of eastern India

    • Emphasizes the fierce and transformative aspects of the Goddess

    • Associated with the Dasha Mahavidya (Ten Wisdom Goddesses) tradition

    • Key texts: Mahakala Samhita, Todala Tantra, Kamakhya Tantra

    • The Kali Kula tends toward Vamachara (left-hand) practices more than Sri Vidya

    4.3.3 Tara Kula¶

    The Tara Kula tradition focuses on the goddess Tara, who occupies a unique position as a deity worshipped in both Hindu Tantric and Buddhist Vajrayana contexts.

    • Strong presence in Bengal, Bihar, and the Himalayan region

    • Tara is the second of the Dasha Mahavidya

    • Particularly associated with the power of speech and transcendent knowledge

    • The Tara Tantra and related texts form its scriptural basis

    4.4 Vaishnava Tantra¶

    Tantric traditions centered on Vishnu and his forms have a long and important history, though they are sometimes overlooked in discussions that focus primarily on Shaiva and Shakta Tantra.

    4.4.1 Pancharatra¶

    The Pancharatra tradition is the primary Vaishnava Tantric system.

    • Theology: Centers on Vishnu-Narayana and his emanations (Vyuhas): Vasudeva, Sankarshana, Pradyumna, and Aniruddha

    • Key Texts: Ahirbudhnya Samhita, Jayakhya Samhita, Paushkara Samhita, and approximately 200 other Samhitas

    • Practice: Elaborate temple worship, mantra recitation, and meditation on divine forms

    • Influence: The Pancharatra system deeply influenced the Sri Vaishnava tradition of Ramanuja and continues to govern worship in many Vishnu temples (others follow the Vaikhanasa tradition)

    4.4.2 Sahajiya¶

    The Sahajiya tradition of Bengal represents a Vaishnava Tantric path centered on the divine love of Radha and Krishna.

    • Emphasizes the concept of Sahaja (“the natural” or “the innate”)—the idea that the divine is found in the most natural, spontaneous state

    • Interprets the Radha-Krishna love story as a template for the soul’s relationship with the Divine

    • Incorporates elements of both Vaishnava devotion and Tantric practice

    • Influenced the Baul tradition of Bengal

    4.5 Buddhist Tantra: Vajrayana¶

    Vajrayana (“Diamond Vehicle” or “Thunderbolt Vehicle”) Buddhism represents one of the most developed and systematic Tantric traditions in the world.

    4.5.1 Historical Development¶

    Buddhist Tantra developed within the Mahayana Buddhist context, primarily at the great monastic universities of northern India (Nalanda, Vikramashila) during the 7th–12th centuries CE. Following the destruction of these institutions, the tradition was transmitted primarily to Tibet, where it became the dominant form of Buddhism.

    4.5.2 The Four Classes of Tantra¶

    Vajrayana Buddhism classifies its Tantric texts and practices into four hierarchical classes:

    1. Kriya Tantra (Action Tantra):
      The lowest class, emphasizing external ritual purity, bathing, clean clothing, vegetarian diet, and the precise performance of ritual actions. The deity is viewed as a lord to be served.

    2. Charya Tantra (Performance Tantra):
      Balances external ritual with internal meditation. The deity is viewed as a friend.

    3. Yoga Tantra:
      Emphasizes internal yoga and meditation over external ritual. The practitioner visualizes themselves as the deity. Includes practices of generating divine pride.

    4. Anuttarayoga Tantra (Highest Yoga Tantra):
      The most advanced class, divided into Father Tantras (emphasizing illusory body), Mother Tantras (emphasizing clear light), and Non-dual Tantras. Includes practices of subtle body manipulation, dream yoga, and the transformation of all experience into the path.

    4.5.3 Key Vajrayana Texts¶

    • Guhyasamaja Tantra: The most important Father Tantra

    • Hevajra Tantra: A principal Mother Tantra

    • Chakrasamvara Tantra: Major Mother Tantra closely related to Hindu Tantric traditions

    • Kalachakra Tantra: The “Wheel of Time” Tantra, unique in its cosmological scope

    4.6 Jain Tantra¶

    Though less well-known, Jain communities developed their own Tantric traditions, particularly in the medieval period.

    • Mantra-based practices: Jain Tantra primarily employs mantras, particularly the Namokar Mantra and mantras associated with Jain deities like Padmavati and Chakreshvari

    • Yantra use: Geometric diagrams for meditation and ritual purposes

    • Key texts: Bhairavapadmavatikalpa and other medieval Jain Tantric works

    • Goddess worship: Worship of Jain Yakshis (female guardian deities) with Tantric ritual methods

    • Distinctive feature: Jain Tantric practice maintains strict adherence to Jain ethical principles, particularly non-violence (Ahimsa), which rules out any blood offerings or violent practices

    4.7 Nath Sampradaya¶

    The Nath Sampradaya is one of the most influential Tantric orders, bridging Shaiva Tantra, Kaula practice, and the development of Hatha Yoga.

    4.7.1 The Nine Naths (Nava Nath)¶

    The tradition recognizes nine primary masters, though the exact list varies by lineage:

    1. Matsyendranath (Macchendranath) — The founder, receiver of Tantra from Shiva

    2. Gorakshanath (Gorakhnath) — The systematizer, most famous Nath

    3. Jalandharnath — Associated with the Jalandhar Peetha

    4. Kanifnath (Kanhapa) — A celebrated Siddha

    5. Gahininath — Prominent in the Marathi Nath tradition

    6. Bhartrihari — The king-turned-yogi, grammarian and philosopher

    7. Revananath — Associated with Karnataka

    8. Charpatinath (Charpati) — Known for miraculous powers

    9. Naganath — Associated with the Naga tradition

    4.7.2 Connection to Hatha Yoga¶

    The Nath tradition is the primary source of the Hatha Yoga system:

    • The Hatha Yoga Pradipika draws on Nath teachings, and the Gheranda Samhita and Shiva Samhita belong to the wider Hatha Yoga literature (their relation to the Naths is debated)

    • The emphasis on physical purification (Shatkarma), breath control (Pranayama), and energy locks (Bandha) as means of Kundalini awakening is distinctively Nath

    • The Nath tradition democratized Tantric practice by making it accessible regardless of caste or gender

    4.8 The 84 Siddha Tradition¶

    The tradition of the 84 Siddhas (accomplished ones) represents an important, somewhat amorphous stream within Tantra that cuts across sectarian boundaries.

    4.8.1 The Concept of Siddha¶

    A Siddha is one who has achieved spiritual perfection (Siddhi) through Tantric practice. The number 84 is considered sacred and symbolic, representing completeness.

    4.8.2 Notable Siddhas¶

    • Luipa: A fisherman who achieved realization; associated with the Chakrasamvara tradition

    • Virupa: A monk of Nalanda who became one of the most celebrated Siddhas

    • Tilopa and Naropa: Founders of the Kagyu lineage of Tibetan Buddhism

    • Saraha: Composer of the Doha Kosha, mystical songs expressing Tantric realization

    • Shavari: A tribal hunter who achieved supreme realization

    The Siddha tradition emphasizes that spiritual realization is available to all—regardless of caste, gender, occupation, or social status—a radically egalitarian stance that distinguishes Tantra from many orthodox religious traditions.

    Table 4.1. Comparison of Major Tantric Schools
    Feature Kashmir Shaivism Shakta (Sri Vidya) Kaula Vajrayana
    Supreme Reality Paramashiva Lalita / Devi Shakti (Kula) Shunyata / Buddha-nature
    Philosophy Paramadvaita (Absolute Non-dualism) Shakti-Advaita Non-dual Madhyamaka / Yogachara
    Primary Practice Recognition / Meditation Sri Yantra worship, Mantra Body-based energy practices Deity Yoga, Mandala
    Key Yantra Various Sri Yantra Body as Yantra Mandala
    Orientation Jnana (knowledge) Upasana (worship) Kriya (action) Upaya (skillful means)
    Path Type Primarily Dakshinachara Both Primarily Vamachara Both
    Geography Kashmir South India Bengal, Assam Tibet, Nepal
    Key Text Tantraloka Lalita Sahasranama Kularnava Tantra Guhyasamaja

    Important Note: While this chapter presents these schools as distinct categories, in historical reality there has been significant cross-pollination, overlap, and mutual influence among them. Many practitioners and lineages drew from multiple streams. Abhinavagupta, for example, was initiated into Shaiva, Shakta, and Kaula traditions and synthesized all of them in his work. The boundaries between schools are often more fluid than any categorization can convey.
