    Chapter 13: Shat Karma — The Six Tantric Actions¶

    The knowledge of the Shat Karmas is like fire—it can warm the house or burn it down. The intention of the practitioner determines the outcome.

    The Shat Karma (six actions) or Shat Karmas represent one of the most practically oriented—and most controversial—dimensions of Tantric knowledge. These six categories of ritual action encompass the range of effects that can be achieved through the application of Tantric methods (mantra, yantra, ritual, and subtle energy manipulation) to worldly situations. While some of these categories are entirely benevolent, others raise profound ethical questions that the Tantric tradition itself has grappled with extensively.

    13.1 Overview of the Six Categories¶

    The six categories of Tantric ritual action are:

    1. Shanti Karma — Pacification, peace, healing

    2. Vashikaran Karma — Attraction, influence, magnetism

    3. Stambhan Karma — Immobilization, freezing, stopping

    4. Vidveshan Karma — Creating discord, separation

    5. Uchchatan Karma — Disturbance, displacement, unsettling

    6. Maran Karma — Destruction, annihilation

    Some modern accounts group the last three, which the texts regard as aggressive (the sorcery called Abhichara), apart from the first three, which are regarded as protective or beneficial; sources differ about the grouping, and about the names and the order. The division is also too simple, because the texts themselves say that the same rite can serve good or bad ends depending on intention and context.

    Table 13.1. Correspondences of the Shat Karmas as given in some later manuals (lists vary between texts)

    13.2 Shanti Karma: Peace, Healing, and Removing Negativity¶

    Shanti Karma encompasses all Tantric actions aimed at establishing peace, promoting healing, removing negative influences, and creating harmony. This is the most widely practiced and universally approved of the six categories.

    13.2.1 Applications¶

    1. Healing of diseases:
      Specific mantras, yantras, and rituals are prescribed in the texts for the alleviation of physical and mental ailments. The Netra Tantra is particularly rich in healing methodologies. These are ritual and devotional practices; they are not a substitute for medical care.

    2. Removal of negative planetary influences:
      Shanti rituals for specific planets (Graha Shanti) are among the most commonly performed Tantric rites, particularly for individuals undergoing unfavorable planetary periods (Dasha).

    3. Protection from evil influences:
      Raksha (protection) rituals create a shield of positive energy around the practitioner or client, warding off negative energies, evil eye (Drishti Dosha), and malefic influences.

    4. Purification of spaces:
      Vastu Shanti rituals purify homes, offices, and other spaces of negative energies and establish harmonious vibrations.

    5. Mental peace:
      Mantras and meditation practices designed to calm the mind, reduce anxiety, and establish inner tranquility.

    6. Resolution of conflicts:
      Rituals to promote reconciliation and harmony in relationships, families, and communities.

    13.2.2 Methodology¶

    Shanti Karma typically employs:

    • White-colored offerings and materials

    • Sattvic (pure) substances: milk, ghee, white flowers, white rice, sugar

    • Peaceful mantras with soft, melodious recitation

    • Homa (fire ritual) with cooling substances

    • Performance during the waxing moon phase

    • North-East direction

    13.3 Vashikaran Karma: Attraction and Influence¶

    Vashikaran (from vash, “to attract, to bring under influence”) encompasses practices designed to create attraction, influence, and magnetic power.

    13.3.1 Aims Regarded as Acceptable¶

    Tradition and its commentators regard the following aims of Vashikaran as acceptable:

    1. Self-improvement: Developing personal magnetism, charisma, and the ability to positively influence others—essential for teachers, leaders, and healers

    2. Harmonizing relationships: Rekindling love and harmony in troubled marriages or relationships where both parties desire reconciliation

    3. Public speaking and teaching: Enhancing the ability to hold an audience’s attention and communicate effectively

    4. Healing rapport: A healer’s ability to establish trust and rapport with patients

    5. Spiritual teaching: A Guru’s ability to transmit teachings effectively to disciples

    13.3.2 Ethical Boundaries¶

    Tantric authors state that Vashikaran should never be used to:

    • Override another person’s free will for exploitative purposes

    • Force unwilling individuals into relationships

    • Manipulate people for personal gain at their expense

    • Exert undue influence over another’s decisions

    The karmic consequences of misusing Vashikaran are described as severe and long-lasting.

    13.4 Stambhan Karma: Immobilization and Freezing¶

    Stambhan (from stambh, “to stop, to make firm, to immobilize”) encompasses practices designed to stop, freeze, or immobilize hostile forces.

    13.4.1 Applications¶

    1. Checking hostile speech and action: The texts speak of silencing those who seek to harm the practitioner or others (modern readers take this as the stilling of malicious speech and aggression)

    2. Freezing legal proceedings: Halting unjust litigation or legal attacks

    3. Stopping natural calamities: Traditions describe practices to stop storms, floods, and other natural disasters

    4. Stopping diseases: Arresting the progress of illness

    5. Stopping negative thought patterns: Internal Stambhan—stopping destructive mental habits

    6. Self-control: Stopping one’s own negative impulses

    13.4.2 Association with Baglamukhi¶

    Baglamukhi, the eighth Mahavidya, is the primary deity associated with Stambhan Karma. Her characteristic gesture of seizing the tongue of a demon represents the power to stop hostile speech, lies, and aggression. Her worship is widely sought for success in legal disputes, debates, and competitions.

    13.5 Vidveshan Karma: Creating Discord¶

    Vidveshan (from vidvesh, “to create enmity”) refers to practices designed to create discord, separation, or enmity between individuals or groups.

    13.5.1 Ethical Context¶

    Vidveshan is perhaps the most ethically ambiguous of the Shat Karmas. While its misuse—sowing discord for personal gain—is universally condemned, certain applications have been considered legitimate within the tradition:

    1. Breaking harmful alliances: Separating an individual from harmful companions or influences

    2. Strategic defense: Creating discord among enemies who threaten one’s community

    3. Breaking addiction bonds: Some modern writers apply the category to freeing a person from a harmful dependence (a modern reading, not a classical one; treatment of addiction is a medical matter)

    4. Historical context: Some medieval texts and royal patrons treated Vidveshan as a tool of statecraft in war

    13.5.2 Severe Warnings¶

    The texts are emphatic that Vidveshan motivated by jealousy, revenge, or malice brings devastating karmic consequences. Tantric authors forbid its use for petty personal grievances.

    13.6 Uchchatan Karma: Disturbance and Displacement¶

    Uchchatan (from uchchata, “to disturb, to uproot”) encompasses practices designed to unsettle, disturb, or displace hostile forces or individuals.

    13.6.1 Applications¶

    1. Removing unwanted influences: Driving away negative entities, energies, or persistent troublemakers

    2. Displacing obstacles: Uprooting deep-seated obstacles to spiritual or material progress

    3. Unsettling the hostile: The texts describe the aim as unsettling the hostile (a purpose that modern ethics and law do not accept)

    4. Breaking entrenched patterns: Disrupting deeply rooted negative patterns—whether in individuals, relationships, or situations

    13.6.2 Relationship to Spiritual Practice¶

    On a subtle level, Uchchatan relates to the spiritual process of uprooting the deep-seated impressions (Samskaras) and tendencies (Vasanas) that bind the soul to the cycle of rebirth. In this internal sense, every genuine spiritual practice is a form of Uchchatan—disturbing the comfortable patterns of ignorance.

    13.7 Maran Karma: Destruction (Theoretical Discussion)¶

    Maran (from mri, “to die”) refers to the most extreme of the Shat Karmas—practices aimed at destruction.

    13.7.1 Theoretical Framework¶

    It must be stated clearly that Maran Karma, as discussed in the classical texts, is presented within a specific cosmological and ethical framework:

    1. Cosmic context: Just as the universe itself undergoes cycles of creation (Srishti), maintenance (Sthiti), and dissolution (Samhara), the Shat Karmas mirror these cosmic functions. Maran corresponds to the Samhara function of Shiva/Rudra.

    2. Symbolic interpretation: Many commentators interpret Maran symbolically—the “death” of the ego, the “destruction” of ignorance, the “killing” of negative tendencies.

    3. Historical context: Some medieval texts were composed for royal courts, where such rites were treated as an instrument of war.

    4. Deterrent function: The inclusion of Maran in the texts may have served a deterrent function—the knowledge that such powers existed encouraged respect for practitioners and their communities.

    13.7.2 Ethical Prohibitions¶

    The overwhelming consensus of the Tantric tradition is that Maran Karma is:

    • Prohibited for personal enmity: Using Maran for personal revenge or grudges is universally condemned

    • Karmically devastating: The karmic consequences of unjustified harm are described as extending over multiple lifetimes

    • Self-destructive: Many texts warn that Maran practices, if improperly motivated, rebound upon the practitioner with multiplied force

    • Condemned in modern ethics and law: Some medieval texts tolerate it against enemies in war, but harming another person by any means is both morally and legally unacceptable, and this book gives no procedure for it

    13.8 Ethical Framework and Karmic Consequences¶

    The Tantric tradition provides a sophisticated ethical framework for understanding and governing the use of the Shat Karmas.

    13.8.1 The Law of Karma¶

    The fundamental ethical principle governing all Tantric action is the law of Karma—the understanding that every action generates consequences that inevitably return to the actor. This principle applies with special force to ritual actions, which are deliberately concentrated expressions of intention.

    The Karmic Multiplier: The Tantric texts describe a principle whereby ritual actions generate karmic consequences many times greater than ordinary actions. Just as a mantra concentrates and amplifies the power of intention, the karmic consequences of actions performed through Tantric ritual are correspondingly amplified. This means that both the merit of healing work and the demerit of harmful work are enormously magnified.

    13.8.2 Hierarchical Ethics¶

    The Tantric texts establish a clear ethical hierarchy among the Shat Karmas:

    1. Shanti Karma: Always encouraged and meritorious

    2. Vashikaran: Acceptable when motivated by genuine love and beneficial intention

    3. Stambhan: Acceptable for defensive purposes

    4. Vidveshan: Widely condemned; a few texts allow it only in extreme circumstances

    5. Uchchatan: Similar restrictions as Vidveshan

    6. Maran: Condemned by the overwhelming majority of authors

    13.9 The Role of Intention (Sankalpa)¶

    The concept of Sankalpa (intention, resolve, determination) is central to the ethical evaluation of any Tantric action.

    13.9.1 Sankalpa as the Determining Factor¶

    The same mantra or ritual can serve vastly different purposes depending on the intention with which it is performed. Tantric authors state that it is the Sankalpa—not the technique itself—that determines whether an action is Dharmic (righteous) or Adharmic (unrighteous).

    13.9.2 Three Levels of Intention¶

    1. Sattvic Intention: Motivated by compassion, healing, protection of the innocent, and service to the Divine. This intention sanctifies any practice.

    2. Rajasic Intention: Motivated by personal desire, ambition, or gain—even if not actively harmful to others. This intention generates mixed karmic results.

    3. Tamasic Intention: Motivated by hatred, revenge, jealousy, or the desire to harm. This intention corrupts any practice, regardless of the technique used, and generates severe negative karma.

    13.9.3 The Practitioner’s Responsibility¶

    The Tantric practitioner who possesses knowledge of the Shat Karmas bears a heavy responsibility. With great power comes great accountability, and the texts are clear that knowledge of these practices creates an obligation to use them wisely, compassionately, and always in alignment with Dharma.

    The ideal practitioner, as the texts portray him:

    • Uses Shanti Karma freely and generously for the benefit of all

    • Employs Vashikaran only for the harmonization of relationships and the enhancement of positive influence

    • Reserves Stambhan for the stilling of his own and others’ harmful impulses

    • Avoids Vidveshan, Uchchatan, and Maran

    • Maintains constant awareness of the karmic implications of every ritual action

    • Regularly purifies intention through meditation, self-inquiry, and devotion

    Ethical Declaration: This chapter presents the traditional framework of the Shat Karmas for scholarly and educational purposes. The authors unequivocally affirm that the use of any Tantric practice to cause harm to others is morally wrong, karmically devastating, and contrary to the highest teachings of the Tantric tradition, and that it is a crime in law. The chapter describes what texts say and gives no procedure for any rite intended to injure, control or subjugate another person. The supreme purpose of all Tantric knowledge is liberation (Moksha) and the alleviation of suffering—never its deliberate infliction.
