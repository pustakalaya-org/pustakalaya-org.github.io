    Chapter 7: Classical Tantric Texts¶

    7.1 Introduction to Tantric Literature¶

    The literary corpus of Tantra spans over two millennia, encompassing thousands of texts in Sanskrit, Prakrit, Tibetan, and regional Indian languages. These texts, known variously as Tantras, Agamas, Nigamas, and Samhitas, form the scriptural foundation of tantric practice and philosophy. Unlike the Vedas, which emphasize ritual purity and social hierarchy, the Tantras often proclaim universal accessibility to spiritual knowledge.

    The classification of tantric texts follows several schemes:

    • By Tradition: Shaiva Agamas (28 primary), Shakta Tantras (64 traditionally), Vaishnava Pancharatra Samhitas

    • By Orientation: the Shaiva Siddhanta (Shiva Tantras) and the Bhairava Tantras, which scholars such as Alexis Sanderson treat as two great divisions of the Shaiva scriptures

    • By Path: Dakshinachara (right-hand), Vamachara (left-hand), Kaulachara (integrated)

    7.2 Kularnava Tantra¶

    The Kularnava Tantra is one of the most comprehensive and frequently cited texts of the Kaula tradition. Structured as a dialogue between Shiva and Parvati, it covers the complete spectrum of tantric practice.

    7.2.1 Key Teachings¶

    • Guru-Disciple Relationship: Elaborate exposition of the guru’s qualifications, the disciple’s duties, and the sacred bond of initiation

    • Six Acts (Shat Karma): Discussion of the six ritual aims, with warnings against their misuse

    • Stages of Practice: Progressive levels from Vedachara through Kaulachara

    • Wine Ritual: Philosophical and practical instructions for the use of wine in tantric ritual, emphasizing its symbolic and transformative purpose

    • Mantra Science: Classification of mantras, procedures for awakening, and signs of siddhi

    Verses of the text praise the Kaula teaching as the highest of all paths.

    7.3 Mahanirvana Tantra¶

    The Mahanirvana Tantra (“Tantra of the Great Liberation”) represents a reformist current within tantric tradition, attributed to the 18th century but drawing on older materials.

    7.3.1 Distinctive Features¶

    • Emphasizes ethical conduct and social responsibility alongside tantric practice

    • Provides detailed code for social governance, marriage, inheritance, and property

    • Offers simplified worship procedures accessible to householders

    • Integrates Vedantic philosophy with tantric ritual

    Sir John Woodroffe (Arthur Avalon) translated this text as Tantra of the Great Liberation (1913), making it one of the best-known tantric scriptures in English.

    7.4 Tantraloka of Abhinavagupta¶

    The Tantraloka (“Light on Tantra”) by the great Kashmiri master Abhinavagupta (fl. c. 975–1025 CE) stands as the supreme encyclopedic treatise on tantric philosophy and practice.

    7.4.1 Structure and Scope¶

    Comprising 37 chapters (ahnikas) and some 5,800 verses, the Tantraloka covers, in an order that cannot be summarised chapter by chapter here, the philosophy of Trika Shaivism, the means (upayas) to liberation, the nature of time and space, mantra and the powers of speech, initiation, and the ritual of the Kaula tradition. A concise summary is the Tantrasara.

    Abhinavagupta synthesized multiple tantric traditions into a coherent philosophical framework rooted in the non-dual recognition (Pratyabhijna) school of Kashmir Shaivism.

    7.5 Vijnana Bhairava Tantra¶

    The Vijnana Bhairava Tantra is perhaps the most practically valuable tantric text ever composed. In a mere 163 verses, it presents 112 meditation techniques (dharanas) for directly experiencing the nature of consciousness.

    7.5.1 The 112 Dharanas¶

    Organized around different starting points for meditation:

    • Breath-based: Awareness of breath at nostrils, pause between breaths, prana movement

    • Sensation-based: Intense pleasure, intense pain, taste, touch, sound as portals

    • Imagination-based: Visualizing void, fire, dissolving elements

    • Awareness-based: Witnessing thoughts, awareness of awareness itself

    • Devotion-based: Surrender, gratitude, wonder, sacred trembling

    • Space-based: Contemplating vast spaces, sky, gaps between objects

    Each dharana provides a complete method for transcending ordinary consciousness. As the text states, even one technique practiced deeply can lead to full realization.

    7.6 Shiva Sutras¶

    The Shiva Sutras of Vasugupta (9th–early 10th century CE) are the foundational text of Kashmir Shaivism. According to tradition, Vasugupta discovered these 77 aphorisms (the number in the Kashmiri recension) inscribed on a rock at Shankarapala mountain near Srinagar.

    7.6.1 Three Sections (Upayas)¶

    1. Shambhavopaya (Divine Means): 22 sutras on the path of direct will/grace — “Consciousness is the Self” (Chaitanyam Atma)

    2. Shaktopaya (Energy Means): 10 sutras on the path of knowledge/contemplation — mantra as living consciousness

    3. Anavopaya (Individual Means): 45 sutras on the path of individual effort — breath, body, meditation practices

    7.7 Spanda Karika¶

    The Spanda Karikas (“Verses on the Divine Pulse”) by Vasugupta or his disciple Kallata expound the doctrine of Spanda — the creative throb or vibration of consciousness.

    7.7.1 Core Teaching¶

    The text demonstrates that all experience — perception, thought, emotion, action — arises from the dynamic pulsation of consciousness. By recognizing this Spanda in one’s own experience, one realizes one’s identity with Shiva.

    7.8 Other Major Texts¶

    7.8.1 Rudrayamala Tantra¶

    An ancient and comprehensive tantra covering cosmology, ritual, mantra, yantra, and advanced practices. Contains teachings on the union of Rudra and his Shakti.

    7.8.2 Yogini Tantra¶

    A Shakta text centered on the worship of the Goddess at Kamakhya in Assam. Describes rituals specific to the Kamakhya tradition and the worship of 64 Yoginis.

    7.8.3 Buddhist Tantric Texts¶

    • Guhyasamaja Tantra: “Assembly of Secrets” — earliest Anuttarayoga Tantra, Akshobhya Buddha practice

    • Hevajra Tantra: Non-dual wisdom through the Hevajra deity system

    • Chakrasamvara Tantra: Heruka practice, important in Tibetan Buddhism

    • Kalachakra Tantra: “Wheel of Time” — cosmology, astrology, yoga, and prophecy

    7.8.4 Tamil Siddhar Literature¶

    The 18 Tamil Siddhars (Agastyar, Bogar, Thirumoolar, Konganar, etc.) produced a vast corpus of texts on yoga, alchemy, medicine, and tantra in Tamil. Thirumoolar’s Thirumandiram (over 3,000 verses) is particularly significant.

    7.9 Preservation and Transmission¶

    Tantric texts face unique preservation challenges:

    • Some were intentionally obscure, using “twilight language” (sandhya bhasha) in the Buddhist Tantras

    • Oral transmission was preferred over written form

    • Neglect and loss of manuscripts in the colonial and later periods

    • Digital initiatives (NGMCP, Muktabodha) now preserve remaining manuscripts

    • Academic translations making texts accessible (KSTS series, EFEO publications)
