    Chapter 11: Coasts, Ports and Islands¶

    samudravasane devi parvatastanamaṇḍale
    viṣṇupatni namas tubhyaṃ pādasparśaṃ kṣamasva me
    “O Goddess whose garment is the ocean, whose breasts are the mountains, consort of Viṣṇu, I bow to you; forgive me for touching you with my feet.”

    — Recited by many Hindus on waking, before touching the ground

    The coastline of the Indian mainland and its islands was long given officially as about 7,500 km; a remeasurement by the National Hydrographic Office and the Survey of India, reported in 2024–25, gives about 11,100 km. The coast has not grown. The new figure was measured on more detailed maps and follows more of the creeks, estuaries and islands, and an indented coastline always comes out longer the more finely it is measured. It runs from the salt marshes of Kutch past the gulfs of Khambhat and the Konkan, the lagoons of Kerala and the sandy, surf-beaten shore of the Coromandel to the deltas of the Godāvarī, Kṛṣṇā, Mahānadī and Gaṅgā. Offshore lie Sri Lanka, the coral atolls of Lakshadweep and the Maldives, and, far out in the Bay of Bengal, the Andaman and Nicobar Islands. For two thousand years and more, this coast connected India with Africa, Arabia, the Mediterranean, Southeast Asia and China. Much of the knowledge needed to use it—of winds, currents, tides, shoals and landmarks—belonged to fishermen, boat-builders, pilots and sailors, and is known to us only indirectly (Ray 2003; Chaudhuri 1985).

    11.1 The Coasts¶

    11.1.1 Gujarat and the Konkan¶

    The coast of Gujarat, with its two great gulfs of Kutch and Khambhat, has been a centre of seafaring from Harappan times. Lothal, at the head of the Gulf of Khambhat, was a Harappan town and a centre of bead-making and trade, with a large brick-lined basin whose function is debated.

    Scholarly Note: The “Dockyard” at Lothal

    The basin at Lothal was interpreted by its excavator, S. R. Rao, as a tidal dockyard, and is often described as the world’s earliest dock. This interpretation has been seriously questioned: L. S. Leshnik argued that the basin was more probably a water reservoir for irrigation and drinking water (Leshnik 1968), and the evidence for its connection to a tidal channel is disputed. The dockyard claim should be treated as one interpretation among several, not as established fact.

    In the early historical period the great port was Barygaza (Bharuch) on the Narmadā, whose difficult approach through tidal mudflats is described in the Periplus, which says that local pilots in boats met incoming ships to guide them in (Casson 1989). Later the leading ports were Khambhat (Cambay), from about the tenth to the sixteenth century, and Surat, from the sixteenth to the eighteenth. Gujarati merchants and ships were active from the Red Sea to Malacca.

    South of Gujarat the Konkan coast is a narrow strip between the Western Ghats and the sea, cut by many creeks and estuaries. Its ports—Sopara, Kalyan, Chaul, Dabhol and later Goa and Bombay—linked the Deccan, through the passes, to the sea. At Chaul in 1508 a Portuguese squadron was defeated by a Mamluk fleet allied with the Gujarat sultanate; the next year the Portuguese destroyed that fleet off Diu, and in 1510 they took Goa.

    11.1.2 Malabar¶

    The coast of Kerala is characterised by lagoons and backwaters (kāyal) behind the shore, which allowed sheltered inland navigation, and by the pepper and other spices of its hinterland. Its ports attracted merchants from the Mediterranean, the Middle East and China, and its Muslim communities, studied by Sebastian Prange, grew out of the trade that linked Malabar with Arabia and the Gulf (Prange 2018).

    • Muziris (Muciṟi in Tamil), near modern Kodungallur, was the great pepper port of the Roman period, described in the Periplus and in Tamil Caṅkam poems; excavations at Pattanam have revealed much Mediterranean pottery and are thought by many to locate it (Tomber 2008). Tradition holds that the harbour silted up after a great flood in 1341, which opened the harbour at Kochi.

    • Kollam (Quilon) was a major port of the ninth to fourteenth centuries, visited by Arab and Chinese ships.

    • Calicut (Kozhikode), ruled by the Zamorin (Sāmūtiri), was the leading port of Malabar in the fourteenth and fifteenth centuries. Ibn Baṭṭūṭa saw Chinese junks there; the Ming admiral Zheng He’s fleets called there between 1405 and 1433; and Vasco da Gama arrived there in 1498.

    11.1.3 The Coromandel and the East Coast¶

    The east coast south of the Kṛṣṇā is mostly open, sandy and exposed to surf, with few natural harbours. Ships anchored offshore and cargo and passengers were carried through the surf in flexible sewn-plank boats (the masula boats of the Coromandel) or on log rafts.

    • Pukār (Kāvirippūmpaṭṭiṉam), at the mouth of the Kāverī, was the port of the early Cōḻas. The Caṅkam poem Paṭṭiṉappālai describes horses arriving by sea, pepper brought by land, and goods from Īḻam (Sri Lanka) and Kāḻakam (in Southeast Asia) in its markets. A later Tamil epic, the Maṇimēkalai, tells that the city was swallowed by the sea; underwater and coastal archaeology has found remains of early historic structures in the area.

    • Arikamedu, near Puducherry, was a trading station with Mediterranean contacts in the early centuries CE (Chapter 6).

    • Māmallapuram was the port of the Pallava kings in the seventh and eighth centuries.

    • Nāgapaṭṭiṉam was the main Cōḻa port. Copper plates of the Cōḻa king Rājarāja I record his grant of a village to a Buddhist monastery there built by the Śailendra king of Śrīvijaya—a sign of the close and complicated relations across the Bay of Bengal (Chapter 13).

    • Motupalli, in Andhra, was the port of the Kākatīya kingdom; an inscription of Gaṇapatideva (1244/45) guarantees merchants’ safety and fixes the duties they paid, and Marco Polo landed in the kingdom (Chapter 6). In the seventeenth century Masulipatnam (Machilipatnam) was the great port of the Golconda sultanate.

    • Further north, the ports of Kaliṅga (Odisha) and Bengal—Tāmralipti in early times, Saptagrām, Hugli and Chittagong later—linked the Gangetic plain to the Bay of Bengal (Chapter 8).

    11.2 Surat and the Merchants of Gujarat¶

    In the seventeenth century Surat, on the Tāpī, was the greatest port of the Mughal empire. Through it passed the textiles of Gujarat and the north, indigo, saltpetre and other goods to the Red Sea, the Gulf, Southeast Asia and, increasingly, to Europe through the English and Dutch companies, which had their main Indian factories there. It was also the principal port for the Hajj (Chapter 4). Its merchants included Hindu and Jain vāṇiyās, Muslim Bohras and other Muslim traders, Parsis, Armenians and Arabs. The greatest of them, Mullā ʿAbd al-Ghafūr (d. 1718), a Bohra merchant, owned a fleet of ocean-going ships trading to the Red Sea and the Gulf.

    Ashin Das Gupta’s study of Surat in the early eighteenth century shows how the port’s prosperity depended on the security of the routes to the Red Sea and on the stability of the Mughal, Safavid and Ottoman empires; when all three declined, and as the English company’s power grew, Surat’s Indian shipping declined and Bombay rose in its place (Das Gupta 1979). The story of Surat is a reminder that the geography of ports was shaped by politics as much as by harbours and winds.

    11.2.1 The Backwaters of Kerala¶

    Behind the coast of central Kerala lies a network of lagoons, lakes, rivers and canals, of which Vembanad is the largest. For centuries this network carried much of the region’s traffic in boats: rice from the low-lying fields of Kuttanad, pepper and other spices from the hills, coir, coconuts and passengers moved between the inland towns and the ports of Kochi and Kollam. Large cargo boats of planks sewn with coir (keṭṭuvaḷḷam) were the typical craft. The fields of Kuttanad, partly below sea level and protected by bunds, are an example of land reclaimed and managed by local communities over generations.

    11.3 Winds, Seasons and Navigation¶

    11.3.1 The Monsoon¶

    The word “monsoon” comes, through Portuguese, from the Arabic mawsim, “season”. Over the Arabian Sea and the Bay of Bengal the winds blow from the south-west from about June to September and from the north-east from about November to February, with variable winds in between. The pattern governed sailing (Table 11.1) and shaped a calendar of trade for the whole Indian Ocean until the coming of steam (Chaudhuri 1985).

    Table 11.1. The monsoon calendar of sailing in the Arabian Sea and the Bay of Bengal
    Season Effect on sailing
    Early south-west monsoon (May–June) and its end (late August–September) ships from the Red Sea and Arabia sail to India; Indian ships cross the Bay of Bengal eastward
    Height of the south-west monsoon (June–August) storms and heavy seas; west-coast ports largely closed
    North-east monsoon (about November–February) ships sail from India to Arabia, the Red Sea and East Africa, and from Southeast Asia back to India
    Transitions (March–May, October) variable winds; coastal sailing; cyclones in the Bay of Bengal in October–November

    Indian, Arab and Persian sailors knew these winds long before Greek and Roman merchants learned to use them for the open-sea crossing to India in the late first century BCE or early first century CE. The Periplus associates the crossing with a pilot named Hippalus, after whom the south-west wind was sometimes called; but Hippalus learned what local sailors already knew (Casson 1989).

    11.3.2 Pilots and Their Knowledge¶

    Indian Ocean pilots navigated by the stars, by soundings and by coastal landmarks. They measured the altitude of the Pole Star and other stars with a simple device, the kamāl—a small board held at arm’s length on a knotted string—in units of “fingers”, which gave a measure of latitude. They knew the colour of the water and the kinds of sea-bottom brought up by the sounding lead, the birds and sea-snakes that indicated nearness to land, and the profiles of mountains and headlands seen from the sea. Aḥmad ibn Mājid’s Kitāb al-Fawāʾid (c. 1490) and the works of Sulaymān al-Mahrī record this knowledge in Arabic for the western Indian Ocean, including the coasts of India (Tibbetts 1971). Gujarati and other Indian pilots shared in it; the pilot who guided Vasco da Gama from Malindi to Calicut in 1498 was, according to the Portuguese chroniclers, a Gujarati (Chapter 5).

    Scholarly Note: What the Texts Do and Do Not Tell Us

    Sanskrit texts mention ships, sea voyages and shipwrecks—the Jātaka stories, for instance, tell of merchants sailing to Suvarṇabhūmi and of a blind master mariner of Suppāraka (Sopara) who could tell where a ship was from the water. A Sanskrit compendium, the Yuktikalpataru, traditionally attributed to King Bhoja but of uncertain date, classifies ships by size and timber. But no Sanskrit manual of navigation comparable to the Arabic pilot books survives. The navigational knowledge of Indian sailors was transmitted largely by practice and oral instruction, and much of what is said about it rests on later sources and on inference.

    11.3.3 Boats¶

    Boat-building responded to local conditions. On the west coast, ocean-going ships were traditionally built of planks sewn together with coir rope rather than nailed, a technique noted by Marco Polo and other medieval travellers and used in the western Indian Ocean until recent times. On the surf-beaten Coromandel coast, the masula boat, with planks sewn with coir and no frame, was flexible enough to survive landing through the breakers, and the kaṭṭumaram (Tamil, “tied logs”), a raft of logs lashed together, could be launched and landed through heavy surf; the English word “catamaran” is derived from it, though modern catamarans are twin-hulled boats of a different kind.

    11.4 Seafaring Memories of the East Coast¶

    The seafaring traditions of Kaliṅga (Odisha) and Bengal are remembered in festivals and stories. In Odisha, the festival of Bāli Jātrā at Cuttack, held at the full moon of Kārttika (October–November), when the north-east monsoon allowed ships to sail south-east, recalls the voyages of the sādhabas, merchant-mariners who traded with “Bāli”, Java and Sumatra; on the same day people float small boats on rivers and tanks. In Bengal, medieval narrative poems (maṅgalkāvya) tell of merchants such as Cānd Saudāgar and Dhanapati who sailed down the rivers to the sea and across to Laṅkā and beyond, naming river ports and landmarks along the way. These memories are literary and ritual, not logs of voyages, but they show how deeply sea trade entered the culture of the eastern coast.

    On the west coast, the ports of Kutch and Saurashtra, such as Mandvi, kept alive the building of large wooden sailing ships (vahāṇ) and the knowledge of the routes to Arabia and East Africa well into the twentieth century; Kutchi pilots and sailors were among the last practitioners of traditional Indian Ocean navigation, and some of their pilot books and charts survive (Chapter 5).

    11.5 Sri Lanka¶

    Sri Lanka is separated from India by the Palk Strait and the Gulf of Mannar. Between Rāmeśvaram and Mannar island stretches a chain of sand-banks and limestone shoals, some 30 km long, known as Adam’s Bridge or Rāma Setu. During the low sea levels of the last ice age, when the sea was more than 100 m lower than today, India and Sri Lanka were joined by land; the connection was finally submerged in the early Holocene.

    In Hindu tradition the shoals are the bridge built by Rāma’s army to reach Laṅkā. Satellite photographs published by NASA in 2002 were widely reported as showing a man-made structure; NASA itself stated that the images said nothing about the origin of the shoals, which geologists regard as natural. The proposed Sethusamudram shipping canal through the shoals became a subject of political and legal controversy from 2005. The identification of the Laṅkā of the Rāmāyaṇa with Sri Lanka is traditional and very old, though some scholars have argued that the epic’s Laṅkā was originally imagined elsewhere.

    Sri Lanka’s historical connections with India are documented independently of the epic. The Pali chronicles of the island, the Dīpavaṃsa and Mahāvaṃsa, tell of settlers from northern India led by Vijaya and of the arrival of Buddhism with Aśoka’s son Mahinda. Tamil Caṅkam poetry knows the island as Īḻam and mentions goods from it in the markets of Pukār. Gems, pearls from the Gulf of Mannar and cinnamon were among its famous products, and its ports were stations on the routes between the Arabian Sea and the Bay of Bengal.

    11.6 The Andaman and Nicobar Islands¶

    The Andaman and Nicobar Islands form an arc in the eastern Bay of Bengal, more than 1,000 km from the Indian mainland and much closer to Myanmar and Sumatra. Ships on the route between India and Southeast Asia passed them, and travellers reported them, often with fear of their inhabitants. Yijing in the seventh century describes a “country of naked people”, probably the Nicobars, on his voyage from Kedah on the Malay peninsula to Tāmralipti. The great inscription of the Cōḻa king Rājendra I at Tanjavur, listing the places taken in his expedition against Śrīvijaya around 1025, includes Mānakkavāram, usually identified with the Nicobars. Marco Polo mentions both island groups under the names Necuveram and Angamanain, repeating stories of cannibals that later writers copied.

    The Andamans were inhabited by peoples—the Great Andamanese, Onge, Jarawa and Sentinelese—whose ancestors appear from genetic studies to have lived in the islands for a very long time, and whose languages are unrelated to those of the mainland. The Nicobars are home to the Nicobarese and the Shompen. The British established a penal settlement at Port Blair in 1858, after the revolt of 1857, and built the Cellular Jail there between 1896 and 1906. Colonial settlement, disease and later development reduced the Great Andamanese to a few dozen people; the Sentinelese continue to refuse contact. The 2004 Indian Ocean tsunami devastated the islands, especially the Nicobars. Any account of the “geographical knowledge” of the islanders must start from respect for their autonomy and from the recognition that most of what has been written about them was written by outsiders.

    11.7 Lakshadweep and the Maldives¶

    The coral atolls of Lakshadweep lie some 200–400 km off the coast of Kerala; the much larger Maldive archipelago lies further south. They were landmarks and hazards on the routes across the Arabian Sea, and their inhabitants were skilled sailors. Most of the people of Lakshadweep speak a form of Malayalam; on Minicoy, the southernmost island, the language is Mahl, a form of Dhivehi, the language of the Maldives. The islanders are Muslim, with matrilineal customs like those of parts of the Malabar coast; tradition dates their conversion to Islam to the early centuries of Islam, though the details are legendary. The islands were later controlled for a time by the Ali Rajas of Kannur and then by the British.

    The Maldives converted to Islam in the twelfth century (traditionally in 1153). Ibn Baṭṭūṭa, who served as a judge there in 1343–44 and married into the islands’ ruling families, described an island economy based on coconut fibre (coir), dried fish and cowrie shells, and a society in which he found the women’s freedom of dress and movement remarkable (Gibb 1929; Dunn 1986). Maldivian cowries were exported in great quantities to Bengal and, later, to West Africa, where they served as currency.

    11.8 The Coasts and the European Companies¶

    From 1498 the coasts of India became the arena of European commercial and naval competition. The Portuguese established fortified trading posts and their capital at Goa (1510), tried to control the spice trade of Malabar, and required Asian ships to buy passes (cartaz). In the seventeenth century the Dutch and English East India Companies, followed by the French and Danes, set up factories on both coasts: the English at Surat (1613), Madras (1639), Bombay (1668) and Calcutta (1690); the Dutch at Pulicat, Cochin and Nāgapaṭṭiṉam; the French at Pondicherry; the Danes at Tranquebar.

    The geography of the ports changed with them. Some old ports declined as trade was redirected or harbours silted up; new ones grew around company forts, which became the great colonial port cities of Bombay, Madras and Calcutta. European pilots and hydrographers charted the coasts in detail, relying at first on Indian and Arab pilots and their knowledge. The charts of the coasts made by the English company’s marine surveyors in the late eighteenth and nineteenth centuries, like the land surveys described in Chapter 14, drew on and gradually replaced the knowledge held by local mariners (Chaudhuri 1985; Pearson 2003).

    11.9 The Sacred Coast¶

    The coasts of India are lined with sacred places of many religions. Hindu shrines stand at the extremities and landmarks of the coast: Somanātha and Dvārakā in Saurashtra, Gokarṇa on the Karnataka coast, Kanyākumārī at the southern tip, where the goddess is said to wait for her divine bridegroom, Rāmeśvaram, Purī and Gaṅgāsāgar at the mouth of the Hugli, where pilgrims bathe each January. Sailors and fishermen worship their own deities of the sea and of safe return. Muslim shrines include the dargāh of the saint Shāhul Hamīd at Nagore on the Coromandel coast, visited by fishermen and sailors of all religions; Christian shrines include the basilica of Our Lady of Good Health at Velankanni nearby, associated in tradition with the rescue of Portuguese sailors from a storm, and the church of St Thomas at Mylapore. The mosques of the Malabar coast, some of them among the oldest in South Asia, and the synagogues of Kochi record the communities that the sea brought to India. The coast is thus a sacred landscape as plural as the communities that lived and traded along it.

    11.10 Underwater Archaeology and Its Claims¶

    Marine archaeology in India began in the 1980s, when the National Institute of Oceanography undertook underwater surveys off Dvārakā and Bet Dwarka in Gujarat under the direction of S. R. Rao. Stone structures, anchors and other finds were reported and were interpreted by some as the remains of the city of Kṛṣṇa described in the Mahābhārata, submerged by the sea. The dating and interpretation of these finds are disputed: many of the stone anchors are of types used over long periods, and the structures have not been securely dated to the second millennium BCE. Similar claims have been made about a “submerged city” in the Gulf of Khambhat, based on sonar images and dredged objects, which most archaeologists reject. Marine archaeology has produced valuable results on ancient ports and shipwrecks, but claims that it has confirmed the literal history of the epics are not established (Ray 2003).

    11.11 The Pearl Fisheries of the Gulf of Mannar¶

    The shallow banks of the Gulf of Mannar, between southern Tamil Nadu and north-western Sri Lanka, were for two thousand years among the world’s most famous sources of pearls. Greek and Roman writers knew of them, the Periplus names Kolchoi (Korkai) as the centre of the fishery under the Pāṇḍyas, and Caṅkam poems mention the pearls of Korkai. Marco Polo described the divers of the Coromandel coast and the charms used to protect them from sharks; later the Portuguese, then the Dutch and finally the British controlled the fisheries on both sides of the gulf, leasing them out and taxing the catch (Casson 1989; Yule 1903).

    The fishery depended on detailed local knowledge: of the position and condition of the oyster banks, which were inspected before each season; of the brief calm season in spring when diving was possible; and of the skills of the divers, most of them from the Parava and other fishing communities, who dived without apparatus, using stones to descend. The banks were exhausted by over-exploitation and changing conditions in the twentieth century, and the great fisheries came to an end. Their history illustrates both the richness of coastal knowledge and the way in which states and companies appropriated its profits.

    11.12 Cyclones and Storm Surges¶

    The east coast of India and the coasts of Bangladesh and Myanmar are exposed to tropical cyclones that form over the Bay of Bengal, chiefly in the transitional seasons before and after the south-west monsoon. The funnel shape and shallow waters of the northern Bay amplify the storm surges that accompany them, and some of the deadliest natural disasters in recorded history have struck the deltas of the Gaṅgā, Brahmaputra, Mahānadī and Godāvarī. Seafarers knew the dangerous months and avoided them; Indian Ocean sailing calendars in Arabic and Indian sources mark the closing of the seas.

    Coastal communities developed their own knowledge of storm signs—changes in the swell, the colour of the sky, the behaviour of birds—and built in ways that took floods into account, while temples and other strong buildings served as refuges. Modern meteorology, satellite tracking and organised evacuation have greatly reduced the loss of life from cyclones on the Indian coast since the Odisha cyclone of 1999, which killed thousands. How much local knowledge contributed to that improvement has not been measured; that the two kinds of knowledge could work together in warning and evacuation is a reasonable hope rather than a demonstrated result.

    11.13 Fishing Communities¶

    The fishing communities of the Indian coasts—the Kolīs of Gujarat and the Konkan, the Mukkuvar of Kerala and southern Tamil Nadu, the Paravar of the pearl-fishing coast, the Pattanavar and others of the Coromandel, and many more—hold detailed knowledge of the sea: the seasons and movements of fish, the local winds, currents and tides, the location of reefs and fishing grounds, and the signs of approaching storms. They were also, historically, among the people most exposed to the power of states and companies: the Paravar of the pearl coast converted to Catholicism in large numbers in the 1530s under Portuguese protection, and fishing communities have often been socially marginalised.

    Modern Relevance: Coastal Knowledge Today

    Marine scientists and fisheries managers increasingly recognise the value of fishers’ knowledge, alongside scientific surveys, for understanding fish stocks, coastal change and the effects of climate change. The 2004 tsunami and later cyclones showed both the vulnerability of coastal communities and the importance of local knowledge in warning and recovery.
