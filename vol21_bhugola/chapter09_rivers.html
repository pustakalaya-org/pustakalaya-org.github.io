    Chapter 9: Rivers: Hymns, Goddesses and Water Management¶

    gaṅge ca yamune caiva godāvari sarasvati
    narmade sindhu kāveri jale ’smin saṃnidhiṃ kuru
    “O Gaṅgā, Yamunā, Godāvarī, Sarasvatī, Narmadā, Sindhu and Kāverī, be present in this water.”

    — A verse widely recited before ritual bathing

    The rivers of the Indian subcontinent are at once the basis of its agriculture, its main highways before the railway, and some of its most important deities. The verse quoted above, recited by many Hindus while bathing, calls seven great rivers from the north, the Deccan and the far south into a single vessel of water: a small act of sacred geography that unites the subcontinent through its rivers. This chapter looks at the rivers in the Vedic hymns, the problem of the Sarasvatī, the Gaṅgā and the rivers of the peninsula, and the long history of water management.

    9.1 Rivers in the Ṛgveda¶

    The Ṛgveda speaks often of “the seven rivers” (sapta sindhavaḥ); the Avesta similarly knows a land of the “seven rivers” (hapta hindu). Which seven are meant is not stated and was probably not fixed. The geography of the hymns is centred on the Indus (Sindhu) and its Punjab tributaries, with the Sarasvatī to the east.

    The Nadīstuti hymn (Ṛgveda 10.75), one of the later hymns of the collection, praises the Sindhu as the greatest of rivers and names others in two groups. The first runs from east to west, beginning with the Gaṅgā, which appears here for one of the very few times in the Ṛgveda.

    Ṛgveda 10.75.5

    imaṃ me gaṅge yamune sarasvati śutudri stomaṃ sacatā paruṣṇy ā
    asiknyā marudvṛdhe vitastayārjīkīye śṛṇuhy ā suṣomayā

    “O Gaṅgā, Yamunā, Sarasvatī, Śutudrī, with the Paruṣṇī attend to this praise of mine; O Marudvṛdhā with the Asiknī, O Ārjīkīyā with the Vitastā and the Suṣomā, hear me.”

    The Śutudrī is the Sutlej, the Paruṣṇī the Ravi, the Asiknī the Chenab and the Vitastā the Jhelum; the Marudvṛdhā, Ārjīkīyā and Suṣomā are smaller streams whose identification is less certain. The next verse names western tributaries of the Indus, among them the Kubhā (Kabul), Krumu (Kurram) and Gomatī (Gomal). The ordering reflects real knowledge of the river system of the north-west. Michael Witzel and others have used the distribution of river names in Vedic texts of different periods to trace the movement of the centre of Vedic culture from the Punjab and the Sarasvatī region eastward into the Gaṅgā–Yamunā Doab (Witzel 1995).

    9.2 The Sarasvatī¶

    9.2.1 In the Texts¶

    In the Ṛgveda the Sarasvatī is a great river and goddess, “best of mothers, best of rivers, best of goddesses” (ambitame nadītame devitame sarasvati, 2.41.16). One hymn (7.95.2) describes her as flowing pure “from the mountains to the samudra”; whether samudra here means the sea or another great body of water is debated. In later Vedic texts the river is described as disappearing in the sands at a place called Vinaśana (“the disappearing”), and rites were performed along its course. In the Mahābhārata, Balarāma makes a pilgrimage along the Sarasvatī, and in later tradition the river is said to flow underground to join the Gaṅgā and Yamunā invisibly at Prayāga.

    9.2.2 Identification¶

    Since the nineteenth century most scholars have identified the Sarasvatī of the later Vedic texts with the Ghaggar–Hakra, a now seasonal river system that flows from the Siwalik hills through Haryana and Rajasthan and into Cholistan in Pakistan, where its dry bed continues towards the Indus. Many sites of the Harappan civilisation lie along this system. Its small modern tributary, the Sarsuti, preserves the name. Some scholars have instead identified the Ṛgvedic Sarasvatī, at least in its oldest hymns, with the Helmand in Afghanistan, whose Avestan name Haraxvaitī corresponds to Sarasvatī.

    9.2.3 What the Geological Evidence Shows¶

    Satellite images and field surveys have mapped a broad palaeochannel along the Ghaggar–Hakra, showing that a large river once flowed there. The crucial question is when, and from what source. Recent studies of the sediments have given a clearer answer than was available a generation ago.

    • Dating of sand grains in the palaeochannel indicates that it carried sediment from the high Himalaya during the late Pleistocene, when it was probably fed by the Sutlej or the Yamunā, and that this connection had been lost well before the Harappan period (Clift et al. 2012).

    • A study of the landscape of the Harappan period concluded that the Ghaggar–Hakra was then fed by the monsoon, not by glaciers, and that the Harappans settled along a river whose seasonal floods made agriculture possible (Giosan et al. 2012).

    • A later study found that the Sutlej abandoned this course around eight thousand years ago, so that the Harappan towns grew up along a former river valley that still held monsoon-fed flows and groundwater rather than a great glacier-fed river (Singh et al. 2017).

    Some Indian geologists, notably K. S. Valdiya, have argued for a larger, Himalayan-fed river persisting into the Holocene (Valdiya 2002), and the debate is not closed. The most widely read statement of that view, Michel Danino’s The Lost River, combines the palaeochannels seen from satellites, the distribution of Harappan sites and the Vedic descriptions into a single story of a great river that dried in stages, its last waters failing near the end of the mature Harappan period (Danino 2010). The book explains why the popular view persists: each line of evidence seems to confirm the others. It parts from the sediment studies on the decisive point of timing, since those studies date the loss of the Himalayan supply thousands of years before the Harappan cities. But the popular claim that a mighty, glacier-fed Sarasvatī flowed until about 1900 BCE and that its drying caused the end of the Harappan cities is not supported by the recent sediment studies. The decline of the Harappan cities had several causes, of which changing rainfall and river behaviour were probably important ones (Wright 2010).

    The Sarasvatī Debate in Summary

    • Textual: the Vedic Sarasvatī is a great river in the Ṛgveda and a disappearing one in later texts.

    • Identification: usually with the Ghaggar–Hakra; some scholars propose the Helmand for the oldest hymns.

    • Geology: a Himalayan-fed river flowed in the Ghaggar–Hakra valley in the late Pleistocene; by the Harappan period the river was monsoon-fed.

    • Open questions: the exact timing of river shifts, the meaning of the Ṛgvedic descriptions, and the relation between the hymns and the Harappan landscape.

    9.3 The Gaṅgā¶

    9.3.1 Physical Geography¶

    The Gaṅgā rises at Gaumukh, the snout of the Gangotri glacier in the Garhwal Himalaya, at nearly 4,000 m; this headstream is called the Bhāgīrathī. At Devaprayāg it is joined by the Alakanandā, which has gathered the waters of several tributaries at a series of confluences (Vishnuprayag, Nandaprayag, Karnaprayag and Rudraprayag) on the way. The combined river leaves the mountains at Haridvār and flows some 2,500 km in all across the plains, joined by the Yamunā at Prayāga and by the Ghaghara, Gandak, Son and Kosi further east, before dividing into the distributaries of the Bengal delta: the Hugli to the west and the Padmā, which joins the Brahmaputra, to the east. Its delta, with the Sundarbans mangrove forests, is the largest in the world.

    9.3.2 The River Goddess¶

    The descent of the Gaṅgā is told in the Rāmāyaṇa (Bālakāṇḍa) and the Mahābhārata: King Bhagīratha brings her down from heaven to purify the ashes of his ancestors, and Śiva breaks her fall in his matted hair. The name Bhāgīrathī preserves the story, as Jāhnavī recalls the sage Jahnu who swallowed and released the river. Every city on the river’s course has sacred bathing places, and the confluences of the upper river are tīrthas in their own right (Eck 2012).

    9.3.3 River Goddesses at the Temple Door¶

    From the Gupta period onward, many temple doorways in north and central India carry figures of the river goddesses Gaṅgā and Yamunā, Gaṅgā standing on a makara (a crocodile-like water creature) and Yamunā on a tortoise. Placed at the threshold of the sanctum, they purify those who enter, as bathing in the rivers purifies the pilgrim. Gupta and post-Gupta examples survive at Udayagiri near Vidiśā and at Deogarh, and the motif became a standard part of temple architecture. Through it the sacred geography of the two great rivers of the Middle Country was carried into temples throughout the land (Eck 2012).

    9.4 The Indus and the Brahmaputra¶

    9.4.1 The Indus¶

    The Indus (Sanskrit Sindhu) rises in western Tibet, not far from Mānasarovar, flows north-west through Ladakh and Gilgit, turns south through the mountains and enters the plains of the Punjab, where it is joined by the five rivers of the Punjab (the Jhelum, Chenab, Ravi, Beas and Sutlej) before flowing through Sindh to the Arabian Sea. It is some 3,000 km long. The first cities of the subcontinent grew up in its basin, and the hymns of the Ṛgveda were composed there. From its name come the words Hindu, India and Hindustan (Chapter 1). Since 1947 most of its course has lain in Pakistan, and the sharing of its waters between India and Pakistan has been governed by the Indus Waters Treaty of 1960.

    9.4.2 The Brahmaputra¶

    The Brahmaputra rises as the Yarlung Tsangpo in south-western Tibet, flows east for more than 1,000 km along the northern side of the Himālaya, turns south through a deep gorge round the eastern end of the range, and enters Assam as the Siang (Dihang). It flows west through the Assam valley and then south into Bangladesh, where it joins the Gaṅgā. Whether the Tsangpo and the Brahmaputra were the same river was one of the great geographical questions of the nineteenth century, and the explorer Kinthup was sent to answer it (Chapter 14).

    The Brahmaputra, “son of Brahmā”, is one of the few rivers in Indian tradition regarded as male. Its valley was the ancient kingdom of Prāgjyotiṣa or Kāmarūpa; the temple of Kāmākhyā stands on a hill above the river at Guwahati (Chapter 4), and the Paraśurāma Kuṇḍa on the Lohit in Arunachal Pradesh draws pilgrims for a winter bathing festival. Majuli, an island in the river, is the centre of the Vaiṣṇava monasteries (satra) founded in the tradition of Śaṅkaradeva (traditionally 1449–1568), the Assamese saint who also wrote and staged devotional dramas. The river’s floods and shifting channels make its valley one of the most dynamic landscapes of the subcontinent.

    9.5 The Rivers of the Peninsula¶

    Six of the major rivers of the peninsula are summarised in Table 9.1. Their lengths are rounded, and published figures differ by several per cent according to the point taken as the source and the channel measured through the delta.

    9.5.1 The Narmadā¶

    The Narmadā is unusual among large Indian rivers in flowing west. In religious tradition it is one of the holiest of rivers. A verse quoted in the Purāṇic praises of the river says that the Sarasvatī purifies in three days, the Yamunā in seven, the Gaṅgā at once, and the Narmadā at the mere sight of it. Stones from its bed, smoothed by the current, are worshipped as liṅgas (bāṇaliṅga). The Narmadā parikramā, in which pilgrims walk from the source to the sea along one bank and back along the other, is described in Chapter 4. In recent decades the river has also been the scene of the conflict over the Sardar Sarovar dam and the displacement of villages, much of it Adivasi land, that it caused.

    9.5.2 The Godāvarī and Kṛṣṇā¶

    The Godāvarī rises at Tryambakeshwar near Nashik, at one of the twelve jyotirliṅgas, and flows east across the Deccan to the Bay of Bengal. Nashik is one of the four sites of the Kumbh Melā, and the river’s bathing festival (puṣkaram) is held every twelve years when Jupiter enters Leo. The Kṛṣṇā and its tributary the Tuṅgabhadrā water the central Deccan; the capital of Vijayanagara stood on the Tuṅgabhadrā.

    9.5.3 The Kāverī¶

    The Kāverī rises in the hills of Kodagu and flows through Karnataka into the plains of Tamil Nadu, where its delta was the agricultural heartland of the Cōḻa kingdom. The Tamil epic Cilappatikāram opens in Pukār (Kāvirippūmpaṭṭiṉam), the port at the river’s mouth, and celebrates the river. The sharing of its water has been the subject of a long dispute between the states of Karnataka and Tamil Nadu, settled by a tribunal award in 2007 and a Supreme Court judgment in 2018.

    9.5.4 The Vaikai and the Tamil Rivers¶

    Tamil poetry has its own rivers. The Paripāṭal, one of the Caṅkam anthologies, contains songs in praise of the river Vaikai at Madurai, describing the people of the city going out to bathe and play in the floodwaters, with music, ornaments and quarrels between lovers. The Kāverī, the Tāmraparṇī of the Pāṇḍya country and the Periyār of Kerala appear in poems, inscriptions and later māhātmyas. In Tamil tradition the Kāverī flood festival (āṭipperukku), celebrated in the month of Āṭi (July–August) when the river rises, honours the river as the source of the delta’s prosperity.

    9.6 Moving Rivers¶

    The rivers of the northern plains do not stay in one place. Carrying huge loads of sediment from the Himālaya, they build up their beds and periodically break out into new courses. The Kosi in Bihar has moved westward by more than a hundred kilometres over the last few centuries and broke its embankment disastrously in 2008. The Gaṅgā and the Brahmaputra have shifted their lower courses many times; in the late eighteenth century the Brahmaputra moved into a new channel (the Jamuna) in Bengal, and the main flow of the Gaṅgā shifted from the Bhāgīrathī–Hugli eastward towards the Padmā, which contributed to the silting of western Bengal’s ports. The Indus delta, too, has moved and changed.

    These movements explain many features of the historical geography of the plains: abandoned river beds and oxbow lakes, towns that once stood on a river and now stand inland, ports that silted up, and the frequent changes in the course of the boundary-rivers of kingdoms and districts. They also explain why the identification of ancient river names and courses—the Sarasvatī is the best-known case, but by no means the only one—requires the help of geology as well as of texts.

    9.7 Rivers as Boundaries¶

    Rivers have often marked political frontiers. In the seventh century the Narmadā became the boundary between the kingdom of Harṣa in the north and that of the Cālukya king Pulakeśin II in the Deccan, after Harṣa’s attempt to cross it failed. In the fourteenth to sixteenth centuries the fertile land between the Kṛṣṇā and the Tuṅgabhadrā, the Raichur Doab, was fought over again and again by the kings of Vijayanagara and the Bahmani and later Deccan sultans. In 1809 the Treaty of Amritsar fixed the Sutlej as the boundary between the Sikh kingdom of Ranjit Singh and the territories under British protection. In 1947 the boundary drawn by the Radcliffe commission between India and Pakistan cut across the rivers of the Punjab and the deltas of Bengal, dividing irrigation systems and leaving questions of water sharing that were addressed, for the Indus basin, in the treaty of 1960.

    Rivers make awkward boundaries, because they move (see above) and because the people on both banks usually belong to a single economy and culture. Ferry towns, bridges and fords were places of exchange as well as of control. In Indian tradition, too, the crossing of a great river often marks a change of region, and the names of many regions—Antarvedī (the land between the Gaṅgā and the Yamunā), Pañcanada or Punjab (“five rivers”), Doāb itself (Persian, “two waters”)—are drawn from rivers.

    9.8 Water Management¶

    9.8.1 Texts¶

    The Arthaśāstra encourages the king to build water works (setu), distinguishing those that use naturally available water from those to which water is brought, and sets out rules for irrigation charges and for penalties for damaging embankments and channels (Olivelle 2013). The lexicographers distinguish lands watered by rivers (nadīmātṛka, “having a river for mother”) from lands that depend on rain (devamātṛka, “having the god for mother”).

    The Arthaśāstra also describes a rain gauge (varṣamāna), a vessel placed in front of the royal storehouse, and gives the expected amount of rain for different kinds of land and regions in units of droṇa, so that the superintendent of agriculture could decide which crops to sow (Arthaśāstra 2.5 and 2.24; (Olivelle 2013)). It is one of the earliest known prescriptions for measuring rainfall; how far it was ever put into practice is not known. Varāhamihira later describes the measurement of rain in an āḍhaka vessel (Section 3.4).

    9.8.2 The Sudarśana Lake¶

    One of the best documented water works of ancient India is the Sudarśana reservoir near Girnar in Gujarat. The rock inscription of the Western Kṣatrapa ruler Rudradāman at Junagadh (150 CE) records that the lake had been made by Puṣyagupta, provincial governor under Candragupta Maurya, provided with conduits under Aśoka, burst in a great storm, and been rebuilt by Rudradāman’s minister. A later inscription of the Gupta emperor Skandagupta on the same rock records a further breach and repair in 455–56. Together they give a history of a single reservoir over more than seven centuries.

    9.8.3 Tanks and Anicuts in South India¶

    In much of peninsular India, where rivers are seasonal, agriculture depended on tanks (ēri in Tamil): earthen embankments across shallow valleys that stored the monsoon rains, often in chains in which the overflow from one tank fed the next. Pallava and Cōḻa inscriptions record gifts for building and repairing tanks and the work of village committees responsible for them; the inscriptions of Uttaramērūr from the reign of the Cōḻa king Parāntaka I (early tenth century) describe a committee for tanks among others.

    The Kallaṇai or “Grand Anicut” on the Kāverī, traditionally attributed to the early Cōḻa king Karikāla, is a masonry weir that diverts water into the channels of the delta. The tradition of its ancient origin is strong, though the structure has been repaired and rebuilt many times; in the 1830s the British engineer Arthur Cotton studied it and used its principles when he built new works on the Kāverī system and later on the Godāvarī.

    9.8.4 Reservoirs and Channels of the Deccan Kingdoms¶

    The kingdoms of the Deccan built large water works. The rulers of Vijayanagara (fourteenth to sixteenth centuries) built weirs across the Tuṅgabhadrā and long channels to irrigate the land round their capital, some of which are still in use, as well as tanks and aqueducts within the city. In Telangana, tank systems built under the Kākatīyas, such as the great lakes at Ramappa and Pakhal, stored monsoon water for irrigation; and the Qutb Shāhī rulers of Golconda built the Hussain Sagar lake at Hyderabad (1562). Such works show the continuity of hydraulic expertise across dynasties of different religions and regions.

    9.8.5 Water in the Desert¶

    In the arid west, where rivers are few and rainfall scanty and uncertain, communities developed ways of harvesting and storing every drop: village ponds (nāḍī, talāb), covered underground cisterns (kuṇḍ or ṭāṅkā) fed by carefully prepared catchment surfaces, earthen check-dams (johaḍ), and stepwells (Chapter 8). The town of Jaisalmer depended on the Gaḍīsar lake, an artificial reservoir built in the fourteenth century and surrounded by shrines and steps. Such systems required collective maintenance and rules for use, and their decay in the twentieth century, and their revival by voluntary organisations in parts of Rajasthan, have been much discussed in debates on water policy.

    Modern Relevance: Rivers Today

    Many of India’s sacred rivers are now heavily polluted and over-extracted, and the reverence in which they are held has not by itself protected them. Government programmes such as the National Mission for Clean Ganga (Namami Gange, launched in 2014) appeal to that reverence. Disputes over water between states, dams and the displacement they cause, and the effects of climate change on Himalayan glaciers and monsoon rainfall all show that the management of rivers remains one of the central geographical questions of the subcontinent.
