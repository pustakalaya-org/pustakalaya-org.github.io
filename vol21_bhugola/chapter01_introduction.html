    Chapter 1: Bhūgola-Vidyā: India’s Geographical Imagination¶

    mātā bhūmiḥ putro ahaṃ pṛthivyāḥ
    “The Earth is my mother; I am the son of the Earth.”

    — Atharvaveda (Śaunaka) 12.1.12, the Bhūmi Sūkta

    The people of the Indian subcontinent have known, named, crossed and venerated their land in many different ways. The Vedic poets praised rivers and the Earth; the authors of the Purāṇas built an immense symbolic cosmos of ring-shaped continents around a golden central mountain; astronomers from Āryabhaṭa onwards treated the earth as a sphere and worked out latitudes and longitude differences; administrators described districts, roads and revenue; pilgrims, merchants, monks and sailors carried a practical knowledge of routes, seasons and harbours across South Asia and far beyond it. This volume, the twenty-first in the series Bhāratīya Jñāna Paramparā, surveys these traditions, together with the accounts of India left by foreign visitors and the modern survey that eventually mapped the subcontinent trigonometrically.

    The aim is to describe these traditions accurately, in their own terms and in the light of current scholarship. Two temptations have to be resisted. One is the older colonial claim that pre-modern Indians had no interest in geography; the other is the more recent habit of reading modern science back into ancient texts, as if the Purāṇic continents “encoded” the modern map of the world or as if an ancient value for the earth’s size could be checked to the kilometre. Neither is supported by the evidence. What the evidence does show is a set of distinct and often sophisticated ways of thinking about space, place and travel, which deserve study as they actually were.

    1.1 What Is Bhūgola-Vidyā?¶

    The Sanskrit compound bhūgola joins bhū (earth) and gola (ball, sphere). It is a term of the astronomical (jyotiḥśāstra) tradition: in the Siddhānta texts the golādhyāya, the “chapter on the sphere”, explains the spherical earth set within the celestial sphere (khagola or bhagola) and the geometry that connects them. Bhāskara II (b. 1114 CE) gives the name Bhuvanakośa (“treasury of the world”) to the section of his Golādhyāya that sets the Purāṇic world-picture beside the astronomers’ spherical earth (Plofker 2009; Pingree 1981). In this narrow sense bhūgola-vidyā is mathematical and astronomical geography: the shape and size of the earth, latitude (akṣa), longitude difference (deśāntara), the length of daylight at different places, and the conversion of terrestrial distances into angular ones.

    This volume uses the term more broadly, for at least four overlapping bodies of knowledge:

    1. Mathematical and astronomical geography: the spherical earth, its size, the reference meridian through Laṅkā and Ujjayinī, latitudes and longitude differences (Chapter 3).

    2. Cosmography: the Purāṇic, Jain and Buddhist pictures of the universe, with Jambūdvīpa, Mount Meru and the concentric continents and oceans (Chapter 2). These are cosmologies, not maps of the physical earth.

    3. Sacred geography: tīrthas, kṣetras, pilgrimage circuits and the networks that linked them, in Brahmanical, Buddhist, Jain, Sikh and Muslim traditions (Chapter 4).

    4. Practical, administrative and trade geography: regions and peoples, roads, passes, rivers, ports and seasons, as known to rulers, revenue officials, merchants and sailors (Chapters 8–13).

    Bhūgola, Khagola, Bhuvanakośa

    Bhūgola: the earth-sphere of the astronomers.
    Khagola (also bhagola): the celestial sphere, on which the stars and planets appear to move.
    Bhuvanakośa: “treasury of the world”; the name given in the Purāṇas to their descriptions of the cosmos and its regions, and adopted by Bhāskara II for his discussion of the same material.

    1.2 Sources of Indian Geographical Knowledge¶

    The sources are spread across genres, languages and some three thousand years. None of them is a “geography” in the modern sense; each has its own purpose, and its geographical content has to be read with that purpose in mind.

    1.2.1 Vedic and Epic Sources¶

    The Ṛgveda (core composed c. 1500–1200 BCE) is set in the north-west of the subcontinent, in the region of the Indus and its tributaries. Its hymn in praise of rivers, the Nadīstuti (Ṛgveda 10.75), names rivers from the Gaṅgā in the east to the Kubhā (Kabul) in the west (Chapter 9). Later Vedic texts (c. 1200–500 BCE) show the centre of Vedic culture moving east into the Kuru–Pañcāla country of the Gaṅgā–Yamunā Doab and then towards Kosala and Videha (Witzel 1995). The Bhūmi Sūkta (Atharvaveda 12.1), a hymn of sixty-three verses to the Earth, describes her hills, plains, rivers and peoples “of many speeches”.

    Atharvaveda (Śaunaka) 12.1.1

    satyaṃ bṛhad ṛtam ugraṃ dīkṣā tapo brahma yajñaḥ pṛthivīṃ dhārayanti
    sā no bhūtasya bhavyasya patny uruṃ lokaṃ pṛthivī naḥ kṛṇotu

    “Great truth, mighty order, consecration, austerity, sacred formulation and sacrifice uphold the Earth. May she, the mistress of what has been and what will be, make wide space for us.”

    The two Sanskrit epics contain long lists of peoples, rivers and mountains: the geographical sections of the Bhīṣmaparvan of the Mahābhārata (the Jambūkhaṇḍa and Bhūmiparvan), the Tīrthayātrāparvan of its third book, and the Kiṣkindhākāṇḍa of the Rāmāyaṇa. These passages combine real names with mythical ones; they are valuable evidence for how the land was imagined and named, but they were not written as surveys, and their details cannot be checked on the ground as if they were (Section 1.3).

    1.2.2 The Purāṇas¶

    Most of the major Purāṇas contain a bhuvanakośa: a description of the universe, of the seven ring-continents and oceans, of Jambūdvīpa and its nine regions, and of Bhāratavarṣa with its mountains, rivers and peoples. The fullest and most often quoted versions are in the Viṣṇu, Vāyu, Brahmāṇḍa, Matsya, Mārkaṇḍeya and Bhāgavata Purāṇas; they overlap heavily but differ in detail. Willibald Kirfel’s comparative study of these texts remains the standard analysis (Kirfel 1920); on the Purāṇas generally see Rocher (1986). The Purāṇic māhātmya literature, which praises particular sacred places, is a second large body of geographical material (Chapter 4).

    1.2.3 The Arthaśāstra and Administrative Literature¶

    The Arthaśāstra attributed to Kauṭilya is a composite text which, in its present form, is best dated c. 50 BCE–300 CE; its traditional attribution to Cāṇakya, minister of Candragupta Maurya (c. 300 BCE), cannot be confirmed (Olivelle 2013). Its second book describes the settlement of new land, the hierarchy of administrative centres, the storehouse, mines, roads, ferries and the work of the superintendents of agriculture, shipping and trade. It is a prescriptive text, describing an ideal administration, but its vocabulary of land, water and routes is a precious record of practical geographical concepts. Its scheme for settling the countryside, its frontier posts and its theory of the circle of states are discussed with the other political geography in Chapter 12.

    1.2.4 Grammar¶

    Pāṇini’s grammar, the Aṣṭādhyāyī (c. 4th century BCE), is not a geographical work, but because it gives rules for forming the names of places, peoples and their products, it preserves a large number of geographical names from the north-west and the Gangetic plain. Tradition, recorded by Xuanzang, makes Pāṇini a native of Śalātura near the Indus, in Gandhāra. One rule distinguishes the names of wells on the northern bank of the river Vipāś (the Beas) from those on other banks (4.2.74), a sign of the grammarian’s attention to local usage. V. S. Agrawala collected the cultural and geographical information in the grammar in India as Known to Pāṇini (Agrawala 1953).

    1.2.5 Astronomical and Encyclopaedic Texts¶

    The astronomical Siddhāntas, from Āryabhaṭa’s Āryabhaṭīya (499 CE) to Bhāskara II’s Siddhāntaśiromaṇi (1150 CE), contain the mathematical geography discussed in Chapter 3. Varāhamihira’s Bṛhatsaṃhitā (6th century CE) has a chapter, the Kūrmavibhāga (“division of the tortoise”), which arranges the regions and peoples of India in nine groups around the Middle Country, for use in astrological prognostication (Bhat 1981; Sircar 1971). The poetician Rājaśekhara’s Kāvyamīmāṃsā (early 10th century) includes a chapter on the regions of India for the use of poets (Section 12.2).

    1.2.6 Inscriptions¶

    Inscriptions are among the most reliable geographical sources because they are dated and fixed in place. Aśoka’s edicts (r. c. 268–232 BCE) name the peoples on the frontiers of his empire and five Hellenistic kings in the west; their find-spots, from Kandahar to Karnataka, outline the empire itself. Samudragupta’s Allahabad pillar inscription (4th century CE) lists kings of Āryāvarta, of the forest tracts and of Dakṣiṇāpatha. Thousands of later land-grants define village boundaries by streams, trees, tanks and neighbouring villages, and are the closest thing to local maps that survives from early medieval India (Sircar 1971).

    1.2.7 Travel and Pilgrimage Literature¶

    Foreign visitors—Greek, Chinese, Arab, Persian, Central Asian and European—left accounts of India (Chapter 6). Indian travellers left fewer formal travelogues, but there is a large literature of pilgrimage: the tīrthayātrā section of the Mahābhārata, the Purāṇic māhātmyas, the medieval digests on pilgrimage such as Lakṣmīdhara’s Tīrthavivecanakāṇḍa (12th century), Jain tīrthamālās and Jinaprabhasūri’s Vividhatīrthakalpa (14th century), Sikh accounts of Guru Nānak’s journeys, and, from the eighteenth century, Persian and Urdu travel narratives by Indians who went to Mecca, Iran or Europe (Chapters 4 and 13).

    1.3 Epic Geography¶

    The two Sanskrit epics, composed and expanded over many centuries (roughly 400 BCE–400 CE), contain the earliest long descriptions of the land as a whole. In the Bhīṣmaparvan of the Mahābhārata, before the great battle, the bard Sañjaya describes the earth to the blind king Dhṛtarāṣṭra: first the cosmic geography of Jambūdvīpa and Meru, then Bhāratavarṣa, with its seven principal mountain ranges, a long catalogue of rivers, and lists of the peoples of the Middle Country, the north, the south and the borderlands. In the Sabhāparvan, the Pāṇḍava brothers’ campaigns of conquest in the four directions and the peoples who bring tribute to Yudhiṣṭhira’s royal consecration provide further lists (Section 12.3.4). The Tīrthayātrāparvan adds the sacred places (Chapter 4).

    In the Rāmāyaṇa, Rāma’s exile takes him from Ayodhyā across the Gaṅgā at Śṛṅgaverapura, to Citrakūṭa, through the forests of Daṇḍaka to Pañcavaṭī on the Godāvarī, and then south to Kiṣkindhā and the sea. In the Kiṣkindhākāṇḍa, Sugrīva sends search parties in the four directions with descriptions of the lands they will pass. The places on Rāma’s route became pilgrimage sites, and the identification of others—above all Kiṣkindhā and Laṅkā—has been debated. The epics are therefore sources for how the land was named and imagined, and many of their names can be identified; but they were not written as geographical surveys, and their descriptions should not be treated as such.

    1.4 Cosmography and Observational Geography¶

    A recurring difficulty is the relation between two very different kinds of description. Purāṇic cosmography imagines the earth as a flat disc of concentric ring-continents (dvīpa) and ring-oceans (samudra) round Mount Meru, with Bhāratavarṣa as the southern region of the central continent, Jambūdvīpa. Its dimensions run to hundreds of thousands of yojanas, and its outer oceans are of sugar-cane juice, wine, ghee, curds, milk and sweet water. It is a cosmological and religious picture, not a map of the physical world, and attempts to identify its continents with Asia, Africa or the Americas have no basis.

    The astronomers of the Siddhānta tradition, by contrast, treated the earth as a small sphere at the centre of the celestial sphere and calculated with it. Their geography was mathematical but thin: they needed latitudes and longitude differences for a limited number of places in order to compute local times of sunrise, eclipses and planetary positions.

    Cosmography and Geography

    Cosmography (Purāṇic, Jain and Buddhist traditions): a picture of the whole universe, including heavens and underworlds, organised by religious meaning and by number.
    Mathematical geography (Siddhānta tradition): the earth as a sphere, described by angles and calculated distances.
    Descriptive and practical geography (epics, Purāṇic lists, inscriptions, administrative and travel literature): named regions, peoples, rivers, routes and places.

    The two pictures were not easily reconciled, and Indian astronomers knew it. Their attempts to fit Meru and the continents into the spherical earth, al-Bīrūnī’s criticism of both sides and the debate that continued into the seventeenth century are described in Section 2.9. It is more accurate to speak of a long and sometimes uneasy coexistence than of a harmony in which the two never contradicted each other (Minkowski 2001).

    1.5 Why Study Indian Geographical Knowledge?¶

    1.5.1 Correcting Two Kinds of Distortion¶

    Colonial writers often asserted that Indians had no sense of geography or history. The work of historians of cartography, above all Joseph E. Schwartzberg’s long contribution on South Asian cartography to the History of Cartography (Harley and Woodward 1992) and his Historical Atlas of South Asia (Schwartzberg 1978), of Susan Gole on Indian maps (Gole 1989), of Irfan Habib on Mughal geography (Habib 1982), and of Diana Eck on sacred geography (Eck 2012), has shown that this was false. At the same time, Schwartzberg stressed that relatively few pre-modern Indian maps survive, most from after 1500, and that Indian cartography had purposes and conventions of its own. Honest scholarship has to avoid both the colonial dismissal and its mirror image, the claim that ancient Indians knew everything that modern geography knows.

    1.5.2 Sacred Geography and Shared Space¶

    The networks of pilgrimage places that cover the subcontinent created, over many centuries, a shared sense of a land that extended far beyond any single kingdom. Eck argues that this “imagined landscape” was one of the main ways in which people in pre-modern India understood the subcontinent as a whole (Eck 2012). Pilgrimage networks were also plural: Buddhists, Jains, Sikhs and Muslims had their own sacred places and routes, often overlapping with Hindu ones, and access to many sacred sites was restricted by caste and gender in ways that must not be forgotten (Chapter 4).

    1.5.3 Practical Knowledge¶

    Much of the most important geographical knowledge was never written down: the knowledge of herders, cultivators, boatmen, caravaneers, fishermen and pilots. It is known mainly through its effects (routes, tank systems, sailing seasons) and through the observations of outsiders and modern ethnographers. It deserves respect, but claims for it should be made on the basis of evidence rather than assumed.

    1.6 The Vocabulary of Land, Water and Season¶

    Sanskrit has a rich vocabulary for landforms and waters. Many of these words are already Vedic; the lexicon Amarakośa (mid-first millennium CE) groups them in its sections on earth, towns, mountains and waters. Table 1.1 gives a selection with their usual meanings. Like words in any language, they overlap; they were not a technical classification.

    Table 1.1. Some Sanskrit words for land and water

    śikhara, śṛṅga peak srotas stream, current
    droṇī valley saṅgama confluence
    sthalī dry land, upland kūla, taṭa bank, shore

    1.6.1 Land Types in Āyurveda¶

    The medical classics classify regions (deśa) as jāṅgala (dry, open country with sparse trees and little water), ānūpa (wet, marshy country) and sādhāraṇa (“common” or mixed). The classification concerns health: jāṅgala land is described as healthy and ānūpa land as productive of disorders of phlegm and wind. Francis Zimmermann showed that the word jāṅgala, from which English “jungle” derives, originally meant dry, open savanna, almost the opposite of its modern sense (Zimmermann 1987). The Arthaśāstra also distinguishes jāṅgala and ānūpa land in its rules on settlement and rainfall.

    1.6.2 The Six Seasons¶

    Indian texts divide the year into six seasons (ṛtu) of two months each (Table 1.2). The months are lunar-solar, so the correspondence with the Gregorian calendar is approximate.

    Table 1.2. The six seasons (ṛtu) and their months
    Ṛtu Months Approximate period and character
    Vasanta (spring) Caitra, Vaiśākha mid-March to mid-May; flowering
    Grīṣma (hot season) Jyeṣṭha, Āṣāḍha mid-May to mid-July; heat, then first rains
    Varṣā (rains) Śrāvaṇa, Bhādrapada mid-July to mid-September; the monsoon
    Śarad (autumn) Āśvina, Kārttika mid-September to mid-November; clear skies, harvest
    Hemanta (cool season) Mārgaśīrṣa, Pauṣa mid-November to mid-January
    Śiśira (cold season) Māgha, Phālguna mid-January to mid-March

    Bhagavadgītā 10.35cd

    māsānāṃ mārgaśīrṣo ’ham ṛtūnāṃ kusumākaraḥ

    “Of months I am Mārgaśīrṣa; of seasons, the maker of flowers [spring].”

    The rains governed travel. Buddhist and Jain monks were required to stop wandering during the rainy season and to stay in one place (the Buddhist vassa, the Jain cāturmāsya); kings were advised to set out on campaigns after the rains, in autumn; merchants and sailors planned their journeys round the monsoon winds, whose calendar is set out in Chapter 11. The Arthaśāstra’s rain gauge is described with the other texts on water in Chapter 9.

    1.7 Cities and Settlements¶

    The first cities of the subcontinent belong to the Indus or Harappan civilisation, whose mature urban phase is dated c. 2600–1900 BCE, with earlier and later phases extending from c. 3300 to c. 1300 BCE. Harappan sites of all phases are spread from Shortugai on the Amu Darya in northern Afghanistan to Daimabad in Maharashtra (a Late Harappan site) and from Sutkagen Dor on the Makran coast to Alamgirpur near Delhi. The Harappans left no deciphered texts, so their geographical knowledge can only be inferred from what they built: streets broadly aligned to the cardinal directions, raised mounds, covered drains, and at Dholavira in Kutch a series of reservoirs cut into the rock to store the scarce water of an arid island. Their settlements spread across river plains, coasts, desert margins and hill valleys, and they traded with Mesopotamia and the Gulf by sea (Kenoyer 1998; Possehl 2002; Wright 2010). The Harappan port at Lothal is discussed with the coasts in Chapter 11, and the question whether the Ghaggar–Hakra, along which many Harappan sites lie, was the Vedic Sarasvatī in Chapter 9.

    Urban life returned to the subcontinent in the Gangetic plain from about the sixth century BCE, in what historians call the second urbanisation. Cities such as Rājagṛha, Pāṭaliputra, Śrāvastī, Kauśāmbī, Vārāṇasī and Ujjayinī, many of them fortified with ramparts and moats, grew at river crossings and on routes, and their names fill Buddhist and Jain literature. In the far south, the Caṅkam poems describe the capitals of the three Tamil kingdoms—Madurai of the Pāṇḍyas, Uṟaiyūr and the port of Pukār of the Cōḻas, and Vañci of the Cēras—with their markets, streets and harbours (Thapar 2002). The ideal city plans of the Arthaśāstra and the architectural treatises are described in Chapter 5.

    1.8 Names for the Land¶

    The subcontinent has been called by many names, and their history is itself a piece of historical geography. Jambudvīpa, the central continent of the cosmologies, is used by Aśoka in the third century BCE for the land in which he spread the Dharma. Bhārata or Bhāratavarṣa, “the land of the descendants of Bharata”, is the name defined in the Purāṇas (Chapter 2) and remains the Sanskrit and Hindi name of the country.

    Other names came from outside and from the Indus. The Sanskrit Sindhu, “river” and the name of the Indus, became Hindu in Old Persian, in which the inscriptions of Darius I name a province “Hindush”; from the Persian form came Greek Indos for the river and India for the land beyond it, Arabic al-Hind, and Persian Hindūstān. The Chinese called the country Shendu, Tianzhu and later Yindu; Xuanzang explained the last name as coming from indu, the moon. The Constitution of India of 1950 uses two of these names together: “India, that is Bharat, shall be a Union of States.”

    1.9 Landscapes in Tamil Poetry¶

    A distinctive classification of landscapes comes from the classical Tamil poems of the Caṅkam anthologies (early centuries CE) and the grammar that describes them, the Tolkāppiyam. Love poetry (akam) is set in one of five landscapes or tiṇai, each named after a characteristic flower and each associated with a deity, a season and time of day, plants, animals, occupations and, above all, a phase of love (Table 1.3; (Ramanujan 1967; Zvelebil 1973)).

    Table 1.3. The five landscapes (tiṇai) of classical Tamil love poetry
    Tiṇai Landscape Associations
    kuṟiñci mountains the god Murukaṉ; night; the lovers’ union
    mullai forest and pasture Māyōṉ (Kṛṣṇa); evening in the rainy season; patient waiting
    marutam cultivated river plains Vēntaṉ (Indra); dawn; quarrels caused by the husband’s infidelity
    neytal seashore Varuṇaṉ; nightfall; anxious waiting and lament
    pālai parched wasteland no fixed region; midday in summer; separation and dangerous journeys

    The tiṇai system is a poetic convention, not a survey; a poem’s landscape is a code for its emotion. But the conventions rest on a precise observation of the different environments of the Tamil country—hills, forests, paddy fields, coasts and drought-stricken land—and of the plants, animals and ways of life that belong to each. The second great genre of Tamil poetry, puṟam (“exterior”), on war, kings and public life, also uses a set of tiṇai. In the tiṇai scheme, feeling, season and terrain are classified together, so that to name a hill flower is already to name a mood.

    1.10 Geography in the Regional Literatures¶

    Sanskrit is only one of the languages in which Indians described their land, and the literatures of the regional languages are often closer to everyday experience. In Tamil, besides the landscape poetry of the Caṅkam anthologies, the “guide poems” (āṟṟuppaṭai) of the Pattuppāṭṭu direct a bard along the road to a generous patron, describing the lands, peoples and food he will meet on the way, and the hymns of the Nāyaṉmār and Āḻvār name hundreds of temple towns (Chapter 4). The Marathi Līḷācaritra (late thirteenth century), a biography of Cakradhara, founder of the Mahānubhāva sect and one of the earliest works of Marathi prose, records the places he visited in such detail that later scholars have used it to reconstruct the geography of the region in his time. The Assamese buraṅjīs, chronicles of the Ahom kingdom of the Brahmaputra valley, record routes, rivers, forts and the movements of armies and embassies, and the Bengali maṅgalkāvyas follow merchants down the rivers to the sea (Chapter 11). From the thirteenth century histories, gazetteers and travel accounts in Persian, and later in Urdu, described provinces, routes and cities (Chapters 7 and 13). The geographical knowledge of the subcontinent was multilingual, and this volume can give only a sample of it.

    1.11 Social Space¶

    Geographical description in the Sanskrit sources is not socially neutral. The normative texts define the land where Brahmanical norms apply and treat regions beyond it as impure; the law books describe a village in which those regarded as “untouchable” (such as the caṇḍālas) were to live outside the settlement. Faxian, around 400 CE, observed that caṇḍālas lived apart and had to announce their approach (Chapter 6). In many parts of India, villages were divided into separate quarters by caste, with Dalit hamlets—the cēri of Tamil villages, for example—set apart from the main settlement, often with separate wells and paths. Access to roads, water and temples was unequal.

    This social geography belongs to the history of Indian space as much as the cosmologies of the Purāṇas or the calculations of the astronomers. It was also one of the main targets of social reformers from the nineteenth century onward: B. R. Ambedkar’s Mahad satyāgraha of 1927 was fought over the right of Dalits to draw water from a public tank, that is, over who might use a particular place (Olivelle 2005; Thapar 2002). The same questions of access return in the chapter on pilgrimage (Section 4.13).

    1.12 A Note on Method¶

    Scholarly Note: How This Volume Treats Its Sources

    • Dates are given as the range accepted in current scholarship; traditional dates are labelled as such. Thus the Buddha is placed c. 480–400 BCE (short chronology), Śaṅkara c. 700–750 CE (the conventional 788–820 is traditional), and the Arthaśāstra c. 50 BCE–300 CE.

    • Quotations from Sanskrit and other texts are given only where the wording and reference are well established. Verse numbers vary between editions.

    • Units: the yojana and krośa varied in length between texts and regions. Modern equivalents are given only as rough ranges, and calculations that depend on an exact value of the yojana are treated with caution (Chapter 3).

    • Comparisons with modern science are offered as comparisons. Ancient texts are not read as anticipating modern discoveries.

    • Maps in this volume are drawn on a simple frame of latitude and longitude, with a simplified coastline. Places are plotted at their modern positions; positions that are uncertain are marked as approximate, and no modern boundaries are drawn.

    1.13 Plan of the Volume¶

    Chapter 2 describes Purāṇic, Jain and Buddhist cosmography: Jambūdvīpa, Meru, the seven continents and oceans, and the world-picture of the Purāṇas, presented as cosmology. Chapter 3 turns to the spherical earth of the astronomers, the size of the earth, latitude and longitude. Chapter 4 examines sacred geography and pilgrimage, Hindu, Buddhist, Jain, Sikh and Muslim, including the Hajj from India. Chapter 5 surveys Indian maps and plans. Chapter 6 reads the accounts of foreign travellers from Megasthenes to Bernier and Tavernier, and Chapter 7 the geographical writing in Persian produced in India from the Delhi Sultanate to the early colonial period, with the Āʾīn-i Akbarī at its centre. Chapters 8–11 treat routes and trade, rivers, mountains, and coasts and islands. Chapter 12 turns to political and administrative geography: the janapadas and the normative regions of the early texts, the divisions of kingdoms in the inscriptions, the names of the historical regions, place-names, boundaries and frontiers, and the making of the linguistic states. Chapter 13 follows Indians abroad: merchants, monks, sailors and travellers. Chapter 14 describes the colonial surveys, including the Great Trigonometrical Survey and the Indian surveyors who worked for it, the growth of geography as a modern discipline, and the legacy of the earlier traditions in modern India. A gazetteer at the end of the volume gives the modern locations of the places most often named.

    Some subjects are treated more thinly than they deserve. The oral and written geographies of the Himalayan and north-eastern peoples appear only in passing; Sikh sacred geography is followed only as far as the janamsākhīs, the principal gurdwaras and the Kartarpur corridor; the painted narrative scrolls of Rajasthan (phaḍ) and other folk spatial narratives are left aside; and before 1800 the only women travellers whose journeys are described are Gulbadan Begum and, in tradition, Mīrābāī. These are limits of this survey, not of the traditions.

    Several neighbouring volumes of the series treat related subjects more fully: Indian astronomy, including the Siddhāntas used in Chapter 3, in Volume 12, Khagola Jyotiṣa; the Purāṇas as literature in Volume 7, Itihāsa–Purāṇa; Buddhist and Jain traditions, including the Chinese pilgrims’ accounts of Indian monasteries, in Volume 4, Buddha–Jina Prabhā; architecture and the vāstupuruṣamaṇḍala in Volume 16, Vāstu–Śilpa; trade, ports and seafaring in Volume 19, Vāṇijya and Samudra; and the knowledge of Adivasi and folk communities in Volume 23, Jana–Jāti.

    Further Reading¶

    On the historical geography of early India: Law (1954), Sircar (1971) and Cunningham (1871). On cartography: Harley and Woodward (1992), Schwartzberg (1978) and Gole (1989). On sacred geography: Eck (2012) and Bhardwaj (1973). On mathematical geography: Plofker (2009).
