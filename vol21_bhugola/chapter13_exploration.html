    Chapter 13: Indians Abroad: Merchants, Monks, Sailors and Travellers¶

    Indian geographical horizons were never limited to the subcontinent. Merchants and sailors from India traded across the Bay of Bengal and the Arabian Sea from at least the last centuries BCE; Buddhist monks carried texts and teachings to Central Asia, China and Tibet; Indian pilgrims went to Mecca; and from the eighteenth century Indians travelled to Europe and wrote about what they saw. This chapter follows these journeys. It also addresses some exaggerated claims: that Indian “colonies” were planted across Southeast Asia, or that Indian sailors reached the Americas.

    13.1 Southeast Asia¶

    13.1.1 Early Contacts¶

    Archaeology shows that the Bay of Bengal was crossed regularly by the late first millennium BCE. At sites such as Khao Sam Kaeo in peninsular Thailand, Indian-style pottery, beads of carnelian and agate made with Indian techniques, and other objects date from the fourth to second centuries BCE; at Óc Eo in the Mekong delta, a port of the kingdom the Chinese called Funan, Indian, Roman and Chinese objects were found together. The Pali Mahāniddesa, a canonical commentary of the early centuries CE, lists among the places that merchants visited in search of wealth Suvaṇṇabhūmi (“Land of Gold”), Takkola, Java and Tambapaṇṇi (Sri Lanka). Sanskrit sources speak also of Suvarṇadvīpa (“Island of Gold”). These names refer broadly to mainland and island Southeast Asia; their exact application varied and is still debated. A Sri Lankan Buddhist tradition holds that Aśoka sent the monks Soṇa and Uttara to Suvaṇṇabhūmi, which Burmese and Thai tradition each identify with their own land. Himanshu Prabha Ray has shown how closely the early sea routes were bound up with the spread of Buddhist monasteries and their lay patrons (Ray 1994).

    13.1.2 Sanskrit, Kingship and Temples¶

    From about the fourth and fifth centuries CE, Sanskrit inscriptions appear in Southeast Asia: the sacrificial-post inscriptions of King Mūlavarman at Kutai in Borneo, the inscriptions of Pūrṇavarman of Tārumānagara in western Java, and others in Champa and Cambodia. Rulers took Sanskrit names, patronised Brahmins and Buddhist monks, and built temples to Śiva, Viṣṇu and the Buddha. Over the following centuries this tradition produced Angkor, Borobudur, Prambanan and My Son.

    George Coedès called this process “Indianisation” and attributed it largely to Indian merchants and Brahmins (Coedès 1968). Later scholars have emphasised the initiative of Southeast Asian rulers, who selected and adapted Indian ideas to their own purposes, and the two-way nature of the exchange; the terms “localisation” and “convergence” are sometimes preferred. There is no evidence of Indian conquest or colonisation in Southeast Asia before the Cōḻa expedition of the eleventh century, and the older idea of “Indian colonies” has been abandoned (Kulke et al. 2009).

    13.1.3 Śrīvijaya and the Cōḻas¶

    Śrīvijaya, centred on Palembang in Sumatra, controlled the Straits of Malacca from the late seventh century and was a major Buddhist centre where, as Yijing reported, monks studied Sanskrit (Chapter 6). Its rulers had close ties with India: a copper-plate grant of the Pāla king Devapāla (ninth century) records the endowment of a monastery at Nālandā built by Bālaputradeva, king of Suvarṇadvīpa, and the Larger Leiden plates record the grant of a village by the Cōḻa king Rājarāja I to the Buddhist monastery that the Śailendra king of Śrīvijaya had built at Nāgapaṭṭiṉam.

    Relations changed under Rājarāja’s son Rājendra I (r. 1012–1044). Around 1025 a Cōḻa naval expedition attacked Śrīvijaya and a series of ports in Sumatra and the Malay peninsula; Rājendra’s inscriptions list Kaḍāram (Kedah), Śrīvijaya, Malaiyūr, Takkolam, Mānakkavāram (the Nicobars) and others. Historians debate the motives—whether the raid aimed to protect Tamil merchants’ access to the Straits and the China trade, to punish Śrīvijayan restrictions, or to display royal power—but agree that it was a raid rather than a lasting conquest (Kulke et al. 2009). Tamil merchant guilds were active in the region before and after: a Tamil inscription of the Maṇigrāmam at Takua Pa in southern Thailand dates from the ninth century, and a Tamil inscription of the “Five Hundred” merchant body, dated 1088, was found at Barus in Sumatra.

    13.1.4 Indian Merchants in China and the Straits¶

    Indian merchants also reached China. At Quanzhou, the great port of southern China in the Song and Yuan periods, a Tamil inscription dated 1281 records the founding of a Śiva temple by a Tamil merchant, and Hindu sculptures from temples of this period survive in the city. In the fifteenth century Malacca became the main entrepôt of the Straits; the Portuguese apothecary Tomé Pires, writing shortly after the Portuguese conquest of the city in 1511, reported that Gujarati merchants were the most numerous and important of the foreign traders there, alongside merchants from the Coromandel coast (the Keling) and Bengal. Indian cloth was the principal commodity exchanged for the spices of the archipelago.

    13.2 The Western Indian Ocean¶

    13.2.1 Socotra and the Red Sea¶

    Indian sailors and merchants were present in the western Indian Ocean in the Roman period. The Periplus reports that the island of Dioscorida (Socotra) was inhabited by a mixture of Arabs, Indians and Greeks (Casson 1989). In the cave of Hoq on Socotra, explorers in 2000–01 found more than two hundred inscriptions and drawings left by visiting sailors between about the first and the fifth centuries CE; most are in Indian Brāhmī script and Indian languages, many by sailors from Gujarat (Strauch 2012). At the Egyptian Red Sea ports of Berenike and Myos Hormos (Quseir al-Qadim), excavations have found sherds with short Tamil-Brāhmī inscriptions, Indian pottery and large quantities of black pepper (Tomber 2008).

    13.2.2 East Africa¶

    The Periplus lists Indian cloth, grain, rice, ghee, sesame oil and sugar among the goods brought to the Horn of Africa and the East African coast (Azania), and ivory, tortoise-shell and rhinoceros horn among the exports from there. In the medieval period the Swahili towns of the East African coast traded with Arabia, Persia and India. A recent study of ancient DNA from medieval Swahili burials found that their Asian ancestry came mainly from Persia, with a smaller contribution from India (Brielle et al. 2023). From the sixteenth century, and especially in the nineteenth, Gujarati merchants and financiers were established in Mombasa, Zanzibar and other East African ports (Pearson 2003).

    13.2.3 The Arabian Sea¶

    India’s links with the Gulf and Arabia are among the oldest of all: Harappan seals and beads have been found in Mesopotamia and the Gulf, and Mesopotamian texts of the later third millennium BCE name a land called Meluhha, usually identified with the Indus region. In the medieval period Indian ships, merchants and goods were familiar in Aden, Hormuz and the Red Sea, and Arab, Persian and Jewish merchants lived in the ports of Gujarat and Malabar. The documents of the Cairo Geniza include letters of Jewish merchants of the eleventh and twelfth centuries who traded between Egypt, Aden and India and settled on the Malabar coast.

    13.3 Central Asia and the Silk Roads¶

    Overland, the north-west of the subcontinent was part of a wider world that included Afghanistan, Bactria and the oasis cities of the Tarim basin. Under the Kuṣāṇas (first to third centuries CE) an empire stretched from Bactria to the Gangetic plain. Buddhist monasteries spread along the routes north of the Hindu Kush, and Indian scripts and languages travelled with them: Gāndhārī Prakrit in Kharoṣṭhī script was used for administration in the kingdom of Kroraina in the Tarim basin in the third and fourth centuries, and Sanskrit Buddhist manuscripts have been found at Gilgit, Bamiyan, Kucha, Turfan and Dunhuang (Neelis 2011). The colossal Buddhas of Bamiyan, destroyed in 2001, stood on the route between Bactria and Gandhāra.

    Indian merchants, monks and craftsmen took part in this traffic, along with Sogdian, Bactrian, Chinese and Persian traders. The knowledge it generated is preserved mainly in Chinese and Central Asian sources and in archaeology; Indian texts say comparatively little about the lands beyond the mountains. In later centuries the overland link continued through the trade of Multani and Punjabi merchants in Central Asia and Iran (Chapter 8).

    13.4 The Question of the Americas¶

    Some popular books claim that Indian sailors reached the Americas before Columbus. The arguments usually rest on the presence of certain plants in both hemispheres, on supposed resemblances between Mesoamerican and Asian art or calendars, or on speculative readings of texts. There is no secure evidence for such contact. Some plants did cross between the hemispheres before 1492—the sweet potato reached Polynesia from South America, and the bottle gourd appears to have spread naturally or with the earliest human settlers—but these cases involve Polynesian voyaging or natural dispersal, not India. No Indian artefact has been found in a securely pre-Columbian context in the Americas, nor any American artefact in pre-Columbian India. The question should be regarded as settled in the negative unless new evidence appears.

    13.5 Buddhist Monks Abroad¶

    The transmission of Buddhism from India to Central Asia, China, Korea, Japan, Tibet and Southeast Asia was carried in large part by monks who travelled in both directions. Indian and Central Asian monks went to China as translators; Chinese, Korean and later Tibetan monks came to India as pilgrims and students (Chapter 6).

    • Kumārajīva (conventionally 344–413), born in Kucha to an Indian father and a Kuchean mother, was carried off from Kucha by a Chinese general in the 380s, held for many years at Liangzhou, and reached Chang’an only in 401, where he became one of the greatest translators of Buddhist texts into Chinese.

    • Paramārtha (499–569), from Ujjayinī, reached China in 546 and translated works of the Yogācāra school.

    • Bodhidharma, the legendary founder of Chan (Zen) Buddhism in China, is said to have come from southern India around 500; his biography is largely legendary, and the stories linking him with martial arts are much later inventions.

    • Śāntarakṣita and, according to tradition, Padmasambhava went to Tibet in the eighth century; Atiśa Dīpaṅkara Śrījñāna (982–1054), from Bengal and Vikramaśīla, went to Tibet in 1042 and spent his last years there.

    These journeys produced knowledge of routes and places in both directions. Tibetan records of Indian teachers, Chinese records of Indian monks, and the travel accounts of Chinese pilgrims together form a body of geographical knowledge of Buddhist Asia (Sen 2003). When Buddhism declined in India after the twelfth century, Indian Buddhist teachers continued to travel to Tibet and Nepal, and Tibetan pilgrims, such as Dharmasvāmin in the 1230s, continued to visit India.

    13.5.1 Sri Lanka, Burma and the Theravāda Networks¶

    Monastic connections across the Bay of Bengal continued long after Buddhism declined in India. Sri Lanka became the centre of Theravāda Buddhism, and monks travelled between Sri Lanka, Burma, Thailand and Cambodia to obtain valid ordination lineages and texts; in the eleventh century King Anawrahta of Pagan in Burma and King Vijayabāhu I of Sri Lanka exchanged monks and scriptures, and in the fifteenth century monks from Pegu were sent to Sri Lanka for reordination. These networks kept alive the memory of the Buddhist sacred places of India, to which pilgrims and royal missions continued to travel (Chapter 4).

    13.5.2 Scholars¶

    Scholars travelled too. In the eighth century an Indian astronomer brought a Sanskrit astronomical text to the court of the caliph al-Manṣūr at Baghdad, where its Arabic version carried Indian methods and the meridian of Ujjayinī into Islamic astronomy (Section 3.2). How Sanskrit texts themselves named the peoples and lands beyond the subcontinent is described with the literary “conquest of the quarters” in Section 12.3.4.

    13.6 Memory and Diplomacy¶

    The long history of Indian Ocean connections has become a resource for modern diplomacy and cultural policy. India’s Ministry of Culture launched Project Mausam in 2014 to study and promote the shared maritime heritage of the countries of the Indian Ocean, and governments of the region regularly invoke the monsoon trade, the spread of Buddhism and the Cōḻa voyages in speeches and exhibitions. Such uses of the past are understandable, but historians have cautioned against presenting the ancient connections as a story of Indian influence radiating outward. The evidence surveyed in this chapter shows a world of exchange in many directions, in which Indians were one group of travellers among many, and in which the peoples of Southeast Asia, East Africa and Arabia were active partners rather than passive recipients (Kulke et al. 2009).

    13.7 Indian Travellers and Their Accounts¶

    Pre-modern India produced few travel narratives of the kind written by Faxian or Ibn Baṭṭūṭa. Knowledge of distant lands was carried by merchants, sailors and pilgrims, but rarely written down as a personal narrative. The pilgrimage literature described in Chapter 4 is an exception of a different kind. From the eighteenth century, however, Indians began to write accounts of journeys abroad, first in Persian and later in English, Urdu, Bengali, Marathi and other languages (Khan 1998; Fisher 2004).

    • Mīrzā Iʿtiṣām al-Dīn, a Mughal official, travelled to Britain in 1765–68 as part of a mission to George III and described the journey in Persian in Shigarf-nāma-yi Wilāyat (“The Wonder-Book of Europe”).

    • Sake Dean Mahomed (1759–1851), from Patna, served in the East India Company’s army, moved to Ireland and England, and published The Travels of Dean Mahomet (1794), the first book written in English by an Indian.

    • Mīrzā Abū Ṭālib Khān (1752–c. 1805), from Lucknow, travelled through Europe in 1799–1803 and wrote a Persian account of Britain, Ireland and France, translated into English in 1810.

    • Nawab Sikandar Begum of Bhopal (r. 1860–68) made the Hajj in 1863–64 and wrote an account of the journey and of conditions in Arabia, published in English translation as A Pilgrimage to Mecca (1870)—one of the few pre-modern travel accounts by a woman from South Asia.

    • Pandita Ramabai (1858–1922), the Marathi scholar and reformer, travelled to Britain and the United States in the 1880s and published a Marathi account of American society (1889).

    These writers looked at other lands from an Indian standpoint, comparing institutions, manners and religions. Their works are part of the history of Indian geographical knowledge as much as the foreign accounts of India discussed in Chapter 6.

    13.7.1 Lascars¶

    From the seventeenth century many Indian seamen—known to Europeans as lascars, from the Persian lashkar—served on European ships, including those of the East India Company. By the late eighteenth and nineteenth centuries thousands of them sailed to Britain and elsewhere each year; some were stranded in British ports, and small communities of Indian seamen grew up in London and other cities. They were poorly paid and often harshly treated, and their experiences are known mainly from official records and the reports of charities. They too were Indian travellers, whose knowledge of the sea and of distant ports was rarely written down (Fisher 2004).

    13.7.2 Migration¶

    From the nineteenth century, migration carried millions of Indians abroad: as indentured labourers to Mauritius, the Caribbean, Fiji, Natal and Malaya (1834–1917), as soldiers of the British Indian army, as traders and professionals in East Africa and Southeast Asia, and later to the Gulf, Britain and North America. Letters, songs and memoirs of migrants record their own geography of distant places, often a painful one. Sunil Amrith has traced how, across the Bay of Bengal alone, millions of labourers moved between India, Burma, Malaya and Ceylon in the century before 1940, under the same monsoon that had governed the older trade (Amrith 2013). This later history lies beyond the scope of this volume.
