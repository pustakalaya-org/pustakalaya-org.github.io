    Preface¶

    This is Volume 21 of the series Bhāratīya Jñāna Paramparā: Bhūgola: Geography and Travel. It surveys the ways in which the land of the Indian subcontinent, and the wider world, were described, imagined, measured, mapped and travelled: the cosmography of the Purāṇas and of the Jain and Buddhist traditions; the spherical earth of the astronomers from Āryabhaṭa to Bhāskara II; the sacred geography of pilgrimage, Hindu, Buddhist, Jain, Sikh and Muslim; Indian maps and plans; the accounts of foreign visitors from Megasthenes and the Chinese pilgrims to al-Bīrūnī, Ibn Baṭṭūṭa and the visitors to Vijayanagara and the Mughal court; geographical writing in Persian, from the Ḥudūd al-ʿĀlam to the Āʾīn-i Akbarī and the Mughal road books; routes, rivers, mountains, coasts and islands; the historical regions, administrative divisions and boundaries of the subcontinent, from the janapadas to the linguistic states; the Indians who travelled abroad as merchants, monks, sailors and pilgrims; and the colonial surveys, including the Great Trigonometrical Survey and the Indian surveyors and explorers who worked for it.

    The volume follows a few simple principles. Primary texts are quoted only where the wording and the reference are well established; figures are given only with a source; ancient texts are not read as “anticipating” modern science; Purāṇic cosmography is presented as cosmology, not as an encoded map of the modern world; and the calculations of the astronomers are reported as they stand, with the uncertainty that the length of the yojana introduces into any modern comparison. Dates are given as the range accepted in current scholarship, with traditional dates labelled as such. Maps are drawn on a frame of latitude and longitude with a simplified coastline, and the diagrams of the Purāṇic, Jain and Buddhist world-systems are labelled as diagrams of cosmology. The main secondary works on which the volume relies—among them the History of Cartography, Schwartzberg’s Historical Atlas of South Asia, Eck’s India: A Sacred Geography, Plofker’s Mathematics in India, Casson’s Periplus and Edney’s Mapping an Empire—are cited and listed in the bibliography, followed by a glossary, a chronology and a gazetteer of the places most often named.

    No survey of this size can be exhaustive: readers should turn to the editions and studies cited, and check every quotation against a critical edition before use.

    Nikhil Kumar Rajput, Ramanujan College, University of Delhi
    Guru Pūrṇimā, 2026

    A Note on Transliteration and Names¶

    Sanskrit, Pali and Prakrit words are given in the International Alphabet of Sanskrit Transliteration (IAST): bhūgola, Jambūdvīpa, Arthaśāstra. Tamil words follow the conventions of the Tamil Lexicon (Madras), which marks the letters peculiar to Tamil, ḻ, ṟ and ṉ: tiṇai, Paṭṭiṉappālai, Cōḻa. Arabic and Persian words and names are given in a simplified scholarly form, with long vowels and the letters ʿayn (ʿ) and hamza (ʾ) marked and dotted consonants for the emphatic letters: Āʾīn-i Akbarī, ṣūba, Riḥla, al-Bīrūnī. Chinese names are given in pinyin (Xuanzang, Faxian), with older spellings where they are the titles of translations (Si-yu-ki).

    Modern place-names are given in their current official or customary English spelling without diacritics (Varanasi, Prayagraj, Kodungallur), except where a place is discussed under its classical name (Vārāṇasī, Prayāga, Ujjayinī); older forms such as Banaras, Calcutta and Madras are used for the period in which they were current. The gazetteer at the end of the volume gives the modern equivalents of the principal ancient names.
