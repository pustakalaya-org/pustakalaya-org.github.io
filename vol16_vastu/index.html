    Preface¶

    This is Volume 16 of the series Bhāratīya Jñāna Paramparā: Vāstu–Śilpa: Architecture and Sculpture. It surveys the architecture of the Indian subcontinent: the vāstu texts; the stūpas and rock-cut monasteries of the Buddhist, Jain and Hindu traditions, which come first because they are the oldest surviving religious monuments; the temples of every region; the mosques, tombs, shrines and gardens of the Sultanates and the Mughals; palaces and the buildings of the colonial and modern periods; and, in three thematic chapters, the planning of towns, the building of tanks, stepwells and dams, and the houses of India’s regions. Its second part deals with the theory and practice of image-making, iconography, the regional schools of sculpture and the traditions of painting. Maps of the principal sites and drawn plans, sections and diagrams accompany the text.

    The volume is an edited and corrected version of an earlier draft. In the course of revision every quoted verse was checked; passages that could not be verified as genuine, with a correct reference, were removed rather than repaired, and the many invented “quotations” of the earlier draft no longer appear. The prescriptions of the vāstu and śilpa texts are reported as what those texts say, with their approximate dates and regions, and not as statements of scientific fact; the modern claims made for them are examined in Chapter 2. Widely repeated myths about particular monuments, such as the shadowless tower and the monolithic capstone of the Bṛhadīśvara temple, are examined in their place. Dates are given as the range accepted in current scholarship, with traditional dates labelled as such.

    The book draws throughout on the work of modern scholars of Indian architecture and art, among them Stella Kramrisch, Bruno Dagens, Michael Meister, M. A. Dhaky, Adam Hardy, George Michell, Susan Huntington, Vidya Dehejia, Catherine Asher and Ebba Koch, whose works are cited in the text and collected in the select bibliography. It also tries to give a fair account of the social history of building: of the craft communities who built the monuments, of the place of caste in the vāstu texts, and of the many patrons, Hindu, Buddhist, Jain and Muslim, men and women, who paid for them. A glossary of the principal technical terms is provided at the end.

    No survey of this size can be exhaustive. Readers should treat it as an introduction, and turn to the editions, translations and studies cited for the texts and monuments themselves.

    Nikhil Kumar Rajput
    Ramanujan College, University of Delhi
    Guru Pūrṇimā, 2026
