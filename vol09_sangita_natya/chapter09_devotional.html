    Chapter 9: Devotional Music: Kīrtan, Bhajan and Sacred Song¶

    Most of the music made in India, now as in the past, has been made in worship. Long before the concert hall and the radio, and alongside the art music of courts and salons, people sang the names and deeds of their gods, the hymns of their saints and the scriptures of their communities: in temples and gurdwārās, at Sufi shrines and in Shia mourning assemblies, on pilgrim roads, in village squares and in the homes of devotees. This devotional music is not a single genre. It includes the professional temple singer and the congregation that sings without training; the solo narrator who preaches through song and the chorus that repeats a single name for hours; music set to strict rāgas and music sung to the tunes of film songs. What unites it is that its purpose lies outside the music itself, in devotion, merit, remembrance or ecstasy, and that its participants judge it by that purpose.

    Earlier chapters have touched on several of these traditions: the hymns of the Tamil saints (Chapter 8), qawwālī and Sikh kīrtan (Chapter 7), and the devotional theatres and dances of Assam and Manipur (Chapter 18). This chapter looks at devotional music as a whole. It explains the basic forms, and then describes the principal regional and religious traditions: the temple hymnody of the Tamil country, music as a ritual offering in the temples and the sopāna singing of Kerala, the bhajana and harikathā traditions of the south, the abhaṅg and kīrtan of Maharashtra, the temple music of the Puṣṭimārga, the padāvalī kīrtan of Bengal, the nām and borgīt of Assam, the songs of Kabīr, Mīrā and Ravidās as sung by the communities that keep them, the musical structure of the Sikh scripture, the devotional music of Indian Islam beyond the qawwālī, Christian devotional song, and the song of the Jains and of the Newar Buddhists of Nepal. The poetry of the saints, as literature, is treated in Volume 8, Kāvya Kalpalatā.

    9.1 Kīrtan and Bhajan: Terms and Forms¶

    Two words cover much of the field. Kīrtana (from the root kīrt, “to mention, to tell, to praise”) means the telling or singing of the names, qualities and deeds of a deity. Bhajana (from bhaj, “to share, to partake of, to worship”, the root of bhakti itself) means worship and, in musical usage, a devotional song or a session of devotional singing. The Bhāgavata Purāṇa lists kīrtana second, after hearing (śravaṇa), among the nine forms of devotion (7.5.23), and teaches that what was achieved in earlier ages by meditation, sacrifice and temple worship is achieved in the present age of Kali by the singing of the names of Hari (12.3.52). The saṅkīrtana, the singing of the name “together”, thus became for many devotional movements both a practice and a theology: the sound of the divine name was held to make the deity present (Beck 1993).

    In practice, three broad forms can be distinguished, though most traditions combine them.

    Congregational name-singing (nāma-kīrtan, nām-saṅkīrtan).
    A leader sings a short phrase, usually a name or a series of names of the deity, and the group repeats it. The music is simple and responsorial, and its power lies in repetition and in a gradual increase of tempo and intensity, often leading to dance or ecstatic states. Cymbals and a barrel drum keep the time; the harmonium has been added almost everywhere since the late nineteenth century.
    Devotional song.
    The bhajan, pad, abhaṅg or, in the southern sense, kīrtana: a composed song with a refrain, by a known or attributed saint-poet, sung by a soloist or a group. These songs carry the signature (mudrā, chāp, bhaṇitā) of their author in the last verse, and they form the largest body of devotional music in India.
    Narrative and preaching kīrtan.
    A single performer, often a Brahmin or a learned devotee, combines song, recitation, story-telling and commentary on a text, with a small group of accompanists. The harikathā of the south and the kīrtan of Maharashtra (below) are examples; the form is related to the epic recitation discussed in Chapter 21.

    Devotional music has always stood in a two-way relation with art music. The great composers of Karnatak music were devotional poets, and the dhrupad of the north grew up partly in temple contexts; conversely, temple and congregational singers have drawn on the rāgas and tālas of the art traditions. Alongside both, the devotional singing of villages and neighbourhoods keeps genres and occasions of its own (below).

    9.2 The Temple Hymns of the Tamil Country¶

    The earliest body of devotional song in an Indian vernacular that is still sung is that of the Tamil saints. The Śaiva hymns of Campantar, Appar and Cuntarar (seventh to ninth centuries), gathered into the first seven books of the Śaiva canon, the Tirumuṟai, under the name Tēvāram, were composed as songs in ten-verse units (patikam) for singing in particular temples, and were arranged in the canon by paṇ (Chapter 8). Inscriptions of the Cōḻa period record endowments for singers of the hymns (piṭārar) and for the drummers who accompanied them in the great temples, and the hereditary singers called ōtuvār have sung them ever since, at the conclusion of the daily rites and on festival occasions. Indira Peterson’s study shows that the hymns were conceived from the start as songs for a community of worshippers moving from shrine to shrine, and that their musical setting was integral to their meaning (Peterson 1989). In the twentieth century the Śaiva monasteries (ātīṉam) such as Dharmapuram ran schools for ōtuvārs and sponsored research into the paṇs, and the ōtuvār became, with the temple musicians of the periya mēḷam (Chapter 8), one of the last hereditary custodians of a pre-modern temple music in the south.

    The Vaiṣṇava counterpart is the Nālāyira Divya Prabandham, the “four thousand sacred compositions” of the twelve Āḻvārs, compiled according to tradition by Nāthamuni in the tenth century. Tradition also credits Nāthamuni with setting the hymns to music and teaching them to his nephews, the first araiyars. The hymns are recited in Śrīvaiṣṇava temples and homes, and their performance reaches its climax in the winter festival of recitation (adhyayanotsava) at Śrīraṅgam and other temples, during which the whole collection is recited before the deity over some three weeks. At Śrīraṅgam, Śrīvilliputtūr and Āḻvār Tirunagari the hereditary araiyars, wearing a tall conical cap and keeping time with cymbals, sing selected hymns and interpret them with gesture and enacted dialogue (araiyar cēvai), a combination of song, recitation and abhinaya that is among the oldest surviving forms of devotional performance in India. Vasudha Narayanan’s study of the festival shows how the Tamil hymns were placed on a par with the Sanskrit Veda and given a liturgical setting that mirrored the Vedic one (Narayanan 1994).

    A third Tamil tradition is that of the Tiruppugaḻ of Aruṇagirinātar (fifteenth century), songs in praise of Murukaṉ whose dense patterns of rhyme and rhythm (cantam) make them an exacting test of a singer’s command of tāla. In the twentieth century groups devoted to singing them spread among Tamil devotees in India and the diaspora, an example of a medieval hymnody revived as congregational music.

    9.3 Music in Temple Ritual: Āgama and Sopāna¶

    In the great Hindu temples music was not only sung by devotees but offered to the deity as part of the ritual. The Āgamas and the ritual manuals that govern temple worship in the south list song (gīta), instrumental music (vādya) and dance (nṛtta) among the services (upacāra) offered to the deity in the daily and festival rites, alongside bathing, adornment, food and lamps, and the larger temples employed musicians and dancers for this purpose (Volume 6, Tantra–Āgama). The periya mēḷam of the Tamil temples (Chapter 8), the hymns of the ōtuvārs and araiyars, the dance of the temple women (Chapter 15) and the singing of the Gītagovinda before Jagannātha at Puri (Chapter 17) were all, in this sense, ritual services, with fixed times, places and hereditary performers.

    Kerala preserves a distinctive form of such music, the sopāna saṅgītam, named after the steps (sopāna) that lead up to the sanctum. During the evening worship a singer from one of the hereditary temple-servant communities stands beside the steps and sings, accompanying himself on the hourglass drum iṭakka, above all the songs of Jayadeva’s Gītagovinda, in a slow, ornamented style and in rāgas that have their own Kerala names and characteristics. The singing is part of the ritual sequence and ends when the sanctum is closed. In the second half of the twentieth century singers such as Njeralath Rama Poduval took sopāna music out of the temple to the concert stage, where it has been recognised as a regional tradition of art music in its own right. It shows how a Sanskrit lyric composed in eastern India in the twelfth century became, in the far south, the core of a ritual music with its own melodic system, much as the same text became the basis of Odissi music and of the aṣṭapadī singing of the southern bhajana (below).

    9.4 The Bhajana Tradition of the South¶

    In the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries a distinct tradition of congregational singing took shape in the Kāvēri delta, under the influence of Brahmin teachers associated with the cult of the divine name. Bodhendra Sarasvatī and Śrīdhara Veṅkaṭeśa Ayyāvāḷ, both of the seventeenth and early eighteenth centuries, taught that the repetition of the name of God was the supreme means of salvation, and Marudanallur Sadguru Svāmī, active in the late eighteenth and early nineteenth centuries, is credited with fixing the order of service (paddhati) of what came to be called the southern bhajana sampradāya.

    The bhajana is remarkable for its multilingual repertoire. A full service draws on Sanskrit, Tamil, Telugu, Kannada, Marathi and Hindi: the aṣṭapadīs of Jayadeva’s Gītagovinda, the taraṅgas of Nārāyaṇa Tīrtha, the songs of Purandaradāsa and Bhadrācala Rāmadāsu, the abhaṅgs of the Marathi saints, the songs of Tyāgarāja, and nāmāvalis (litanies of names). Several of Tyāgarāja’s songs, the utsava sampradāya and divyanāma kīrtanas, were composed for exactly such worship and are simpler than his concert compositions. The great bhajanas culminate in the enactment of the marriage of Rādhā and Kṛṣṇa or of Sītā and Rāma (Rādhā Kalyāṇam, Sītā Kalyāṇam), with songs for each stage of the wedding, and in the divyanāma saṅkīrtanam, in which the singers circle a lamp, singing and dancing (Singer 1972).

    Milton Singer’s study of bhajana groups in Madras in the 1950s found that they had become an important institution of the new urban middle class. Meeting in homes and halls, they brought together people of different castes and occupations, at least among non-Dalit Hindus, and Singer argued that they allowed educated professionals to combine modern occupations with an intense religious life, and to express in song an egalitarian ideal that their social world did not always realise (Singer 1972). The presence of Marathi abhaṅgs in the southern bhajana reflects the period of Maratha rule at Thanjavur (1676–1855), and the movement of songs, saints and practices between Maharashtra and the Tamil country is one of the clearest examples of the circulation of devotional music across language boundaries.

    9.5 Harikathā: The Story-Sermon of the South¶

    The same Maratha connection produced the southern harikathā (“story of Hari”), also called harikathā kālakṣepam (“passing the time with the story of Hari”). In the nineteenth century performers in the Thanjavur region adapted the Marathi narrative kīrtan (below) to Tamil and Telugu audiences, and Tanjore Krishna Bhagavatar (1847–1903) is generally credited with giving the Tamil form its classic shape. A harikathā performer stands before the audience, keeping time with a pair of wooden clappers fitted with jingles (ciplā), accompanied by a supporting singer, a violinist or harmonium player and a mṛdaṅgam player. He — and from the early twentieth century occasionally she — tells a story from the epics, the Purāṇas or the lives of the saints, in prose that moves between learned exposition and colloquial humour, and punctuates it with songs: Sanskrit verses, Tamil hymns, Telugu kṛtis, Marathi abhaṅgs and Hindi bhajans, often in elaborate rāga settings. The performer must therefore be at once a scholar, a story-teller and a trained singer.

    In Andhra, Ādibhaṭla Nārāyaṇa Dāsu (1864–1945), a scholar, poet and composer, made the Telugu harikathā a major art and composed many harikathās of his own; he is remembered as its foremost master. In the twentieth century harikathā was broadcast on the radio and performed at temple festivals, weddings and the music season of Madras, and it carried new themes as well as old: performers told the lives of nationalist leaders and of reforming saints, and the form provided one of the few public platforms on which a woman could speak at length before a mixed audience. Its decline in the late twentieth century, in the face of television and of the shorter attention of urban audiences, has been partly offset by its use in religious broadcasting and on the internet. Harikathā also shows how the boundary between music, theatre and preaching was drawn differently in India than in modern Western categories: a single performer could be at once singer, actor and teacher.

    9.6 Maharashtra: Abhaṅg, Bhajan and Kīrtan¶

    9.6.1 The Vārkarīs and their songs¶

    The Vārkarī tradition of Maharashtra centres on the god Viṭhobā (Viṭṭhala) of Pandharpur and on a line of saint-poets whose songs are its scripture: Jñāneśvar (Jñānadev, late thirteenth century), Nāmdev (traditionally 1270–1350), Eknāth (sixteenth century) and Tukārām (seventeenth century), together with many others, among them the women Muktābāī and Janābāī and the Mahār (Dalit) saint Cokhāmeḷā. Their songs are mostly in the abhaṅg (“unbroken”) metre of Marathi, short stanzas with a refrain, composed to be sung. The tradition gave a voice to people of low caste and to women; the authority of Cokhāmeḷā and Janābāī among its saints, and the stories of the hardships they suffered at the hands of Brahmin priests, are part of its memory, though the tradition did not abolish caste in the society around it (Novetzke 2008; Hawley and Juergensmeyer 1988).

    The defining practice of the tradition is the vārī, the pilgrimage to Pandharpur, made above all for the ekādaśī of the month of Āṣāḍha (June–July). Palanquins bearing the sandals of Jñāneśvar from Alandi and of Tukārām from Dehu set out for Pandharpur, a journey of some three weeks, accompanied by hundreds of thousands of pilgrims organised in singing groups (diṇḍī). Each group has its leader, who carries a vīṇā (here a drone instrument), a drummer with the mṛdaṅg or pakhāvaj, and many singers with small cymbals (ṭāḷ), and the pilgrims sing abhaṅgs and the names of the saints as they walk. At certain halts the pilgrims form great circles (riṅgaṇ) around which a horse runs, and they dance and play games in the manner of the cowherd boys of Kṛṣṇa’s childhood. Irawati Karve’s account of walking with the pilgrims, written in the 1950s, remains one of the best descriptions of the experience (Karve 1962).

    9.6.2 Two kinds of kīrtan¶

    Maharashtra developed two forms of narrative kīrtan. The Vārkarī kīrtan, which tradition traces to Nāmdev, is led by a kīrtankār who takes an abhaṅg as his text, sings it with a chorus of cymbal players and a drummer, and expounds it with stories and other songs, the chorus taking up the refrain. Christian Novetzke has shown that the memory of Nāmdev himself, as a saint and as the founder of kīrtan, has been carried for centuries less by manuscripts than by the performances of kīrtankārs, who tell his story and sing his songs, so that kīrtan is at once a form of devotion and a form of public history (Novetzke 2008).

    The other form, called Nāradīya or Haridāsī kīrtan, is performed by a single kīrtankār (traditionally a Brahmin) standing before the audience, accompanied by harmonium and tablā. It has two parts: the pūrvaraṅg, an exposition of a philosophical or devotional text, usually a verse or an abhaṅg, and the uttararaṅg, a story (ākhyān) that illustrates it, told in prose, verse and song. Its songs draw freely on rāga music and on the musical theatre (nāṭyasaṅgīt), and its performers are expected to be scholars, story-tellers and singers at once. In the late nineteenth and twentieth centuries some kīrtankārs used the form for nationalist themes (rāṣṭrīya kīrtan), and the reformer Gadge Maharaj (1876–1956), himself from a low-caste background, used the Vārkarī kīrtan to preach cleanliness, education and the rejection of caste prejudice and animal sacrifice, showing how flexible a medium the form could be.

    The abhaṅg also crossed into art music. Singers of the Marathi musical theatre recorded abhaṅgs in semi-classical style, and from the 1970s Bhimsen Joshi’s concerts of saints’ songs (Santavāṇī) made the abhaṅg part of the repertoire of a great khayāl singer. The same songs continue to be sung by pilgrims who have never heard of either.

    9.7 The Music of the Havelī¶

    9.7.1 Sevā and kīrtan in the Puṣṭimārga¶

    The Puṣṭimārga (“path of grace”), founded by Vallabhācārya (1479–1531) and organised by his son Viṭṭhalnāth (1516–1586), worships Kṛṣṇa as a child and youth of Braj. Its temples are called havelī (“mansion”), because the deity is served as the master of a great household, and its worship (sevā) consists in caring for him through the day and the year: waking, dressing, feeding, entertaining and putting him to rest. Music is an essential part of this care. At each of the eight daily “viewings” (darśan) of the deity — maṅgalā at dawn, śṛṅgār, gvāl, rājbhog at midday, utthāpan, bhog, sandhyā-ārtī and śayan at night — temple singers (kīrtankārs) sing songs appropriate to the hour, the season and the festival, in rāgas prescribed for each occasion (Gaston 1997).

    The songs are the pads of the aṣṭachāp, the “eight seals”: four poets associated with Vallabha — Sūrdās, Kumbhandās, Paramānanddās and Kṛṣṇadās — and four with Viṭṭhalnāth — Nanddās, Chītasvāmī, Govindsvāmī and Caturbhujdās — together with later poets of the sect. Their poems describe the life of Kṛṣṇa as the devotee imagines it at each hour: waking at dawn, going out with the cows, returning in the evening, and the festivals of the year, from the spring songs of Vasant and Holī to the swing songs of the rains. The music is in the style of dhrupad and dhamār, sung by a lead singer and others, accompanied by the pakhāvaj, cymbals and a drone, and in some temples by stringed instruments. Anne-Marie Gaston’s study of the temples of Nathdwara in Rajasthan, the principal seat of the sect since the image of Śrīnāthjī was brought there from Braj in the seventeenth century, shows a hereditary musical service organised by liturgical calendar and maintained by families of temple singers and drummers (Gaston 1997).

    9.7.2 Havelī music and the art tradition¶

    The relation of havelī music to Hindustani art music is close but debated. The repertoire and style of the temples preserve compositions in dhrupad and dhamār form, many attributed to the aṣṭachāp poets, and the system of assigning rāgas to the hours of worship parallels the time theory of rāga (Chapter 4). Some musicians and scholars see the temples as one of the sources of dhrupad; others regard havelī saṅgīt as a parallel tradition that borrowed from the court music of the period. What is clear is that the temples kept alive a body of composition and a style of performance that had otherwise largely disappeared, and that in the twentieth century singers such as Pandit Jasraj drew on it in their concert repertoire.

    Other temples of Braj have their own traditions. In the samāj gāyan of the Rādhāvallabha and Haridāsī communities of Vrindavan, a group of men seated before the deity sings the pads of the sect’s poets, the leader singing each line and the others repeating it. The Haridāsī community traces its line to Svāmī Haridās, the sixteenth-century saint-musician whom later tradition made the teacher of Tānsen; the story cannot be confirmed, but it expresses the conviction, widespread in the north, that the highest art music had its roots in devotion (Beck 1993).

    9.8 Bengal: Padāvalī Kīrtan¶

    In Bengal and Odisha the devotional movement inspired by Caitanya (1486–1533) made congregational singing the centre of religious life. According to his biographers, Caitanya led processional singing of the names of Kṛṣṇa through the streets of Navadvīp (nagar-kīrtan), and the ecstatic saṅkīrtan with the clay barrel drum khol (also called mṛdaṅga) and hand cymbals (karatāl) became the hallmark of Gauḍīya Vaiṣṇavism. Out of this practice grew a more elaborate art form, the padāvalī kīrtan or līlā kīrtan, which sets the lyric songs (pada) of Vidyāpati, Caṇḍīdās, Govindadās, Jñānadās and many others, in Bengali and in Brajabuli, a literary language mixing Bengali with Maithili forms (Hawley and Juergensmeyer 1988).

    A padāvalī kīrtan performance presents an episode (pālā) of the love of Rādhā and Kṛṣṇa — the first stirrings of love, the secret journey to the meeting place, the lovers’ quarrel, separation after Kṛṣṇa’s departure for Mathura — through a sequence of songs chosen and linked by the lead singer. It begins with a gauracandrikā, a song about Caitanya (Gaurāṅga) that introduces the mood of the episode, because the Gauḍīya tradition understands Caitanya as Rādhā and Kṛṣṇa in one person and his life as a re-enactment of their love. The singer elaborates the songs with ākhar, improvised lines inserted between the lines of the text that draw out their meaning, so that a short poem may be sung for a long time; the khol player and the chorus respond, and the music uses long and intricate tāla cycles proper to the genre. Tradition holds that the style of this kīrtan was fixed at a great gathering of Vaiṣṇavas at Kheturi, in the late sixteenth century, organised by the poet and teacher Narottama Dāsa, and later writers distinguish several regional styles named after the districts in which they arose, among them the Garāṇhāṭī and the Manoharśāhī.

    Kīrtan has remained a living art in Bengal, sung by professional singers, men and women, at religious festivals, in homes after a death and on the radio and recordings. It has also travelled further than any other Indian devotional music. The International Society for Krishna Consciousness, founded in New York in 1966 by A. C. Bhaktivedanta Swami, made the congregational singing of the Hare Kṛṣṇa mantra with mṛdaṅga and karatāl a familiar sight in cities around the world, and the participatory kīrtan has since become part of the devotional and “yoga” culture of Europe and North America. In Manipur the Gauḍīya saṅkīrtana developed into a distinctive art of song and drum dance (Chapter 18).

    9.9 Assam: Nām and Borgīt¶

    The Vaiṣṇava movement of Śaṅkaradeva in Assam (Chapter 18) made the singing of the name (nām) the centre of its religious life, and created a devotional music that is still practised in every Assamese village. Its two central texts are collections of verse for congregational singing and recitation: Śaṅkaradeva’s Kīrtana-ghoṣā, narrative poems in Assamese on the deeds of Kṛṣṇa and the incarnations of Viṣṇu, and Mādhavadeva’s Nām-ghoṣā, a collection of devotional verses. In the village prayer hall (nāmghar) and in the sattras, the congregational service (nām-prasaṅga) is led by a precentor who sings a line that the congregation takes up, clapping or playing cymbals, in a form of responsorial singing that can build to great intensity. These services, held daily in the sattras and on many occasions in the villages, are open to all members of the community, and they gave the Assamese peasantry a shared liturgy in its own language.

    The borgīts (“great songs”) of Śaṅkaradeva and Mādhavadeva are a more learned form. Composed in Brajabuli and set to named rāgas, they are sung at the beginning of services and of dramatic performances (bhāonā), often with a long unmetred introduction exploring the rāga before the song proper, accompanied by the khol and cymbals. Tradition says that many of Śaṅkaradeva’s borgīts were lost in a fire and that only a few dozen survive. The musical tradition of the sattras — the borgīt, the drumming of the khol, the songs of the plays and the dances of the monks — was one of the main sources from which Sattriya dance was reconstructed for the stage (Neog 1980). Assam also has narrative forms in which a lead singer and chorus perform sacred stories with song and gesture, such as the ojāpāli, whose singers present episodes from the epics and from the cult of the snake goddess Manasā.

    9.10 Songs of the Margins: Kabīr, Mīrā and Ravidās¶

    Not all devotional music belongs to temples, sects with institutions, or professional singers. Some of the most widely sung devotional songs of north India belong to saints who stood outside or against the religious establishments of their time, and whose songs have been kept alive above all by communities of low caste.

    Kabīr, the fifteenth-century weaver of Banaras, composed songs (pad), couplets (sākhī) and longer poems that attack ritual, caste, the authority of Brahmin and mullah alike, and the illusions of the world, and that speak of a formless (nirguṇ) divine reality found within. His songs were collected in several traditions — the Bījak of the Kabīr Panth, the Sikh scripture and the anthologies of the Dādūpanthīs of Rajasthan — but they have lived above all in oral performance. Linda Hess’s study of Kabīr singing in the Malwa region of central India describes groups (maṇḍalī) of singers, most of them from Dalit communities such as the Balāīs and Meghvāls, who meet to sing Kabīr’s bhajans through the night, accompanied by the tambūrā, ḍholak, kartāl and mañjīrā; their leading singer, Prahlad Singh Tipanya, became widely known in India and abroad from the 1990s. Hess shows how the songs work through the bodies and voices of the singers, how they are adapted and recombined in performance, and how, for Dalit singers, Kabīr’s critique of caste is a living message rather than a historical curiosity. The same Malwa tradition inspired the Hindustani singer Kumar Gandharva, who settled in Dewas and recorded nirguṇī bhajans that brought this music into the concert hall (Hess 2015).

    Mīrābāī, the sixteenth-century Rajput princess who, according to tradition, defied her marital family to devote herself to Kṛṣṇa, is known chiefly through songs sung in her name. Parita Mukta’s study of the Mīrā traditions of Rajasthan and Saurashtra shows that her songs were preserved and sung above all by peasant and artisan communities, among them the Meghvāl weavers, who saw in her defiance of the Rāṇā a model of resistance to the powerful, while the Rajput elite long regarded her memory with unease; only in the twentieth century, through nationalist and middle-class appropriation, film and the concert stage, did she become a respectable figure of devotional womanhood (Mukta 1994). The songs of Ravidās (Raidās), a leather-worker saint of Banaras whose hymns are included in the Sikh scripture, are sung in the gatherings of the Ravidāsī communities of Punjab and Uttar Pradesh, for whom his songs and his memory have been central to a modern assertion of Dalit religious identity. These traditions show that devotional song in India was never only an expression of shared piety; it was also a means by which the marginal remembered their own saints and voiced their own claims.

    9.11 Devotion in the Village: Name-Singing and Seasonal Song¶

    Much devotional music in north India is made neither in temples nor in sectarian institutions but in villages and neighbourhoods, by groups of ordinary devotees. Edward Henry’s study of the Bhojpuri-speaking region of eastern Uttar Pradesh and western Bihar distinguishes the occasions and genres of this music with care. Groups of men gather for kīrtan and bhajan in the evenings and at festivals, singing the names of Rāma or Kṛṣṇa and the songs of the saints to harmonium, ḍholak and cymbals; for special occasions a village or a family sponsors continuous name-singing for a fixed period, often twenty-four hours (aṣṭayām) or longer, in which teams of singers take turns so that the chant never stops. Such performances are believed to bring merit and protection to the village, and they draw singers from neighbouring settlements, so that they also create networks of friendship and rivalry between villages (Henry 1988).

    Alongside these, women sing their own devotional and ritual songs: songs to the goddess, songs for the festivals of the year, and the songs of the life-cycle — the sohar sung at a birth, the many songs of the wedding, among them teasing and abusive songs addressed to the bridegroom’s party — in which devotion, humour and social comment mix freely. The nirguṇ songs on death and the impermanence of the body, often attributed to Kabīr, are sung by professional and amateur singers alike. Henry shows that the villagers themselves classify this music by occasion and purpose rather than by musical style, and that a single tune may serve several genres; what makes a song devotional is the setting and the intention of its singers, not its musical form (Henry 1988). The same holds for much of the music described in this chapter, and it is a reminder that the categories of “classical”, “folk” and “devotional” music used in this volume are analytical conveniences rather than the categories of the people who make the music (Chapter 21).

    9.12 The Rāgas of the Guru Granth Sahib¶

    Chapter 7 introduced the Sikh tradition of śabad kīrtan. Its scripture is the only one of the great religious canons organised by musical mode, and its structure deserves a closer look (Mann 2001; Singh 2000).

    The Guru Granth Sahib opens with texts for recitation and daily prayer — the Japjī of Guru Nānak, which is recited rather than sung, and the short collections used at evening and bedtime prayer — and then presents the main body of hymns in thirty-one sections, each named after a rāga. Within each rāga section the hymns are arranged first by poetic form — hymns of four stanzas (caupade), of eight (aṣṭapadī), longer songs (chant) and the ballads called vār — and within each form by author, beginning with Guru Nānak and continuing with the later Gurus, followed by the songs of the bhagats, saint-poets from outside the Sikh line such as Kabīr, Nāmdev, Ravidās and the Sufi Shaikh Farīd. The headings of the hymns carry further musical directions. Many bear the word ghar (“house”) with a number; its meaning is disputed, and it has been interpreted as indicating a tāla, a melodic variant of the rāga or a manner of performance. A few hymns are marked partāl, indicating a change of tāla during the song. Several of the vārs carry instructions that they are to be sung to the tune (dhunī) of a named heroic ballad of the Punjab, an indication that popular as well as art melodies shaped the music of the early community. The volume ends with a Rāgamālā, a list of rāgas and rāgiṇīs in a classification of the family type (Chapter 4), whose place in the scripture has been debated among Sikhs since the nineteenth century.

    The rāgas of the scripture include many familiar from Hindustani music (Āsā, Gūjarī, Bilāval, Rāmkalī, Basant, Sāraṅg, Malār, Kalyāṇ) and some that are regional or distinctive to the Sikh tradition (Mājh, Vaḍahaṃs, Tukhārī). The choice of rāga was associated with mood and season: the hymns in Vaḍahaṃs include songs of mourning, and those in Basant are sung in spring. The liturgy of the gurdwārā follows the day: Āsā dī Vār, with interpolated hymns, in the early morning, the evening and night prayers at their hours, and at the Harmandir Sahib in Amritsar kīrtan by successive groups of professional singers (rāgī jathā) through most of the day.

    Besides the rāgīs and the Muslim rabābīs who sang in the gurdwārās before 1947 (Chapter 7), the Sikh tradition has a third kind of musician: the ḍhāḍhīs, who sing heroic ballads (vārs) of the Gurus and of Sikh history to the small hourglass drum ḍhaḍḍ and the bowed sāraṅgī, a tradition associated with the court of the sixth Guru, Hargobind. Among Sikh groups, the Nāmdhārīs have been notable patrons of Hindustani art music in the twentieth century. The gurmat saṅgīt revival of recent decades (Chapter 7) has been accompanied by debates about authenticity and about the place of women, who have not been part of the regular kīrtan rota in the sanctum of the Harmandir Sahib, a restriction that has been publicly contested.

    9.13 Devotional Music in Indian Islam¶

    The qawwālī of the Sufi shrines (Chapter 7) is the best-known devotional music of South Asian Islam, but it is not the only one, and in some respects it is untypical: Muslim religious scholars have always been divided about the use of music in worship, and much Islamic devotional performance avoids instruments and the word “music” altogether. Regula Qureshi has described the resulting distinction between sound that is recited or chanted, which is acceptable to all, and sound that is “music”, which some reject (Qureshi 1986, 1981).

    9.13.1 The Shia majlis¶

    The most elaborate tradition of this kind is that of the Shia mourning assembly (majlis), held above all in the first ten days of the month of Muḥarram to commemorate the martyrdom of the Prophet’s grandson Ḥusain at Karbala in 680. Its genres are distinguished by their manner of performance. In soz, a lead singer, supported by two or more assistants who sustain the notes (bāzū), sings a lament in a rāga-based melody without instrumental accompaniment; the salām is a salutation to the martyrs; the marsiya, the long elegiac poem in Urdu brought to its height in nineteenth-century Lucknow by Mīr Anīs and Mirzā Dabīr, is declaimed by a reciter with gesture and vocal art; and the nauḥa is a rhythmic lament sung by a group to the beating of the breast (mātam). Qureshi’s analysis shows how the majlis uses the resources of Hindustani music — rāga, refined vocal technique, a listening public trained in the courtly arts — while keeping its distance from “music” as entertainment; many hereditary musicians of Lucknow were also accomplished soz singers (Qureshi 1981; Hyder 2006).

    9.13.2 Other traditions¶

    Sunni devotion has its own sung genres: the naʿt in praise of the Prophet, sung or recited without instruments at gatherings and on the Prophet’s birthday (milād); the remembrance (dhikr) of the Sufi orders; and the songs of the shrines of the countryside. In Kashmir the Sufiyāna kalām or Sufiyāna mūsīqī, a learned art of Sufi poetry sung by an ensemble with the hammered dulcimer santūr, the bowed sāz-e Kashmīr, the setār and drums, uses a system of modes called maqām that is related to, but distinct from, the rāgas of the plains; the santūr of modern Hindustani music (Chapter 10) was adapted from this tradition. In Sindh and the Punjab the kāfī, and in Bengal the mārphatī and murśidī songs, carry Sufi teaching in the language of the countryside, close to the Bāul songs described in Chapter 21. These traditions show South Asian Islam as a creator of devotional music in many languages and styles, adapting local musical resources to its own purposes.

    9.14 Christian Devotional Song¶

    India’s Christian communities created devotional music that combined their own liturgical traditions with the musical languages of their regions. The ancient Syriac Christians of Kerala and the Catholic communities of Goa and the west coast, whose performance traditions are described in Chapter 21, preserved liturgical chant and developed vernacular hymns and paraliturgical songs. Protestant missions from the eighteenth century onwards introduced the European hymn, translated into Indian languages and sung to Western tunes, but Indian converts soon began to compose devotional songs in Indian forms.

    The most important early figure was Vedanāyakam Śāstri (1774–1864) of Thanjavur, a Tamil Protestant poet associated with the court of the Maratha king Serfoji II, who composed hundreds of Tamil kīrtanais set to Karnatak rāgas and tālas, and a Christian dance-drama in the form of the Tamil kuṟavañci, the Bethlehem Kuṟavañci, modelled on the court dance-dramas of Thanjavur (Chapter 15). His songs, and the later Tamil Christian lyrics in the same style, are still sung in Tamil churches, alongside translated Western hymns. In Maharashtra the poet Narayan Vaman Tilak (1861–1919), a Brahmin convert, wrote Christian abhaṅgs and bhajans in the manner of the Vārkarī saints, and in Bengal and the Hindi-speaking region Christian bhajans and kīrtans were composed in the styles of the surrounding devotional music. After the Second Vatican Council (1962–1965) the Catholic Church in India encouraged the use of Indian music, instruments and dance in worship, and Indian musical settings of the liturgy became common.

    In north-east India, where large parts of the Khasi, Mizo and Naga peoples became Christian in the nineteenth and twentieth centuries, missionaries introduced choral hymn-singing in four-part harmony, and the hymn and the choir became central to the musical life of these communities. Indigenous composers adapted the form, and in Mizoram a style of hymn-singing with drum and dance, drawing on older Mizo song, developed alongside the missionary hymn. The choirs of Shillong and Aizawl have since won recognition across India and abroad, an unexpected outcome of the missionary encounter. Christian devotional music thus displays, in a short history, the same processes of translation and adaptation that the older traditions show over many centuries.

    9.15 Jain and Buddhist Song¶

    The Jain community has a large literature of hymns (stotra, stavan) in Sanskrit, Prakrit and the regional languages, from classical Sanskrit hymns such as Mānatuṅga’s Bhaktāmara Stotra to the vernacular stavans of later centuries, and singing is part of temple ritual, of festivals and of the domestic religious life of lay Jains. Whitney Kelting’s study of Śvetāmbara laywomen in Pune describes the singing groups (maṇḍaḷ) in which women meet to sing stavans at rituals and gatherings, often to melodies taken from film songs and folk songs, and shows how the women, through their choice and performance of songs, shape a lay understanding of Jain teaching and devotion that differs in emphasis from that of the monks (Kelting 2001). The study is a reminder that much devotional music in India is made by women, in domestic and semi-public settings, and is easily overlooked by histories that concentrate on temples and professional singers.

    Buddhism, which largely disappeared from the plains of India in the second millennium, kept its musical traditions in the Himalaya. Among the Newars of the Kathmandu valley, Vajrayāna Buddhist priests preserved a repertoire of esoteric songs (caryāgīti) often compared with the Buddhist songs of eastern India of the late first millennium, sung in rāgas and sometimes danced. The Newar towns also preserve a Hindu–Buddhist devotional tradition, the dāphā, in which groups of men meet regularly in public rest-houses and temples to sing hymns in Newari, Maithili and Sanskrit in named rāgas and tālas, accompanied by drums and cymbals. Richard Widdess’s study of dāphā singing in Bhaktapur shows that these groups preserve rāga names, song forms and performance conventions that go back to the medieval courts of the Malla kings, and offers a rare opportunity to study a pre-modern rāga tradition that developed outside the art music of the Indian plains (Widdess 2013). The ritual music and masked dance of Tibetan Buddhism in Ladakh, Sikkim and the eastern Himalaya are described in Chapter 22.

    9.16 Devotional Music, Art Music and the Media¶

    In the twentieth century devotional music moved between the temple, the concert hall and the mass media in ways that changed all three. Concert musicians made devotional songs a regular part of their recitals: the Hindustani concert often ends with a bhajan (Chapter 7), the Karnatak concert includes bhajans and abhaṅgs among its lighter items, and singers built careers on devotional repertoires. M. S. Subbulakshmi’s role as the saint Mīrābāī in the film Meera (1945) and her recordings of Sanskrit hymns made her voice the sound of devotion for millions; Kumar Gandharva’s recordings of the nirguṇī songs of Kabīr and the Nāth yogis of Malwa brought a rural devotional repertoire into the concert hall; and the abhaṅgs of Bhimsen Joshi and the havelī compositions sung by Pandit Jasraj did the same for Maharashtra and Braj.

    The cassette revolution of the 1980s had an even larger effect. Peter Manuel has shown how cheap cassettes allowed devotional music in regional languages, sung by local performers in styles close to everyday practice, to reach audiences far beyond the reach of the film-dominated record industry, and how the producers of devotional cassettes became some of the most successful firms in the music business (Manuel 1993). Film melodies, in turn, became the tunes of new devotional songs, as Kelting’s Jain singers and countless bhajan groups show. Purists deplored the borrowing; for the singers, a familiar tune was a means of carrying devotion, as folk tunes had been for the saints.

    Devotional music has also carried the social tensions of the communities that sing it. The bhakti movements gave a voice to women and to people of low caste — Cokhāmeḷā, Janābāī, Ravidās, Kabīr — and their songs have been sung in the twentieth century as songs of social protest as well as devotion. Yet the professional performance of devotional music in temples has often been the hereditary right of particular castes, and Dalits were long excluded from many of the temples whose music this chapter describes. Women have been central to domestic and congregational singing and largely absent from the professional temple rota. A history of devotional music, like the history of the other arts in this volume, must attend to who sang, where, and for whom, as well as to what was sung.
