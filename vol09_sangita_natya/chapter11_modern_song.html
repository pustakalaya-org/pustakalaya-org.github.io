    Chapter 11: Song in the Modern Languages: Rabīndrasaṅgīt, Nazrulgīti and Their Contemporaries¶

    Between the art music of the concert hall and the anonymous song of the village lies a large body of music that the earlier chapters have met only in passing: the authored song of the modern Indian languages. From the late nineteenth century poets and composers in Bengal, Maharashtra, Assam, the Tamil and Kannada country and the Urdu-speaking north wrote songs in their own languages, set them to melodies drawn from rāga, devotional and folk music and, at times, from European tunes, and published them with their names attached. These songs were sung in homes, schools, political meetings and theatres, carried by the gramophone and the radio, and learnt by millions who never attended a classical concert. Some became the anthems of nations. In Bengal the songs of Rabindranath Tagore and Kazi Nazrul Islam form genres of their own, Rabīndrasaṅgīt and Nazrulgīti, with their own teachers, institutions and standards of correct performance.

    This chapter describes that body of song. It begins with what was new about it, then turns to Bengal, where it took shape first and most fully: the song before Tagore, Tagore’s own songs, the other song-makers of his generation, Nazrul, and the modern and political song of the twentieth century. It then describes the stage song and the lyric song of Maharashtra, the modern song of Assam, the lyric traditions of the south, the popular song of Gujarat and the Punjab, and the Urdu ghazal as a popular song, then the songs of the national movement, and ends with the questions of authorship, notation and fidelity that these traditions share. Film song, which grew out of the same world and soon overshadowed it, is treated in Chapter 23; the poetry of these writers, as literature, in Volume 8, Kāvya Kalpalatā.

    11.1 What Was New about Modern Song¶

    Song with a named author was not new in India. The vāggeyakāra, the composer who wrote both words and music, is described in the Saṅgītaratnākara, and the kṛtis of Tyāgarāja and the pads of the Puṣṭimārga poets carry their makers’ names (Chapters 6, 8 and 9). What changed in the nineteenth and twentieth centuries was the setting in which such songs were made and heard. Four changes stand out.

    The first was the urban, educated, largely middle-class public of the colonial cities — Calcutta, Bombay, Pune, Madras, Guwahati, Lahore — which wanted songs in its own language that were neither the rustic song of the village nor the courtly art of the hereditary musician, and which was suspicious of the courtesan’s salon where much light classical music was heard (Section 7.7). The second was print. Song texts were printed in collections, and from the late nineteenth century melodies too were printed in the new staff-less notations of Bengal and Maharashtra, so that a song could be learnt from a book and its correct form fixed in writing. The third was the new machinery of sound: the harmonium, cheap and easy to play, which became the domestic instrument of the new song (Chapter 10); the gramophone record, whose three-minute side suited the song exactly; and, from the 1930s, the radio. The fourth was politics. Many of the songs were written for the reform movements and for the nationalist struggle, and the movement against the partition of Bengal in 1905, in particular, produced a flood of patriotic songs in Bengali that were sung in processions and meetings.

    The result was a kind of song that its makers and listeners regarded as art but not as śāstrīya (classical) music. Its value lay first in the union of word and melody: the singer was expected to make the words clear and to render the melody as its composer had fixed it, not to improvise. In the language of All India Radio, which created units for it after Independence, it was “light music” (sugam saṅgīt); in Bengali it was simply gān, song, distinguished by its author.

    11.2 Bengal before Tagore¶

    The song of nineteenth-century Calcutta was varied. Rāmnidhi Gupta, known as Nidhu Babu (1741–1839), adapted the Hindustani ṭappā to Bengali love lyrics and made it the fashionable song of the city’s wealthy households; his songs, short, intricate in ornament and frankly secular, were sung for a century. Popular contests of improvised verse (kavigān), in which rival poet-singers answered each other before large audiences, the pāṃcālī narrative songs and the music of the jātrā theatre (Chapter 20) formed the popular end of the spectrum, while the kīrtan of the Vaiṣṇavas remained the great devotional tradition of the countryside (Chapter 9).

    A new kind of song came from religious reform. For the services of the Brahmo Sabha, which he founded in 1828, Rammohan Roy (1772–1833) wrote hymns (brahmasaṅgīt) in Bengali on the formless God of the Upaniṣads, set in the manner of dhrupad, the most dignified of the Hindustani genres; Brahmo services were sung by trained musicians, and the Brahmo hymn became a model of serious, unostentatious song in the mother tongue. The Tagore family of Jorasanko, leading members of the Brahmo community, made their house one of the musical centres of the city. Its members heard and studied Hindustani music with professional musicians — among them the dhrupad singer Jadu Bhatta (Jadunath Bhattacharya, 1840–1883) of the Bishnupur tradition — and also heard European music; Jyotirindranath Tagore (1849–1925), Rabindranath’s elder brother, composed songs and devised the ākārmātrik notation, which writes the notes in Bengali letters with signs for duration above them and was later used for most Bengali song (Som 2009).

    11.3 Rabīndrasaṅgīt¶

    11.3.1 The songs and their arrangement¶

    Rabindranath Tagore (1861–1941) wrote and set to music more than two thousand songs over some sixty years, from his teens until the last months of his life. He regarded them as his most lasting work, and they are the part of it that is most alive in Bengal and Bangladesh today. He collected them in the Gītabitān, first published in 1931–32 with the songs in chronological order; for the revised edition of 1941 he rearranged them not by date but by theme (paryāy), and the collection was completed after his death: songs of worship (pūjā), of the homeland (svadeś), of love (prem), of nature and the seasons (prakṛti), miscellaneous songs (vicitra) and songs for ceremonies (ānuṣṭhānik), with sections for the songs of the plays and dance-dramas. The categories overlap in a characteristic way: many of the “worship” songs are addressed to a divine beloved and many love songs can be read as songs of the divine, and the seasonal songs, above all those of the rains and of spring, carry the deepest feeling of all (Som 2009).

    11.3.2 Sources of the music¶

    Tagore’s melodies came from many sources, which scholars have traced through the phases of his work. In his early years he wrote many songs on the model of Hindustani compositions, often keeping the melody and metre of a known dhrupad or khayāl and replacing its words with Bengali ones; such songs are called bhāṅgā gān (“broken” or “adapted” songs). His musical plays of the early 1880s, Vālmīki-pratibhā (1881) and Kāl-mṛgayā (1882), mixed rāga-based songs with tunes drawn from European, especially Irish and Scottish, melodies he had heard during his stay in England; the best-known song with a European source, Purāno sei diner kathā, follows the melody of “Auld Lang Syne”. From the 1890s, when he managed the family estates in rural east Bengal, he drew increasingly on the kīrtan of the Vaiṣṇavas and on the songs of the Bāuls (Chapter 21), whose mystical idiom and plain melodies he admired; the tune of Āmār sonār Bāṃlā is taken from a song of the Bāul Gagan Harkara.

    Out of these sources he made a style of his own. A Tagore song usually has a short refrain (sthāyī) and a contrasting middle section (antarā), often followed by further stanzas that return to the refrain, in the manner of the Hindustani composition; but the melody follows the shape and stress of the Bengali words closely, ornament is restrained, and the elaboration of the rāga by improvised passages, which the concert singer of khayāl took for granted, is excluded. Many songs use a rāga only as a point of departure, mixing notes of two or more rāgas for expressive effect. Tagore also devised several tālas of his own, among them jhampak, ṣaṣṭhī, rūpakaḍā, navatāl, ekādaśī and navapañcatāl, asymmetrical cycles without the “empty” beat (khālī) of the Hindustani tālas (Chapter 5), designed to follow the rhythm of his verse.

    His essays and conversations on music, collected after his death, show him arguing for this ideal against two opponents: the Hindustani virtuoso, for whom words were a pretext for display, and the singer who took liberties with his tunes. In a well-known exchange with the singer Dilip Kumar Roy (1897–1980), who argued for the performer’s right to embellish, Tagore insisted that his songs be sung as written. He also condemned the harmonium as a crude, tempered instrument unsuited to Indian melody, and it was long excluded from the teaching of his songs at Santiniketan (Sections 10.4.3 and 23.2).

    11.3.3 Plays, dance-dramas and the seasons¶

    Song was at the centre of Tagore’s theatre. His plays are full of songs, and in his last years he created a new form, the dance-drama (nṛtyanāṭya), in which the story is told entirely through song and dance: Citrāṅgadā (1936), Caṇḍālikā (1938), on the love of an untouchable girl for a Buddhist monk, and Śyāmā (1939). The dances drew on Manipuri, which he had brought to Santiniketan from 1919, on Kathakaḷi and on other forms (Chapter 18); his earlier play Naṭīr pūjā (1926), in which a girl of the school danced the role of a court dancer, was a landmark in making dance acceptable for girls of educated Bengali families. At Santiniketan he also created seasonal festivals — the festival of spring (Vasantotsav) and the welcome of the rains (Varṣāmaṅgal) — in which songs, recitation and dance were performed by students and teachers; these festivals, and the spring festival in particular, are still held and widely imitated.

    11.3.4 Anthems¶

    Two of Tagore’s songs are national anthems. Jana-gaṇa-mana, first sung at the Calcutta session of the Indian National Congress in December 1911, was adopted in its first stanza as the national anthem of India in January 1950; Āmār sonār Bāṃlā, written during the agitation against the partition of Bengal in 1905, was adopted by Bangladesh at its independence. The national anthem of Sri Lanka was written and composed by Ananda Samarakoon, a former student at Santiniketan; claims that Tagore wrote or set it are disputed and rest on no direct evidence. The tune of Jana-gaṇa-mana as now sung is close to the version Tagore taught in 1919 at Madanapalle in south India, where Margaret Cousins wrote it down in staff notation.

    11.3.5 Transmission, ownership and fidelity¶

    Tagore’s songs were transmitted from the beginning both orally and in notation. His grandnephew Dinendranath Tagore (1882–1935), a singer of prodigious memory, learnt and notated a large part of them and taught them at Santiniketan; the notations were published in the many volumes of the Svarabitān, and the institution Tagore founded, Visva-Bharati, held the copyright in his works until the end of 2001. A music board of Visva-Bharati approved recordings of the songs, and its insistence on the published notations made Rabīndrasaṅgīt one of the most closely regulated song traditions in the world. The policy preserved the songs from casual distortion; it also led to conflicts with singers whose interpretations departed from the norm, of which the best known involved Debabrata Biswas (1911–1980), whose free and dramatic style was popular with listeners and repeatedly questioned by the board.

    The great singers of the twentieth century — Santidev Ghosh (1910–1999), who had learnt directly from Tagore, Kanika Bandyopadhyay (1924–2000), Suchitra Mitra (1924–2011), Debabrata Biswas, and the film singers Pankaj Mullick and Hemanta Mukherjee, who brought the songs to the cinema and the radio — created distinct “schools” of interpretation within the limits of the notation. Since the copyright lapsed, new arrangements with Western instruments and popular styles have multiplied, to the delight of some listeners and the distress of others.

    In East Pakistan, Tagore’s songs became a political question. When the government of Pakistan discouraged the celebration of his birth centenary in 1961 and later restricted his songs on state radio, Bengali intellectuals and musicians in Dhaka defended them as part of their own heritage; the cultural organisation Chhayanaut, founded in 1961, made the teaching of Rabīndrasaṅgīt and the celebration of the Bengali new year central to a Bengali identity that fed into the movement for independence. Rabīndrasaṅgīt is thus a national tradition of two countries, sung by Hindus and Muslims alike (Som 2009).

    11.4 The Other Song-Makers of Bengal¶

    Tagore was not alone. Bengali tradition groups three other poet-composers with him and with Nazrul as the five makers of the modern Bengali song, each with a recognisable musical personality.

    Dvijendralāl Rāy (1863–1913), a civil servant who studied agriculture in England, wrote plays on historical subjects whose songs were sung throughout Bengal, comic and satirical songs (hāsir gān) that mocked the anglicised Bengali and the hypocrite, and patriotic songs, of which Dhana-dhānya-puṣpa-bharā (“filled with wealth, grain and flowers”) remains one of the best loved. His melodies are vigorous and often choral, with a directness that he admired in English and Scottish song; his son Dilip Kumar Roy became a singer and a writer on music.

    Rajanīkānta Sen (1865–1910), a lawyer of Rajshahi, wrote mainly devotional songs of great simplicity and intensity, and during the agitation of 1905 the patriotic song Māyer deoyā moṭā kāpaṛ (“the coarse cloth given by the mother”), which urged Bengalis to wear Indian-made cloth. His songs are still sung as kāntagīti.

    Atulprasād Sen (1871–1934), a barrister who spent most of his working life in Lucknow, wrote some two hundred songs whose melodies show the influence of the ṭhumrī, dādrā and ghazal he heard there, joined to Bengali kīrtan and Bāul melody. He introduced the lilting manner of the north Indian light classical song into Bengali, and his song in praise of the Bengali language, Moder garab moder āśā, became an anthem of the language movement in East Bengal.

    11.5 Nazrulgīti¶

    11.5.1 The poet as composer¶

    Kazi Nazrul Islam (1899–1976) was born into a poor Muslim family of Churulia in the Bardhaman district of Bengal, sang as a boy with the leṭo troupes, a rural Muslim song-and-theatre form of western Bengal, served in a Bengali regiment of the British Indian army at the end of the First World War, and became famous overnight in 1922 with his poem “The Rebel” (Bidrohī; see Volume 8). In the following two decades he wrote and composed songs on a scale without parallel in modern Bengali: estimates of his output range from about three thousand to more than four thousand songs, many of them made for the gramophone and the radio. His working life as a composer ended in 1942, when he was struck by an illness that left him unable to speak or write for the remaining thirty-four years of his life. In 1972 the new state of Bangladesh brought him to Dhaka, where he died in 1976 and is honoured as the national poet; his marching song Cal cal cal became the military march of Bangladesh.

    11.5.2 Range and style¶

    Nazrul’s songs cover almost every genre known in Bengal, and several that he introduced. He brought the Persian and Urdu ghazal into Bengali, with its couplets, refrain and imagery of the nightingale and the rose, and his Bengali ghazals, sung in a light classical style close to that of the Urdu ghazal singers, were among the most popular records of the late 1920s and 1930s. With the singer Abbasuddin Ahmed (1901–1959) he created a repertoire of Islamic devotional songs in Bengali, of which the song for the end of the fast of Ramaḍān, O man ramjāner oi rojār śeṣe (1931), is still sung throughout Bangladesh at Īd; there had been little such song in Bengali before, and it gave Bengali Muslims a devotional music in their own language. At the same time he wrote many hundreds of songs to the goddess Kālī (śyāmāsaṅgīt) and to Kṛṣṇa, in the manner of the Bengali Śākta and Vaiṣṇava traditions, so that his work as a whole crosses the line between the communities in a way that both Indian and Bangladeshi admirers have emphasised.

    Nazrul was also a serious student of Hindustani music, and many of his songs are rāga-based compositions in the manner of khayāl or ṭhumrī. For a series of radio programmes in Calcutta around 1940 he composed songs in new rāgas of his own, with Bengali names such as Dolan-cāṃpā, Veṇukā and Sandhyā-mālatī; accounts of the number of such rāgas vary. He drew too on folk song, on Middle Eastern and Latin American dance rhythms heard on records, and on marching songs; several of his books of revolutionary poems and songs were banned by the colonial government. He worked for the Gramophone Company in Calcutta as a composer and trainer of singers from the late 1920s, wrote and directed music for the early Bengali sound cinema, and composed for the Calcutta station of the radio.

    11.5.3 Notation and authenticity¶

    Because Nazrul’s songs were made quickly, for the record industry, and his illness came before he could supervise their publication, their texts and melodies survive in many variants. The singers who had learnt from him — among them the composer Kamal Dasgupta (1912–1974) and the singer Feroza Begum (1930–2014), both of whom later settled in Dhaka — became the authorities for correct versions. In Bangladesh the Nazrul Institute, founded in Dhaka in 1985, has published standardised notations and recordings, and the question of authentic versions, as with Tagore’s songs, is debated between those who would fix the songs and those who value the variety of their performance.

    11.6 Modern Song, Mass Song and After in Bengal¶

    The gramophone and the radio created, from the 1930s, a third category beside the songs of the great poet-composers: the “modern song” (ādhunik gān), written by professional lyricists and set by professional composers for recording. Its first masters included the composer Himangshu Dutta (1908–1944), Kamal Dasgupta, and Sachin Dev Burman, who sang Bengali songs before he became a composer of Hindi film music in Bombay; and Pankaj Mullick (1905–1978), who as a composer and singer worked across Tagore songs, film and radio, and whose music for the dawn programme Mahiṣāsuramardinī, broadcast every year since the early 1930s on the morning that begins the Durgā Pūjā season, remains one of the best-loved radio programmes in Bengal. The decades after Independence were the high period of ādhunik gān: the singers Hemanta Mukherjee (1920–1989), Manna Dey (1919–2013) and Sandhya Mukherjee (1931–2022), the lyricist Gauriprasanna Majumdar and many others produced the songs released each autumn for the Pūjā season, a commercial calendar that shaped Bengali popular music for half a century.

    A different strand came from the left. The Indian People’s Theatre Association (Chapter 19) created in Bengal a “mass song” (gaṇasaṅgīt) for choirs and political meetings. Jyotirindra Moitra’s song-cycle Navajībaner gān on the Bengal famine of 1943–44, the songs of Hemanga Biswas (1912–1987), who drew on the folk music of Assam and Sylhet, and those of Salil Chowdhury (1925–1995), who combined folk melody, Western harmony and choral writing and later became one of the most original composers of the Hindi and Bengali cinema, made the political song an art in its own right. In East Pakistan the songs of the language movement after 1952 and of the liberation war of 1971, broadcast by the clandestine radio of the independence movement, played a similar role.

    From the 1970s a younger generation reacted against the conventions of both ādhunik gān and the film song. The Calcutta band Moheener Ghoraguli, founded in 1975, set Bengali poetry to a mixture of rock, Bāul and folk music, and in 1992 Suman Chattopadhyay (later Kabir Suman) released Tomāke cāi, an album of songs about city life sung to his own guitar, which began a movement of “songs about life” (jībanmukhī gān) and of Bengali bands that continues today.

    11.7 Maharashtra: Nāṭyasaṅgīt and Bhāvgīt¶

    11.7.1 The stage song¶

    The Marathi musical drama (saṅgīt nāṭak), described as theatre in Chapter 19, produced the most important body of semi-classical song in western India. Its songs, called pads, were the main attraction of the plays, and audiences demanded encores that could extend a performance far into the night. Annasaheb Kirloskar’s Saṅgīt Śākuntal (1880) and Saṅgīt Saubhadra (1882) set their songs mainly to the tunes and metres of the Marathi kīrtan (Chapter 9), the lāvaṇī and other popular forms — the sākī, diṇḍī and āryā metres of the kīrtankārs were sung in recognisable melodies. With Govind Ballal Deval’s Saṅgīt Śāradā (1899), a play against the marriage of young girls to old men, and above all in the plays of the early twentieth century, the songs moved closer to Hindustani art music. Composers took their melodies from the bandiśes of khayāl, ṭhumrī and ṭappā and from Karnatak songs heard from touring companies, and shaped them into short, memorable pads that a trained actor-singer could decorate with tāns. Govindrao Tembe (1881–1955) composed the music of K. P. Khadilkar’s Saṅgīt Mānāpamān (1911), and the singer Bhaskarbuwa Bakhle (1869–1922), trained in the Gwalior, Agra and Jaipur styles, that of Saṅgīt Svayaṃvar (1916) (Ranade 1986).

    The singer-actors became the stars of Marathi public life. Bal Gandharva (1888–1967), whose own company, the Gandharva Natak Mandali, was founded in 1913, sang women’s roles in a voice of great sweetness, with Master Krishnarao Phulambrikar as his composer; Keshavrao Bhosale (1890–1921) of the Lalitkaladarsh company and Master Dinanath Mangeshkar (1900–1942), the father of Lata Mangeshkar, were his rivals. The stage song spread far beyond the theatre through the gramophone, and nāṭyasaṅgīt became, as Ashok Ranade has shown, one of the main bridges by which Hindustani music reached a mass Marathi audience (Ranade 1986). The coming of sound cinema in the 1930s ruined the companies, but the songs survived on record and on the concert platform; in the 1960s the composer Jitendra Abhisheki (1929–1998) gave the form a new lease of life with the music of Katyār kāḷjāt ghusalī (1967), and nāṭyasaṅgīt is still a standard part of the repertoire of Marathi classical singers.

    11.7.2 Bhāvgīt and the radio¶

    The lyric song (bhāvgīt), a setting of a modern Marathi poem for solo voice and small ensemble, grew from the 1920s and 1930s with the gramophone and the radio, and became one of the main forms of Marathi light music. Its most celebrated work is a radio series. The Gīt Rāmāyaṇ, fifty-six songs on the story of Rāma by the poet G. D. Madgulkar (1919–1977), set to music and sung by Sudhir Phadke (1919–2002), was broadcast weekly by All India Radio, Pune, from April 1955 to April 1956; it drew huge audiences, was published and recorded, and has been performed on stage ever since. Later composers, among them Hridaynath Mangeshkar, set the poetry of the Marathi saints and of modern poets with equal success, and the bhāvgīt became a model for the light music of other languages on the radio.

    11.8 Assam: Jyotiprasad, Bishnu Rabha and Bhupen Hazarika¶

    In Assam the modern song is closely tied to three artists whose names have become the names of genres. Jyotiprasad Agarwala (1903–1951), playwright, poet and film-maker, directed the first Assamese film, Joymatī (1935), and wrote songs that combined the borgīt and folk melodies of Assam (Chapter 9) with Western and Bengali influences; they are sung as Jyoti-saṅgīt. Bishnu Prasad Rabha (1909–1969), revolutionary, painter, dancer and musician, called Kalāguru (“master of the arts”), studied the music of the Bodo, Rabha, Mising and other peoples of the Brahmaputra valley and brought their melodies into his songs, which are sung as Bishnu Rabha saṅgīt. The poet Parbati Prasad Baruva (1904–1964) wrote lyrics of great delicacy for the same tradition.

    Bhupen Hazarika (1926–2011), who sang as a child in Jyotiprasad’s second film, studied in Banaras and at Columbia University in New York, where he met the singer Paul Robeson, and returned to Assam to become the most popular singer and composer of the north-east. Working with the Indian People’s Theatre Association, he wrote songs of the Brahmaputra and its boatmen, of tea-garden labourers, of the unity of the valley’s peoples and of human solidarity; his best-known song, Bistīrṇa pārare, on the indifference of the great river to the suffering on its banks, takes its idea and its melody from Robeson’s “Ol’ Man River”. He composed for Assamese, Bengali and Hindi films, received the Dadasaheb Phalke Award for 1992 and was awarded the Bharat Ratna posthumously in 2019. The modern Assamese song as a whole, carried after Independence by the radio station at Guwahati, became a central part of Assamese identity (Chapter 22).

    11.9 The South: Bharati and the Lyric Song¶

    In the Tamil country the poet Subramania Bharati (1882–1921) wrote patriotic, devotional and philosophical songs that he indicated for singing in named rāgas and tālas, in a Tamil far simpler than that of classical poetry. His songs of the nation, of Śakti, of Kṛṣṇa as child, lover and friend, and of the liberation of women were taken up after his death by the nationalist movement and by concert singers — D. K. Pattammal, for one, was known for singing them — and set again and again for the cinema; in 1949 the government of Madras bought the rights to his works and placed them in the public domain. The Tamil Isai movement (Section 8.9) drew on Bharati’s example, and composers such as Papanasam Sivan (1890–1973) wrote Tamil kṛtis for the concert stage and songs for the Tamil cinema, where Tamil song soon found its largest audience.

    In Karnataka the lyric song of modern Kannada poetry (bhāvagīte) became, through the radio, a genre called sugama saṅgīta. P. Kalinga Rao (1914–1981) was among its first popular singers and composers, and Mysore Ananthaswamy (1936–1995) and C. Ashwath (1938–2009) set the poems of Kuvempu, D. R. Bendre, K. S. Narasimhaswamy and others for voice and small ensemble, creating a repertoire sung at festivals, in schools and on stage. Kuvempu’s poem Jaya Bhārata jananiya tanujāte (1924) was declared the state song of Karnataka in 2004, and the choice between the musical settings of Ananthaswamy and Ashwath became a public controversy, reaching the High Court of Karnataka in 2022 — a sign of how seriously the authorship of a tune is taken in this tradition. The radio stations of Andhra and Kerala fostered similar genres, Telugu lalita saṅgītam and Malayalam lalitagānam, whose composers and singers were often trained in Karnatak music.

    11.10 Gujarat and the Punjab¶

    In Gujarat the modern song grew from two roots: the devotional pads of Narsinh Mehta and the other saint-poets, and the songs of the garbā, the circle dance of the Navarātri festival (Chapter 21). The Gujarati theatre companies of Bombay and the Gujarati cinema from the 1930s added their own repertoire, and the composer Avinash Vyas (1912–1984), who wrote and set hundreds of songs for Gujarati films and for the stage, composed many garbā songs that became the standard repertoire of the festival. After Independence the radio station at Ahmedabad fostered a Gujarati sugam saṅgīt of settings of modern poets, and its composers and singers — Purushottam Upadhyay among them — carried it to the concert stage. The vast urban garbā of today, with its professional singers and amplified bands, draws much of its music from these composed songs rather than from village tradition.

    Punjabi popular song developed largely outside the cinema. On records from the 1930s and, after Partition, on both sides of the new border, singers adapted the folk genres of the Punjab — the wedding songs, the bolīs of the giddhā and bhāṅgṛā, the māhiyā and ṭappe couplets, the ballads of lovers such as Hīr and Rāṃjhā — for urban audiences. In India, Surinder Kaur (1929–2006), often singing with her sister Prakash Kaur, made the Punjabi wedding and folk song a respectable recorded genre; Lal Chand Yamla Jatt popularised the one-stringed plucked tumbī; and Asa Singh Mastana was among the most popular male singers of the 1950s and 1960s. In the 1970s and 1980s a style of earthy, often bawdy male–female duets, performed at village gatherings (akhāṛā) and sold on records and cassettes, made Amar Singh Chamkila the most popular Punjabi singer of his time; his murder in 1988, during the violence of that decade, has made him a subject of continuing debate about the morality and the social world of Punjabi song. In Pakistan, Alam Lohar, who accompanied himself on the tongs (cimṭā) and made the jugnī song famous, and the singer Reshma, of a nomadic community, carried the Punjabi and Rajasthani folk song to national audiences. The Punjabi song of the diaspora, transformed into a dance music in Britain, is described in Chapter 23.

    11.11 The Urdu Ghazal as Popular Song¶

    In the Urdu-speaking north the ghazal was already a sung form in the salons of the courtesans (Section 7.7). In the twentieth century, as Peter Manuel has shown, it became a popular song in a series of stages: the gramophone records of the courtesan singers of the 1900s; the theatre and early cinema, which used ghazals as songs; the light classical concert ghazal of Begum Akhtar in India and of Mehdi Hassan (1927–2012) in Pakistan, sung with harmonium, sāraṅgī and tablā and elaborated with the techniques of ṭhumrī; and, from the 1970s, a simplified “pop ghazal” with guitar, violins and electronic instruments, sung by Jagjit Singh (1941–2011) and others, whose album The Unforgettables (1976), with Chitra Singh, opened a large new market (Manuel 1988). The ghazal-song carried Urdu poetry, and with it an idiom of love, longing and irony, to audiences who no longer read the script. It also carried politics: Faiz Ahmad Faiz’s poem Ham dekheṅge, sung by Iqbal Bano at a concert in Lahore in 1986 under the military government of Zia ul-Haq, became an anthem of protest on both sides of the border. The Hindi gīt and the Punjabi song had similar histories on the radio and on record, and the songs of the Partition generation, like the ghazal, remain a shared repertoire of India and Pakistan (Chapter 23).

    11.12 Songs of the Nation¶

    Many of the songs described in this chapter were made for, or taken up by, the national movement, and song was one of its most effective instruments. Meetings opened and closed with song, processions sang through the streets, and a song could be learnt and repeated by people who could not read the pamphlets and newspapers of the movement. The colonial government recognised the danger: song-books were among the publications proscribed under the press laws, plays and jātrās with patriotic songs were censored under the Dramatic Performances Act of 1876 (Chapter 19), and singers such as the jātrā poet Mukunda Das (Chapter 20) and Nazrul were imprisoned.

    11.12.1 Vande Mātaram¶

    The first great song of the movement was Bankim Chandra Chattopadhyay’s hymn to the motherland as goddess, Vande Mātaram, written in a Sanskritised Bengali in the 1870s and placed in his novel Ānandamaṭh (1882), whose story of ascetic rebels made the song a call to arms (see Volume 24). Tagore set it to music and sang it at the Calcutta session of the Congress in 1896, and during the agitation against the partition of Bengal in 1905 its first words became the slogan of the movement throughout India. It was sung to many tunes, regional and rāga-based, and the Gandharva Mahavidyalaya singer V. D. Paluskar made a point of singing it at Congress sessions; a well-known account describes him insisting on singing it in full at the Kakinada session of 1923, despite the objection of the Congress president, Maulana Mohammad Ali, to music at the session. The later stanzas of the song, which identify the motherland with Durgā and other goddesses, were objected to by many Muslims, and in 1937 the Congress recommended that only the first two stanzas be sung at national gatherings. When the Constituent Assembly adopted Jana-gaṇa-mana as the national anthem in January 1950, Vande Mātaram was declared the national song, to be honoured equally with it. The history of the song shows how a devotional idiom could carry national feeling and, by the same token, divide those who did not share the devotion.

    11.12.2 Gandhi and the bhajan¶

    Gandhi made congregational singing a daily discipline of the movement. The prayer meetings of his ashrams at Sabarmati and Wardha, and later the public prayer meetings of his tours, were built around the singing of devotional songs from many traditions, collected in the Āśram Bhajanāvalī. The music was in the charge of Narayan Moreshwar Khare, a pupil of Paluskar, who taught at the Sabarmati ashram and set the hymns to simple melodies for congregational singing. Two songs became inseparable from Gandhi’s name: Narsinh Mehta’s Gujarati Vaiṣṇava jana to, which defines the true devotee as one who feels the pain of others, and the chant Raghupati Rāghava Rājā Rām, in the version sung at his meetings with the line declaring that Īśvara and Allāh are names of one God. The morning procession through a town singing such songs (prabhāt pherī) became a common nationalist practice in the 1920s and 1930s. Gandhi’s choice was deliberate: the bhajan drew on the shared devotional culture of village India (Chapter 9), required no training, and made every participant a singer.

    11.12.3 Anthems, marches and laments¶

    The movement had many other songs. Muhammad Iqbal’s Sāre jahāṅ se acchā (1904), on the beauty of India, was sung in schools and at meetings, and is still played by Indian military bands (see Volume 8). In south-east Asia, Subhas Chandra Bose’s Indian National Army adopted Śubh sukh cain, a Hindustani version of Jana-gaṇa-mana, with music arranged by Captain Ram Singh Thakuri, who also composed its march Qadam qadam baṛhāye jā. The Punjabi revolutionaries of the Ghadar movement circulated their poems in printed collections to be sung and recited, and in every region the forms of folk song — the Marathi povāḍā, the Bengali jātrā song, the Tamil songs of Bharati — were turned to political use. After Independence the patriotic song passed largely to the cinema and the radio: Ai mere vatan ke logo, written by the poet Pradeep and composed by C. Ramchandra, sung by Lata Mangeshkar at a public function in Delhi in January 1963 in memory of the soldiers who died in the war with China, is the best-known example of a film-industry song that became a national lament.

    The national song had its limits. It spoke in the idiom of the communities that made it — often an upper-caste Hindu idiom of the motherland as goddess — and the controversy over Vande Mātaram shows that the same song could unite some listeners and exclude others. Dalit, Ādivāsī and regional movements produced songs of their own, from the Ambedkarite jalsās of Maharashtra (Chapter 20) to the songs of the Jharkhand and north-eastern movements, in which the nation of the anthems was not always the community that mattered most.

    The first is the idea of the song as a fixed work. Unlike the bandiś of the Hindustani singer, which is a framework for improvisation, a song of Tagore, Nazrul or the Gīt Rāmāyaṇ is a finished composition in which words, melody and rhythm belong together, and the singer’s task is to realise it faithfully. Notation, copyright and the authority of institutions — Visva-Bharati, the Nazrul Institute, the radio — were the means by which this idea was enforced, and the tension between fidelity and interpretation, which no notation can resolve, runs through the history of all these traditions.

    The second is their social base. The modern song was made largely by and for the educated middle class, and it allowed women of “respectable” families to sing in public, on record and on the radio at a time when the professional woman singer was still associated with the courtesan. Many of the most admired singers of Rabīndrasaṅgīt and bhāvgīt were such women. At the same time the songs drew constantly on the music of others — the courtesan’s ṭhumrī and ghazal, the Bāul’s song, the kīrtan, the tribal melody — and the history of these borrowings, like that of the classical revival, raises questions about whose music was being made respectable.

    The third is the bond between song and political community. The modern song gave each of the major languages an emblem of itself, sung at its festivals and its protests, and several of its songs became the anthems of nation-states. That it could do so is a measure of how central song, rather than the instrumental or the classical art, has been to the public life of modern South Asia.
