    Chapter 13: Nṛtta, Nṛtya and Nāṭya¶

    Indian writing on dance distinguishes three modes of performance: nṛtta, pure or abstract dance, in which movement is organised by rhythm and has no representational content; nṛtya, expressive dance, in which the dancer conveys the meaning and feeling of a song or verse; and nāṭya, drama, in which performers enact a story with characters. The distinction is a theoretical one — most dance forms combine all three, often within a single item — but it has proved remarkably useful, both to the medieval theorists who formulated it and to the twentieth-century teachers who organised the classical repertoires around it. Watch a Bharatanāṭyam varṇam and the three modes alternate before one’s eyes: a passage of rhythmic footwork to the teacher’s recited syllables, then a line of the song interpreted through gesture and face, then, perhaps, a few moments in which the dancer becomes a character in a story. The history of the distinction, the units and compositions of pure dance, the song-based items of expressive dance and the conventions of dance-drama are taken in turn, followed by the modern recital and the gendered pairing of tāṇḍava and lāsya, a pairing that has shaped the discussion of Indian dance more than its history warrants.

    13.1 The History of the Distinction¶

    The Nāṭyaśāstra speaks of drama (nāṭya) and of dance (nṛtta). Its chapter on the tāṇḍava explains that nṛtta, unlike drama, does not depend on the meaning of a text; it is added to the performance because it creates beauty and because it is auspicious and loved by the gods (Chapter 2). The expressive mode that later writers called nṛtya is implicit in Bharata’s treatment of acting and of the songs (dhruvā) and dance-items (lāsyāṅga) of the stage, but it is not a separate category.

    The threefold distinction appears in the dramaturgical and dance literature of the late first and early second millennia. Dhanañjaya’s Daśarūpaka (late tenth century), a handbook of drama, distinguishes nṛtya, which “rests on feeling” (bhāvāśraya), from nṛtta, which “rests on rhythm and tempo” (tālalayāśraya), and both from drama, which rests on rasa. The Abhinayadarpaṇa defines nāṭya as the enactment of a traditional story, nṛtta as dance devoid of mood and expression, and nṛtya as dance that expresses rasa and bhāva (Chapter 12). Śārṅgadeva’s Saṅgītaratnākara (thirteenth century) adopts the three categories and cross-classifies nṛtta and nṛtya as either vigorous (tāṇḍava) or delicate (lāsya) (Bose 1991; Shringy and Sharma 1978). In twentieth-century writing, beginning with Coomaraswamy and Kapila Vatsyayan, the three terms became the standard framework for describing the classical forms (Vatsyayan 1968, 1974).

    13.2 Nṛtta: Pure Dance¶

    13.2.1 The aesthetics of pure dance¶

    In nṛtta the body creates patterns in space and time: lines of the arms and legs, symmetrical and asymmetrical postures, paths across the floor, and above all the articulation of rhythm by the feet in relation to the music. Its pleasure is the pleasure of form and of rhythm — of precision, energy and the exact coincidence of movement with the beat. Writers have compared it with music without words or with geometry; the comparisons are apt as long as they are not taken as claims about mathematics.

    13.2.2 Basic posture¶

    Each dance form has a basic stance from which its movement proceeds. In Bharatanāṭyam it is the half-seated araimaṇḍi (Tamil, “half-sitting”), with the knees bent outwards and the heels together, which corresponds to the āyata posture of the texts; Kuchipudi uses a similar position. Odissi alternates between the square, wide-legged cauka and the three-bend tribhaṅga, with the body curved at neck, waist and knee as in Odishan temple sculpture (Chapter 17). Kathak dancers stand upright, with the weight on flat feet, which allows rapid footwork and turns. Kathakaḷi actors stand with the feet apart and the weight on the outer edges of the feet, knees bent outwards, a stance derived from the training of Kerala’s martial art (Chapter 18). Manipuri dancers keep the knees soft and the movement continuous and rounded, with no sharp angles.

    13.2.3 Units of movement¶

    The pure dance of each form is built from basic units. In Bharatanāṭyam these are the aḍavus (Tamil, “step” or “placing”), each a short combination of footwork, leg and arm positions and hand shapes performed to a rhythmic syllable pattern. They are grouped into families named after their characteristic action or syllables — among them taṭṭaḍavu (striking the feet), nāṭṭaḍavu (stretching the leg), kuditta meṭṭaḍavu (jumping and striking), tattimeṭṭaḍavu, sarikkal (sliding) and maṇḍi (kneeling) aḍavus — and each is practised in three speeds. The number of aḍavus taught, and their names, differ between schools; the systematic sequences taught today were organised by twentieth-century teachers, especially at Kalakshetra (O’Shea 2007). Kathak’s units are the patterns of footwork (tatkār) and turns (cakkar), Odissi’s the basic steps and sculptural poses (bhaṅgi) taught in the reconstructed curriculum of the 1950s, and Kathakaḷi’s the dance passages called kalaśam, which punctuate the sung text and end with characteristic leaps and turns.

    13.2.4 Rhythmic compositions¶

    Pure dance is organised by compositions of rhythmic syllables, recited by the dance master or drummer and articulated by the dancer’s feet and body (Viswanathan and Allen 2004; Kippen 1988). Table 13.1 compares the terms used in different forms.

    In the southern forms, a jati is a passage of syllables (sollukaṭṭu) composed to fit the tāla and danced as a sequence of aḍavus; the term is unrelated to the jātis that classify the length of the laghu in tāla theory (Chapter 5). A passage of pure dance usually ends with a tīrmāṉam (“conclusion”) or aruḍi, a cadential phrase repeated three times so that its last syllable falls on the beat on which the song resumes. Longer cadences, korvais, combine several phrases. Their shapes can follow traditional designs called yati: in the “cow’s tail” (gopuccha) design the phrases become progressively shorter, in the “river” (srotovaha) design progressively longer, and in the “drum” (ḍamaru) and “mṛdaṅga” designs they first shrink and then grow, or the reverse.

    In Kathak the compositions are those of the tablā and pakhāvaj — ṭukṛā, toḍā, paran, tihāī — recited (paḍhant) by the dancer before being danced, and many end with a tihāī or a chakkardār composition whose final stroke falls on sam. Kathak dancers also engage in rhythmic dialogue with the drummer, and in the most virtuosic passages the dancer’s footwork becomes a percussion instrument (Chapter 16).

    Form Basic units Rhythmic compositions and cadences
    Bharatanāṭyam aḍavu families; araimaṇḍi jati, sollukaṭṭu, tīrmāṉam, aruḍi, korvai
    Kuchipudi aḍugus (steps), jumps jatis, tīrmāṉams
    Odissi basic steps and bhaṅgis (sculptural poses); cauka, tribhaṅga bols, ukuṭa (syllables recited)
    Kathak tatkār, cakkar toḍā, ṭukṛā, paran, tihāī, chakkardār

    13.2.5 The dance master and the musicians¶

    Pure dance is inseparable from its music. In Bharatanāṭyam the naṭṭuvaṉār, the dance master, conducts the performance by reciting the rhythmic syllables and beating time on a pair of small cymbals (tāḷam); the art of conducting, naṭṭuvāṅgam, was for centuries the hereditary profession of men of the families whose women were the dancers, and is now often practised by the dancer’s teacher, male or female (Chapter 15). The mṛdaṅgam player follows the dancer’s feet and the naṭṭuvaṉār’s syllables. In Kathak the tablā or pakhāvaj player and the dancer are partners in a rhythmic dialogue. In Kathakaḷi the drummers on ceṇṭa and maddaḷam accompany every movement of the actor’s body and hands. In Manipuri the drummer may himself dance, as in the pung cholom (Chapter 18).

    13.3 Nṛtya: Expressive Dance¶

    13.3.1 Dancing a song¶

    In nṛtya the dancer interprets the words of a song or verse through gesture, facial expression and movement. The dancer uses the hand gestures (Chapter 14) to show the objects and actions named in the text, the eyes and face to convey the feelings, and the movement of the whole body to embody the character speaking — most often a woman addressing her lover, her confidante or a god. The principles are those of abhinaya described in Chapter 3.

    A line of text is rarely shown only once. In the southern forms the singer repeats each line many times, and the dancer interprets it differently on each repetition (sañcāri): first showing the literal meaning, then elaborating an image or an implied situation, then showing the emotion in a succession of transitory states. A line such as “the moon is burning me”, sung by a woman separated from her lover, may become in performance a scene of evening, the rising moon, the cool breezes that should soothe her and instead torment her, the memory of the lover’s last visit and her anger at his absence. This improvised elaboration (manodharma) is the highest test of an expressive dancer; Balasaraswati, by common consent the greatest Bharatanāṭyam dancer of the twentieth century, was celebrated for it (Knight 2010).

    13.3.2 The genres of expressive dance¶

    Each form has its expressive genres. In Bharatanāṭyam and Kuchipudi they include the śabdam, a song of praise with short descriptive passages; the varṇam, the long central item that alternates pure dance and expression; the slow love-songs called padam, among them the Telugu padams of Kṣetrayya (seventeenth century); the lighter jāvaḷis of the nineteenth century; and Sanskrit verses (ślokas) and devotional songs. Odissi’s main expressive texts are the songs (aṣṭapadī) of Jayadeva’s Gītagovinda and Odia devotional poetry. Kathak dancers interpret ṭhumrī, bhajan and ghazal, often seated or standing almost still, in an improvised style (bhāv batānā, “showing the feeling”) developed by the courtesans and hereditary dancers of nineteenth-century Lucknow and Banaras. Mohiniyāṭṭam uses Malayalam and Sanskrit songs, many composed at the court of Travancore in the nineteenth century, and Sattriya the devotional songs (borgīt) of the Assamese Vaiṣṇava saints.

    Much of this repertoire was the property of hereditary women performers, created for them by court poets and composers and transmitted by them. Its frank eroticism, which the devotional tradition read as a language for the love of god, was one of the reasons why twentieth-century reformers attacked the women who performed it and why the reconstructed classical forms “purified” parts of it (Chapters 3 and 15) (Soneji 2012).

    13.3.3 Expressive elaboration in the Kerala theatre¶

    The Kerala theatres developed the most elaborate techniques of expressive elaboration in India. In Kūṭiyāṭṭam, an actor may take a single verse of a Sanskrit play and spend an hour or more developing it: before speaking the verse he enacts, in gesture alone, the situation behind it, the memories it evokes and the actions it implies. In the technique called pakarnnāṭṭam (“transformed acting”) a single actor plays several characters in turn, showing each by a change of stance and expression; the battle between Rāvaṇa and Jaṭāyu, or the lifting of Mount Kailāsa, may be performed by one actor representing all the participants. Kathakaḷi actors use similar techniques in the passages of free elaboration called iḷakiyāṭṭam, in which an actor departs from the sung text to describe a scene, a memory or a reflection — the beauty of a forest, the sight of a pond, the lament of a bird — before returning to the play (Chapters 18 and 19) (Zarrilli 2000; Richmond et al. 1990).

    13.3.4 Abhinaya with the whole body¶

    Expressive dance is not only a matter of hands and face. Dancers use the gaits and postures of characters (the swaying walk of a woman, the swagger of a hero), the direction of the eyes to establish where an imagined person or object is, and the movement of the whole body to create a scene: rowing a boat across a river, plucking flowers from a tree, bathing in a pond, churning butter. They also move between characters and narrator within a single song, now speaking as the heroine, now showing the absent lover, now describing the scene from outside. This fluid shifting of role and viewpoint, which requires no change of costume, is one of the most distinctive features of Indian solo dance and one of the hardest to learn.

    13.4 Space and the Group¶

    Most of the modern classical recital forms are solo forms, and much writing on Indian dance treats the solo dancer as the norm. Historically, however, group dance was common. The Nāṭyaśāstra describes group formations (piṇḍībandha) in the preliminaries of the theatre; the medieval treatises describe group dances such as the carcarī, the rāsaka and the stick dances (Chapter 12); the temple and court dancers of the south often danced in groups at festivals and processions; and the Rāsalīlā of Manipur and Braj, the group dances of Kerala and the dance-dramas are all ensemble forms. Folk and tribal dance is overwhelmingly communal, performed in circles and lines (Chapter 21).

    The use of space also differs between forms. Bharatanāṭyam and Kuchipudi move along lines, diagonals and squares on a defined floor, often returning to centre-stage for cadences; Odissi traces curved paths; Kathak uses straight lines towards and away from the audience and turns on the spot; Kathakaḷi and Kūṭiyāṭṭam act on a small square stage before a lamp. The twentieth-century proscenium stage, with its frontal audience, lighting and curtains, changed the spatial practice of all these forms: it encouraged the frontal presentation of the solo dancer, and it made possible the large group choreographies of the dance-dramas created from the 1930s by Rukmini Devi at Kalakshetra, by Uday Shankar and by others, which drew on the vocabulary of the classical forms to tell stories with many dancers (Meduri 2005; Purkayastha 2014).

    13.5 Dance and the Martial Arts¶

    Several of India’s dance and theatre forms are closely related to its martial traditions, and the relation illuminates how the dancing body has been trained. The Nāṭyaśāstra already describes the standing positions of archery and the movements of combat among its cārīs and maṇḍalas, and stage fights were an important part of the theatre it describes (Chapter 2). In Kerala, the martial art kaḷarippayaṯṯu, practised in training halls (kaḷari) by the Nāyar warriors and other communities, supplied Kathakaḷi with much of its training regime: its kicks, jumps, stretches and body-preparation exercises, its massage, and its emphasis on the control of breath and the concentration of energy in the lower abdomen. Phillip Zarrilli’s studies show how the same body techniques serve both arts and how practitioners understand the inner energy they cultivate (Zarrilli 1998, 2000).

    Other regions show similar links. The movement vocabulary of Chhau, especially in Mayurbhanj and Seraikela, draws on the martial exercises of the paika soldiers of Odisha and the surrounding region, and the dance was associated with martial displays at the courts that patronised it (Chapter 21). In Manipur, the sword and spear art thang-ta has influenced the vigorous male dances and the stage choreography of Manipuri dance, and the theatre of Ratan Thiyam has drawn on it extensively (Chapters 18 and 19). Many folk dances are or include stick and sword dances, which preserve the movements of combat in stylised form. In the twentieth century choreographers such as Chandralekha turned to kaḷarippayaṯṯu and yoga as sources of a strong, grounded movement that could be set against the decorative image of the classical dancer (Bharucha 1995).

    The relation between dance and yoga is often asserted, but it should be stated carefully. The postural yoga taught today is largely a modern development, and the claim that the classical dance forms derive from yoga, or that particular dance movements correspond to particular āsanas, has no basis in the texts. What is true is that both dance and yoga, like the martial arts, belong to a culture in which the trained body was understood as a vehicle of concentration and transformation, and that many modern dancers practise yoga as part of their training (Volume 5, Yoga Taraṅgiṇī).

    13.6 The Music of Dance¶

    Each form has its own musical ensemble, and its music is as much a part of its identity as its movement. Bharatanāṭyam is accompanied by the naṭṭuvaṉār with cymbals, a singer, the mṛdaṅgam, and usually violin, flute or vīṇā; its music is Karnatak, though the dance repertoire includes compositions (varṇams, padams, jāvaḷis, tillānas) that are rarely heard in concerts. Kuchipudi uses a similar ensemble, with songs mostly in Telugu. Odissi is accompanied by the mardala (a barrel drum), a singer, flute, violin or sitār and cymbals, playing a regional repertoire of rāgas and songs that its musicians have presented as a distinct “Odissi music”. Kathak uses Hindustani music, with tablā or pakhāvaj, sāraṅgī or harmonium, and a singer. Kathakaḷi has two singers, who sing the text of the play with cymbals and a gong, and drummers on the ceṇṭa, maddaḷam and iḍakka. Manipuri is accompanied by the pung drum, cymbals, flute and singers in the Vaiṣṇava devotional tradition. In every case the dancer must know the music thoroughly, and the best dancers are often also accomplished singers or drummers.

    13.7 Nāṭya: Dance-Drama¶

    13.7.1 Dance-drama traditions¶

    In the nāṭya mode a group of performers enacts a story, each taking a character. Many of India’s performance traditions are dance-dramas of this kind: Kūṭiyāṭṭam, which preserves Sanskrit drama in a Kerala performance style; Kathakaḷi and its precursors Kṛṣṇanāṭṭam and Rāmanāṭṭam; the Kuchipudi yakṣagānas or dance-dramas such as Bhāmākalāpam; the Bhāgavata Mēḷa of the Tamil country; the Yakṣagāna of Karnataka; the Ankīyā Nāṭ of Assam; the Rāsalīlā of Manipur and of Braj; and the Rāmlīlā of north India (Chapters 17, 18 and 20). In these forms the performers are at once dancers, actors and, in some forms, singers; the drama is carried by song and narration as much as by dialogue; and the performance may last a whole night or, in Kūṭiyāṭṭam, many nights.

    13.7.2 Conventions of dance-drama¶

    Dance-drama has its own conventions. Characters announce themselves at their entrance with a song (pravēśa daru in Kuchipudi) or a dance (the puṟappāṭu of Kathakaḷi); a stage manager or narrator (sūtradhāra, bhāgavata) introduces the story and links its episodes; characters are classified by type and costumed accordingly; and the stage is defined by convention rather than scenery, so that a walk around the stage can represent a journey and a curtain held by two attendants can mark an entrance. The relation between the conventional (nāṭyadharmī) and the naturalistic (lokadharmī) differs between forms. Kathakaḷi is at the conventional extreme, with silent actors who render every word of the sung text in gesture; Yakṣagāna allows its actors improvised spoken dialogue; Kuchipudi dance-dramas combine song, dance and speech.

    13.8 The Recital¶

    The modern solo recital, which presents pure and expressive dance in a fixed sequence of items, is itself a product of history. The order of the Bharatanāṭyam recital is traditionally credited to the Thanjavur Quartet in the early nineteenth century, though its present form was fixed in the concerts of the 1930s and after (Chapter 15); the Odissi recital was designed by the gurus who reconstructed the form in the 1950s; and the Kathak recital took its present shape in twentieth-century concerts. They share a common logic: the recital opens with an invocation and a display of pure dance, reaches its centre in an item combining pure and expressive dance, develops expression in a series of songs, and ends with a brilliant passage of pure dance and a benediction (Table 13.2).

    Table 13.2. The order of items in three modern solo recitals (simplified)
    Stage Bharatanāṭyam Odissi Kathak
    Invocation puṣpāñjali or kauttuvam maṅgalācaraṇa vandanā
    Pure dance alārippu, jatisvaram baṭu (sthāyī) thāṭ, āmad, toḍās and ṭukṛās
    Pure and expressive śabdam, varṇam pallavī gat nikās, gat bhāv
    Expressive padam, jāvaḷi, śloka abhinaya (e.g. an aṣṭapadī) ṭhumrī, bhajan
    Climax and close tillāna, maṅgaḷam mokṣa tatkār and fast compositions

    The recital format suited the concert hall, the proscenium stage and the urban audiences of the twentieth century. It also allowed dances that had been performed in very different contexts — temple rituals, court entertainments, processions, private salons — to be presented as unified “classical” arts with an internal logic, a feature that their founders often explained in spiritual terms, as a journey from the body to the soul. Such explanations express the meaning the forms have for many dancers today, but they are modern; the earlier contexts were far more varied and often far from spiritual (O’Shea 2007; Chakravorty 2008).

    13.9 Tāṇḍava, Lāsya and Gender¶

    Writers on Indian dance often describe its forms and movements in terms of the pairing of tāṇḍava, the vigorous dance of Śiva, with lāsya, the graceful dance of Pārvatī, and associate the first with men and the second with women. The pairing has textual roots (Chapter 2), and it captures a real contrast between vigorous and delicate qualities of movement. But as a description of the history of dance it is misleading. For most of recorded history, the professional dancers of the courts and temples were women, and their dances included vigorous pure dance as well as expressive items; the men of the same communities were teachers, musicians and conductors, while the all-male dance-drama traditions (Kathakaḷi, Kuchipudi, Yakṣagāna, Bhāgavata Mēḷa) cast men in women’s roles. In the twentieth century the meanings shifted again: the reformed classical forms were largely taken over by middle-class women performers, while male dancers had to justify their presence in what was now seen as a feminine art. The “natural” association of women with grace and men with vigour is thus a modern ideology as much as an ancient doctrine (O’Shea 2007; Chakravorty 2008).

    The most radical modern critique of these categories came from the choreographer Chandralekha (1928–2006), who trained in Bharatanāṭyam and then, from the 1980s, created works that stripped the form of its devotional narratives and its decorative femininity to reveal the strength of its geometry, drawing also on yoga and Kerala’s martial art. Rustom Bharucha’s study of her work describes it as a sustained argument about the female body, sexuality and the nation (Bharucha 1995). Contemporary choreographers continue to work with and against the threefold classification (Chapter 23).

    Further Reading¶

    Vatsyayan (1968) and Vatsyayan (1974) give the classic account of nṛtta, nṛtya and nāṭya in the dance forms, and Bose (1991) traces the categories in the treatises. O’Shea (2007) and Chakravorty (2008) examine the modern recital and its ideologies in Bharatanāṭyam and Kathak; Knight (2010) describes expressive dance in Balasaraswati’s art; and Bharucha (1995) discusses Chandralekha’s critique.
