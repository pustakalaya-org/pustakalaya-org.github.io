    Preface¶

    This is Volume 9 of the series Bhāratīya Jñāna Paramparā: Saṅgīta–Nāṭya: Music, Theatre and Dance. Its subject is the performing arts of the Indian subcontinent — what the Sanskrit tradition called saṅgīta (song, instrumental music and dance taken together) and nāṭya (drama) — from the earliest theoretical text, the Nāṭyaśāstra, to the concert hall, the village square, the film studio and the diaspora stage of today.

    The first three chapters introduce the field, the Nāṭyaśāstra and the theory of rasa and acting as it applies to performance. Chapters 4 to 11 deal with music: the theory of svara and rāga, the theory of tāla, the history of the treatises on music from the Dattilam to Bhatkhande, the Hindustani and Karnatak traditions (including the Persianate and Sufi music of north India), devotional music across regions and religions, the instruments, and the modern song of the regional languages, from Rabīndrasaṅgīt and Nazrulgīti to the Marathi stage song and the Assamese and Kannada lyric. Chapters 12 to 18 deal with dance: the later dance treatises, the grammar of pure and expressive dance, the language of gesture, and the forms now called “classical” — Bharatanāṭyam, Kathak, Odissi, Kuchipudi, Kathakaḷi, Mohiniyāṭṭam, Manipuri and Sattriya. The last five chapters turn to theatre (Sanskrit drama in performance, Kūṭiyāṭṭam and modern theatre; the regional popular theatres), to folk and tribal performance, to the music and dance of the North-East and the Himalaya, and to the institutions, media and debates of the modern period.

    The book quotes primary texts only where the wording and the reference can be given with confidence, and it does not present claims that ancient texts anticipated modern science, or that particular rāgas, gestures or dances cure disease, as established facts. Dates are given as the range accepted in current scholarship, with traditional dates labelled as such; chapter numbers of the Nāṭyaśāstra follow Manomohan Ghosh’s edition and translation. Where the history of an art is contested — the fate of the devadāsīs and courtesans whose arts were “revived” in the twentieth century, the role of caste and hereditary communities, the origin myths of instruments and forms — the book tries to state the evidence and the main scholarly positions plainly. Rasa theory, which belongs equally to poetics, is treated more fully in Volume 8, Kāvya Kalpalatā.

    The performing arts are learnt from teachers, not from books, and no description can substitute for listening and watching. The select bibliography is a guide to the standard studies, editions and translations, and the glossary explains the principal technical terms. Every quotation should, before scholarly use, be checked against a critical edition; the standard editions of the main treatises — the Baroda text of the Nāṭyaśāstra with Abhinavagupta’s commentary and Ghosh’s edition and translation, the Adyar text of the Saṅgītaratnākara and its translation by Shringy and Sharma, the editions of the Dattilam, the Bṛhaddeśī, the Abhinayadarpaṇa and the Nṛttaratnāvalī — are listed in the bibliography and named where they are first discussed.

    Nikhil Kumar Rajput
    Ramanujan College, University of Delhi
