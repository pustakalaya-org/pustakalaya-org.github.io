    Chapter 3: Rasa, Bhāva and Abhinaya in Performance¶

    The theory of rasa, “taste” or aesthetic relish, is the best-known contribution of the Nāṭyaśāstra to Indian thought. It was formulated for the theatre, and it was in the theatre that its terms — the determinants, consequents and transitory states of emotion, the stable emotions and their rasas — had their first practical meaning: they told the actor what to show and the spectator what to look for. From the ninth century onwards the theory was taken up by literary critics and philosophers and became the centre of a long debate about the nature of aesthetic experience; that debate, and the application of rasa to poetry, are treated in Volume 8 of this series, Kāvya Kalpalatā. This chapter is concerned with rasa in performance: the analysis of emotion that the actor and dancer use, the four means of acting (abhinaya) by which emotion is made visible and audible, the conventions of character and situation that give expressive dance much of its repertoire, and the spectator for whom all this is done.

    Nāṭyaśāstra 6, prose after v. 31, before the rasa-sūtra

    na hi rasād ṛte kaścid arthaḥ pravartate

    “No meaning proceeds without rasa.”

    3.1 The Rasa-sūtra and the Analysis of Emotion¶

    The sixth chapter of the Nāṭyaśāstra defines rasa in a single prose sentence:

    Nāṭyaśāstra 6, prose after v. 31 (placement varies between editions)

    vibhāvānubhāvavyabhicārisaṃyogād rasaniṣpattiḥ

    “Rasa arises from the union of the determinants, the consequents and the transitory states.”

    Bharata explains the sentence with the analogy of cooking: as a dish acquires its taste from the combination of many ingredients and spices, so the stable emotions, combined with the other states, become rasa, which the spectator “tastes”. For the performer each term names something concrete (Ghosh 1950; Masson and Patwardhan 1970):

    Vibhāva (determinant).
    The causes of an emotion: its object or support (ālambana), for example the beloved, and the circumstances that excite it (uddīpana), such as moonlight, a garden in spring or the sound of the flute. In performance the determinants are supplied by the story and the poetry, and are often themselves shown in mime: the dancer depicts the moon, the bees, the river.
    Anubhāva (consequent).
    The outward signs of emotion: glances, movements of the brows and lips, gestures, gait, changes of voice. These are what the actor actually does.
    Vyabhicāribhāva (transitory state).
    Also called sañcāribhāva: passing feelings that arise and subside within a dominant mood, such as anxiety, joy, shame, recollection or weariness. Bharata lists thirty-three (Table 3.2).
    Sthāyibhāva (stable emotion).
    The enduring emotion that the other elements serve and that becomes rasa for the spectator. There are eight, one for each rasa.

    The Nāṭyaśāstra adds eight sāttvikabhāvas, involuntary signs of strong feeling: paralysis (stambha), perspiration (sveda), horripilation (romāñca), change of voice (svarabheda), trembling (vepathu), change of colour (vaivarṇya), tears (aśru) and fainting (pralaya). Because they cannot be produced at will, the text says that the actor must achieve them through concentration of mind (sattva); this is the origin of the idea, central to later teaching, that expressive acting requires inner absorption as well as technique. Altogether the text counts forty-nine bhāvas: eight stable, thirty-three transitory and eight involuntary.

    3.2 The Rasas¶

    Bharata names eight rasas and gives each a stable emotion, a colour and a presiding deity (Table 3.1).

    Nāṭyaśāstra 6.15 (Ghosh’s numbering; 6.16 in some editions)

    śṛṅgārahāsyakaruṇā raudravīrabhayānakāḥ
    bībhatsādbhutasaṃjñau cety aṣṭau nāṭye rasāḥ smṛtāḥ

    “The erotic, the comic, the pathetic, the furious, the heroic, the terrible, and those called the odious and the marvellous: these eight are declared to be the rasas in drama.”

    Table 3.1. The eight rasas of the Nāṭyaśāstra (ch. 6), with the later ninth
    Rasa Stable emotion English Colour Deity
    Śṛṅgāra rati (love) Erotic dark (śyāma) Viṣṇu
    Hāsya hāsa (mirth) Comic white the Pramathas
    Karuṇa śoka (grief) Pathetic dove-grey Yama
    Raudra krodha (anger) Furious red Rudra
    Vīra utsāha (energy) Heroic pale gold (gaura) Mahendra (Indra)
    Bhayānaka bhaya (fear) Terrible black Kāla
    Bībhatsa jugupsā (disgust) Odious blue Mahākāla
    Adbhuta vismaya (astonishment) Marvellous yellow Brahmā
    Śānta śama (calm) or nirveda Tranquil not in Bharata’s list; see below

    Bharata’s descriptions are practical. Love has two forms, love in union (sambhoga) and love in separation (vipralambha); the comic is graded into six kinds of laughter, from the faint smile (smita) and the smile that shows a little of the teeth (hasita), proper to superior characters, through vihasita and upahasita, to the coarse apahasita and atihasita, proper to low characters; the heroic arises in giving, in righteous action and in war. For each rasa the text lists its determinants, the consequents by which the actor should show it, and the transitory states that accompany it. The four rasas of love, fury, heroism and disgust are called the sources of the others: the comic arises from love, the pathetic from fury, the marvellous from heroism and the terrible from disgust.

    3.2.1 Śānta and later rasas¶

    Whether there is a ninth rasa, the tranquil (śānta), was debated for centuries. It is not in the core list of the Nāṭyaśāstra, though some manuscripts contain a passage on it that is probably a later insertion. It is first clearly listed by the Kashmiri critic Udbhaṭa (late eighth century) and was defended at length by Abhinavagupta (c. 950–1016 CE), who argued that its stable emotion is the knowledge of truth and that it is the rasa corresponding to the goal of liberation; after him “the nine rasas” (navarasa) became the usual phrase (Raghavan 1940; Masson and Patwardhan 1970). Later theorists proposed further rasas — of parental affection (vātsalya), of devotion (bhakti) and others. The most influential of these proposals was made in the sixteenth century by Rūpa Gosvāmin, a theologian of the Gauḍīya Vaiṣṇava movement, who made devotion to Kṛṣṇa the one true rasa and recast the classical categories as kinds of devotional love; his theory shaped the devotional performing arts of Bengal, Odisha and Manipur (Pollock 2016).

    In modern dance teaching the “nine rasas” are often taught as nine facial expressions, each with a fixed set of movements of the eyes, brows and mouth. This is a useful pedagogical device, but it simplifies the theory: for Bharata, rasa is not an expression on the actor’s face but an experience produced in the spectator by the whole performance.

    3.3 The Transitory States¶

    The thirty-three transitory states are the expressive actor’s most detailed resource. Each is defined in the Nāṭyaśāstra by its causes and by the consequents through which it should be shown. Several are not emotions in the modern sense but physical or mental conditions — sleep, sickness, intoxication, even death — that the actor must be able to represent.

    Table 3.2. The thirty-three transitory states (vyabhicāribhāva) of the Nāṭyaśāstra (ch. 7), in Bharata’s order
    State Meaning State Meaning
    nirveda despondency, world-weariness viṣāda despair
    glāni weakness, languor autsukya impatience, longing
    śaṅkā apprehension nidrā sleepiness
    asūyā envy, jealousy apasmāra epilepsy, loss of control
    mada intoxication supta sleep, dreaming
    śrama weariness vibodha awakening
    ālasya indolence amarṣa indignation
    dainya wretchedness avahitthā dissimulation
    cintā anxiety ugratā ferocity
    moha confusion, distraction mati understanding, resolve
    smṛti recollection vyādhi sickness
    dhṛti contentment, steadiness unmāda madness
    vrīḍā shame, bashfulness maraṇa death
    capalatā inconstancy trāsa fright
    harṣa joy vitarka deliberation, doubt
    āvega agitation
    jaḍatā stupor
    garva arrogance

    In performance the transitory states give a dominant mood its colour and movement. A dancer interpreting a song of love in separation may pass, within a single line, through longing (autsukya), recollection of a past meeting (smṛti), anxiety (cintā), a flash of joy as she imagines the lover’s return (harṣa) and despair (viṣāda) when she realises he has not come, all held within the stable emotion of love. The practice of elaborating a single line of text through a succession of such states, each shown with a different set of gestures and glances while the musicians repeat the line, is the core of expressive improvisation (manodharma) in Bharatanāṭyam and of the long elaborations of Kūṭiyāṭṭam and Kathakaḷi (Chapters 15, 18 and 19).

    3.4 The Four Means of Acting¶

    The word abhinaya is derived from the root nī, “to lead”, with the prefix abhi, “towards”: it is what “leads” the meaning of the play to the spectators. The Nāṭyaśāstra distinguishes four kinds (Ghosh 1950; Vatsyayan 1996).

    3.4.1 Āṅgika: acting with the body¶

    Bodily acting uses the major limbs, the face and the subsidiary limbs, each with its named movements (Chapter 2): the movements of the head, eyes, brows and lips; the hand gestures (Chapter 14); the positions and movements of the chest, waist and legs; the cārīs and gaits. The text distinguishes three applications: the representation of the body itself, of the face, and of movement through the whole body. Bodily acting is the domain that dance shares most fully with drama, and the later dance manuals (Chapter 12) are largely manuals of āṅgika.

    3.4.2 Vācika: acting with the voice¶

    Verbal acting includes everything that is spoken or sung: the text itself, its metres and figures of speech, the languages of the stage (Sanskrit for some characters, Prakrit dialects for others), the modes of address, and the delivery of speech with appropriate pitch, tempo and intonation (kāku). In the dance forms the voice is usually the singer’s: the dancer interprets a song sung by the vocalist, and the words of the song — in Sanskrit, Tamil, Telugu, Kannada, Odia, Hindi, Braj or Manipuri — determine what the body shows. In the dance-dramas the actors may speak (as in Kūṭiyāṭṭam and Yakṣagāna) or remain silent while the singers sing their words (as in Kathakaḷi).

    3.4.3 Āhārya: costume, make-up and properties¶

    The “extraneous” means of acting comprise costume, ornaments, make-up and stage properties (Chapter 2). In the modern forms they range from the restrained costume and jewellery of the solo dance forms, whose conventions were largely fixed in the twentieth century, to the elaborate make-up, head-dresses and skirts of Kathakaḷi, Yakṣagāna and Kūṭiyāṭṭam, which transform the actor into a type before a word is sung.

    3.4.4 Sāttvika: acting with inner feeling¶

    The fourth means is the representation of the involuntary states listed above, and more generally the quality of inner concentration (sattva) that makes acting convincing. Bharata ranks performances in which sattva predominates as the best. The tradition has always debated how far the actor should actually feel what he represents; Abhinavagupta’s view, that the actor too experiences the generalised emotion without being overwhelmed by it as by a personal feeling, is one influential answer.

    3.4.5 General and varied acting¶

    Two later chapters of the Nāṭyaśāstra deal with the combination of the four means. “General acting” (sāmānyābhinaya, ch. 24) discusses the harmonious use of all of them and describes the types of men and women, their temperaments and the stages of love; “varied acting” (citrābhinaya, ch. 26) shows how to represent things that cannot be brought on the stage: the seasons, the times of day, the sun and moon, birds and animals, distant and invisible objects. Much of the mimetic vocabulary of the modern dance forms — the gestures for the lotus, the bee, the peacock, the moon — is of this kind.

    3.5 Characters and Situations¶

    Expressive dance in India has inherited from the theatre and from poetry a repertoire of typical characters and situations, above all those of love.

    3.5.1 The heroine and the hero¶

    The Nāṭyaśāstra (ch. 24) describes eight heroines (nāyikā) according to their situation in love: the heroine who has dressed and prepared the house for her lover (vāsakasajjā); the heroine pining in separation (virahotkaṇṭhitā); the one whose lover is under her control (svādhīnabhartṛkā); the one separated by a quarrel she now regrets (kalahāntaritā); the one whose lover comes to her bearing the marks of another woman (khaṇḍitā); the one deceived by a lover who fails to keep a tryst (vipralabdhā); the one whose husband is away on a journey (proṣitabhartṛkā); and the one who goes out to meet her lover (abhisārikā). Later rhetoricians multiplied these classifications, and the eight became a favourite subject for poets, painters and musicians; the aṣṭanāyikā remain a staple of the dance repertoire. The hero (nāyaka) is classified as noble and exalted (dhīrodātta), light-hearted (dhīralalita), calm (dhīrapraśānta) or haughty (dhīroddhata), and in love poetry as faithful, impartial, deceitful or shameless; the confidante (sakhī), to whom the heroine speaks, is often the implied listener of the dancer’s song.

    3.5.2 Love, devotion and the problem of the erotic¶

    Much of the expressive repertoire of the classical dance forms consists of love songs. In the padams of Kṣetrayya (seventeenth century) and the jāvaḷis of the nineteenth century, the heroine addresses a god — Kṛṣṇa as Muvva Gopāla, or Murukaṉ — or a king or patron in the language of human love, often frankly sexual. Jayadeva’s Gītagovinda (twelfth century), the basis of much Odissi repertoire, is at once an erotic poem and a devotional one. For the devotional traditions this is no contradiction: from the Tamil Āḻvār and Nāyaṉār poets to the Gauḍīya Vaiṣṇavas, human love was the most intense available language for the love of god, and the devotee could be cast as a woman longing for an absent lover.

    In the twentieth century this repertoire became a battleground. Reformers who campaigned against the dedication of devadāsīs regarded the erotic songs of their repertoire as evidence of immorality; some of those who reconstructed the dance for the concert stage removed or “spiritualised” them, while others, notably Balasaraswati, insisted that the śṛṅgāra of the hereditary repertoire was itself devotional and should be performed as it had been taught. The history of this debate, and its consequences for the women whose art was being reshaped, is told in Chapter 15 (Soneji 2012; Knight 2010).

    3.5.3 Improper rasa (rasābhāsa)¶

    Later theorists described as rasābhāsa, a “semblance of rasa”, the case in which an emotion is presented improperly, against the conventions of propriety (aucitya): love that is not reciprocated, or felt for an improper object, as Rāvaṇa’s for Sītā; heroism displayed against an unworthy opponent; emotions attributed to animals or to characters thought incapable of them. The concept thus concerned the content of what was represented and its fit with social and literary norms, not, as is sometimes said, the insincerity of a performer. Its social assumptions — about whose emotions counted — are themselves part of the history of the theory.

    3.6 The Spectator¶

    Rasa is experienced by the spectator, and the theory demands a particular kind of spectator: the sahṛdaya, “one whose heart is in tune”, whose sensibility has been formed by familiarity with poetry and performance and who can respond to a representation without either indifference or merely personal involvement. The Nāṭyaśāstra itself describes the qualities of the ideal spectator and the signs (laughter, tears, applause, horripilation) by which success (siddhi) in performance can be judged (ch. 27; see Chapter 2).

    How exactly rasa arises in the spectator was the subject of the debate between the commentators Bhaṭṭa Lollaṭa, Śrī Śaṅkuka, Bhaṭṭa Nāyaka and Abhinavagupta, known mainly through Abhinavagupta’s report. In the view that prevailed, Abhinavagupta’s, the play does not produce the emotion of a character in the spectator, nor does the spectator merely infer it; rather, the situation presented on the stage is “generalised” (sādhāraṇīkaraṇa), freed from the particular persons involved, and awakens latent impressions (vāsanā) of the corresponding emotion in the spectator’s own mind, which he then “tastes” in a state of absorbed wonder (camatkāra). Abhinavagupta also analysed the obstacles that prevent this experience — an implausible presentation, the spectator’s preoccupation with his own pleasure or pain, inadequate means of presentation, doubt — and explained the function of music, dance and the conventions of the stage as means of removing them. The theory is treated in detail in Volume 8, Kāvya Kalpalatā; for the present purpose it is enough to note that it gave the performer a demanding task, to present emotion in a form that is at once vivid and impersonal (Gnoli 1968; Pollock 2016; Ingalls et al. 1990).

    The comparison of aesthetic experience with the bliss of the realisation of brahman is often attributed to Abhinavagupta. He does compare the two, while insisting on their differences; the famous phrase calling rasa “brother to the tasting of brahman” (brahmāsvāda-sahodara) is from a later and simpler work, Viśvanātha’s Sāhityadarpaṇa (fourteenth century).

    Note: Modern comparisons

    Writers on rasa have compared the sahṛdaya with the “implied reader” of modern literary theory, sādhāraṇīkaraṇa with “aesthetic distance”, and the communication of emotion in performance with research on empathy and “emotional contagion”. Such comparisons can illuminate both sides, but they are analogies between theories built for different purposes. The claim, sometimes made, that modern neuroscience has “confirmed” the rasa theory has no basis in published research.

    3.7 The Tamil Tradition: Meyppāṭu¶

    Sanskrit was not the only language in which Indian theorists analysed the expression of emotion. The Tamil grammar Tolkāppiyam, whose third book on subject-matter (Poruḷatikāram) is of uncertain date but probably belongs to the first half of the first millennium CE, contains a section on meyppāṭu, literally “that which appears in the body”: the outward manifestation of feeling. It lists eight meyppāṭu: laughter (nakai), weeping (aḻukai), contempt or humiliation (iḷivaral), wonder (maruṭkai), fear (accam), pride or valour (perumitam), anger (vekuḷi) and joy (uvakai), each with four causes. The eight correspond closely to the eight rasas of the Nāṭyaśāstra, and scholars have long debated whether the Tamil scheme was borrowed from the Sanskrit, the Sanskrit from a wider South Asian tradition that also produced the Tamil, or whether the two developed in contact. The Tamil scheme belongs to a theory of poetry rather than of the stage, and its own context — the classification of poetic situations into “interior” (akam, love) and “exterior” (puṟam, war, praise and public life) — is quite different from Bharata’s (Parthasarathy 1993).

    The Cilappatikāram, with its description of the dancer Mātavi and of performances in which emotion is shown through gesture and song, and the medieval commentaries on it, show that a Tamil vocabulary for the performing arts existed alongside the Sanskrit one. The Tamil Isai movement of the twentieth century, and some modern Bharatanāṭyam dancers, have drawn on this heritage to emphasise the Tamil roots of the dance. Tamil poetics is treated more fully in Volume 8, Kāvya Kalpalatā.

    3.8 Training the Face and the Eyes¶

    The theory of bhāva and rasa becomes performance only through years of training. In the Kerala theatres, where facial expression is most highly developed, actors practise daily exercises for the eyes, brows, cheeks and lips. Kathakaḷi students perform eye exercises (kaṇṇusādhakam) in which the eyes are moved in set patterns — side to side, up and down, in circles and figures of eight — while the head is held still, often in the early morning, so that the eyes acquire strength, range and control; they then learn to show each of the nine rasas by a fixed configuration of eyes, brows, cheeks and mouth, and to move from one to another without a break. Kūṭiyāṭṭam actors cultivate a similar discipline of the eyes (netrābhinaya); the great Cākyār actor Māṇi Mādhava Cākyār (1899–1990), whose book Nāṭyakalpadrumam describes the techniques of his tradition, was famed for the expressive power of his eyes (Zarrilli 2000; Richmond et al. 1990).

    In the solo dance forms, abhinaya is taught more through the learning of particular compositions than through separate exercises. A student of Bharatanāṭyam or Odissi first learns the gestures for the words of a song and the basic expressions for the feelings it names, then gradually learns from the teacher how to vary and deepen them in repetition, and only after years of study improvises freely. Teachers in all the forms insist that facial expression cannot be learnt as a set of masks: the dancer must imagine the situation, the person addressed, the time and place, so that the expression arises from the imagined situation rather than being placed on the face. This is a practical version of the old debate about whether the actor should feel what he represents (above): the trained performer creates the conditions for a feeling without being overwhelmed by it.

    3.9 Rasa and Its Audiences¶

    The classical theory assumed a particular audience: courtly, educated in Sanskrit poetry, familiar with the conventions of the stage and with the stories represented. The audiences of Indian performance have always been more varied. Temple festival audiences included people of every class, many of whom did not understand Sanskrit; the audiences of Kathakaḷi in Kerala villages watched through the night, following the story through its familiar episodes rather than the words of the songs; the audiences of the courtesans’ salons were connoisseurs of Urdu and Braj poetry and of music, but not necessarily of Sanskrit poetics. Other aesthetic vocabularies were in use alongside the Sanskrit: the Persian and Urdu language of connoisseurship (zauq, “taste”), the Sufi concept of the ecstatic state (ḥāl) produced by music in the assembly (Chapter 7), and the devotional vocabulary of the bhakti movements, for which the aim of performance was not aesthetic savouring but love of god (Qureshi 1986).

    In the twentieth century the classical forms acquired new audiences: the urban middle class, the national and international festival, and, through the diaspora and touring, spectators who did not share the languages or the religious culture of the performers. Dancers responded in different ways — by explaining the stories and gestures before performance, by choosing themes of wider appeal, by reducing the proportion of expressive dance in favour of rhythmic virtuosity, or by creating new work in which abhinaya is applied to modern subjects (Chapter 23). Whether rasa in Bharata’s sense can be experienced by a spectator without the cultural preparation the theory assumes is a question that performers and critics continue to discuss. The theory itself suggests a nuanced answer: the sahṛdaya is made, not born, and familiarity with a tradition is acquired by attending to it.

    3.10 Rasa in the Living Forms¶

    The modern performing arts use the vocabulary of rasa in different ways. Kūṭiyāṭṭam and Kathakaḷi retain the most elaborate techniques for showing the stable and transitory states (above), and in Kūṭiyāṭṭam the actor may spend an evening developing a single verse through the emotions it implies (Chapter 19). In Bharatanāṭyam, Kuchipudi, Odissi and Mohiniyāṭṭam, abhinaya is concentrated in the song-based items — the varṇam, padam, jāvaḷi and aṣṭapadī — and in improvised elaboration of a line. Kathak has its own expressive genre in the interpretation of ṭhumrī and bhajan, much of it improvised while seated or standing in place (bhāv batānā). In music, the association of rāgas with particular rasas is a later and looser tradition, discussed in Chapter 4.

    Across all these forms the theory of rasa gives performers and connoisseurs a common language for talking about what a performance does to its audience. It is a language with a history: formed in the theatre of the early centuries CE, refined by Kashmiri philosophers, transformed by devotional theologians and revived, in the twentieth century, as part of the reconstruction of a “classical” Indian heritage. Used with that history in mind, it remains one of the most subtle vocabularies for the analysis of performance available in any tradition.

    Further Reading¶

    Bharata’s chapters on rasa and bhāva are translated in Ghosh (1950), and the rasa chapter with Abhinavagupta’s commentary in Masson and Patwardhan (1970). Pollock (2016) is an anthology of the main texts of the debate, with an introduction; Gnoli (1968) translates and discusses Abhinavagupta’s account of aesthetic experience; Raghavan (1940) is the classic study of the number of rasas. De (1960) surveys the history of the theory in poetics. For rasa in dance practice see Vatsyayan (1968).
