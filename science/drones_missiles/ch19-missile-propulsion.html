    Chapter 19: Missile Propulsion¶

    19.1 Rocket Propulsion Fundamentals¶

    Missile propulsion encompasses the complete science of converting stored chemical (or other) energy into directed thrust to accelerate a vehicle along a prescribed trajectory. The physical principles governing all rocket systems derive from Newton’s second and third laws combined with thermodynamic analyses of high-temperature, high-pressure combustion processes.

    19.1.1 The Thrust Equation¶

    The thrust produced by a rocket nozzle arises from two contributions: the momentum flux of exhaust gases leaving the nozzle exit plane, and the pressure imbalance between the nozzle exit and the ambient atmosphere acting on the exit area. The general thrust equation is

    \[F = \dot{m}\,V_e + (P_e - P_a)\,A_e\tag{19.1}\]

    where \(\dot{m}\) is the propellant mass flow rate (k g s), \(V_e\) the effective exhaust velocity at the nozzle exit plane (m s), \(P_e\) the static pressure at the nozzle exit (P a), \(P_a\) the ambient (freestream) pressure (P a), and \(A_e\) the nozzle exit area (m 2).

    The first term, \(\dot{m}V_e\), is the momentum thrust; the second term, \((P_e-P_a)A_e\), is the pressure thrust. At the design altitude of a nozzle (\(P_e = P_a\)), pressure thrust vanishes and the nozzle is said to be perfectly expanded. Over-expanded flow (\(P_e < P_a\)) produces oblique shocks at the nozzle lip; under-expanded flow (\(P_e > P_a\)) produces expansion fans.

    The effective exhaust velocity \(c\) or \(V_{eff}\) combines both terms:

    \[c = V_e + \frac{(P_e - P_a)\,A_e}{\dot{m}}\tag{19.2}\]

    so that \(F = \dot{m}\,c\).

    19.1.2 Specific Impulse¶

    Specific impulse \(I_{sp}\) is the single most important figure of merit for any rocket propellant combination or engine cycle. It is defined as the thrust produced per unit weight flow of propellant:

    \[I_{sp} = \frac{F}{\dot{m}\,g_0}\tag{19.3}\]

    where \(g_0 = 9.80665\,\text{m s 2}\) is the standard sea-level gravitational acceleration. This definition makes \(I_{sp}\) dimensionally a time (seconds) and independent of the unit system. Equivalently,

    \[I_{sp} = \frac{c}{g_0}\tag{19.4}\]

    High \(I_{sp}\) means more thrust per kilogram of propellant consumed. Typical values range from \(\sim\)230 s for early double-base propellants, to \(\sim\)270 s for composite HTPB/AP/Al propellants, \(\sim\)340 s for storable liquid bipropellants, and up to \(\sim\)460 s vacuum for cryogenic LOX/LH\(_2\).

    19.1.3 The Tsiolkovsky Rocket Equation¶

    The ideal velocity increment \(\Delta V\) achievable by a rocket burning from initial mass \(m_0\) to burnout mass \(m_f\) in a gravity-free, drag-free environment is

    \[\Delta V = I_{sp}\,g_0\,\ln\!\left(\frac{m_0}{m_f}\right) = c\,\ln\!\left(\frac{m_0}{m_f}\right)\tag{19.5}\]

    The mass ratio \(R = m_0/m_f\) is the key structural efficiency parameter. For a single-stage ballistic missile with \(\Delta V = 7\,\text{k m s}\) and \(I_{sp} = 290\,\text{s}\), the required mass ratio is:

    \[R = \exp\!\left(\frac{\Delta V}{I_{sp}\,g_0}\right) = \exp\!\left(\frac{7000}{290 \times 9.807}\right) \approx 11.5\]

    meaning the propellant mass must be \(\approx 91\%\) of launch mass — a severe constraint driving structural mass minimisation (thin CFRP casings, lightweight warhead structures).

    19.1.4 Characteristic Velocity and Thrust Coefficient¶

    The characteristic velocity \(c^*\) characterises the thermochemical efficiency of the propellant and the combustion chamber independently of nozzle geometry:

    \[c^* = \frac{P_c\,A_t}{\dot{m}}\tag{19.7}\]

    where \(P_c\) is the chamber stagnation pressure and \(A_t\) is the nozzle throat area. Theoretically,

    \[c^*_{th} = \frac{\sqrt{\gamma R_u T_c / M_w}}{\gamma \left(\frac{2}{\gamma+1}\right)^{(\gamma+1)/[2(\gamma-1)]}}\tag{19.8}\]

    where \(T_c\) is the adiabatic flame temperature, \(M_w\) the mean molecular weight of products, \(\gamma\) the effective ratio of specific heats, and \(R_u\) the universal gas constant. The combustion efficiency \(\eta_{c^*} = c^*_{measured}/c^*_{th}\) is typically 0.94–0.98 for well-designed motors.

    The thrust coefficient \(C_F\) relates thrust to chamber pressure and throat area:

    \[C_F = \frac{F}{P_c\,A_t}\tag{19.9}\]

    Combining with Eq. (A.100): \(F = C_F\,c^*\,\dot{m}\) and \(I_{sp} = C_F\,c^*/g_0\). The theoretical thrust coefficient for isentropic nozzle flow is:

    \[C_{F,th} = \sqrt{\frac{2\gamma^2}{\gamma-1}\left(\frac{2}{\gamma+1}\right)^{(\gamma+1)/(\gamma-1)} \left[1 - \left(\frac{P_e}{P_c}\right)^{(\gamma-1)/\gamma}\right]} + \frac{(P_e-P_a)\,A_e}{P_c\,A_t}\tag{19.10}\]

    At vacuum (\(P_a = 0\)) and optimal expansion, \(C_F\) reaches its maximum. Typical values: \(C_F = 1.5\)–\(1.7\) at altitude for composite propellants.

    19.1.5 Nozzle Theory¶

    Area–Mach Relation¶

    The isentropic area–Mach relation governs the flow area at any cross-section:

    \[\frac{A}{A_t} = \frac{1}{M}\left[\frac{2}{\gamma+1} \left(1+\frac{\gamma-1}{2}M^2\right)\right]^{(\gamma+1)/[2(\gamma-1)]}\tag{19.11}\]

    At the throat \(M=1\) and \(A = A_t\). The expansion ratio \(\varepsilon = A_e/A_t\) uniquely determines the exit Mach number \(M_e\) (supersonic solution). For \(\gamma = 1.3\) and \(\varepsilon = 8\): \(M_e \approx 3.4\).

    Nozzle Contour: Bell Nozzle¶

    A conical nozzle of half-angle \(\alpha = 15°\) has a divergence efficiency of \(\lambda = (1+\cos\alpha)/2 = 0.983\). To reduce nozzle length while maintaining high efficiency, bell nozzles are used. The Rao optimum bell nozzle achieves near-ideal flow with a contour that:

    1. Expands rapidly (high expansion angle, \(\sim\)25–45°) near the throat

    2. Transitions smoothly to a low exit angle (\(\sim\)8–12°) at the exit plane

    3. Uses a parabolic approximation for the contour

    An 80% bell nozzle has a length equal to 80% of the equivalent conical nozzle with the same expansion ratio. Despite its shorter length, the 80% bell achieves \(>99\%\) of the conical nozzle’s ideal thrust, making it the standard choice for most modern missile motors.

    Combustion Temperature and Dissociation¶

    The adiabatic flame temperature \(T_{ad}\) for HTPB/AP/Al at \(O/F = 2.1\), \(P_c = 7\,\text{M P a}\) is approximately 3,500 K. However, at these temperatures significant dissociation occurs, absorbing energy. NASA CEA (Chemical Equilibrium with Applications) equilibrium calculations account for 40+ species including HCl, CO, CO\(_2\), H\(_2\)O, H\(_2\), OH, O, H, Al\(_2\)O\(_3\). The effective \(\gamma\) ranges from 1.16–1.24 for composite propellants (lower than 1.4 for air because of the complex product mixture).

    19.2 Solid Rocket Motors¶

    Solid rocket motors (SRMs) dominate tactical and strategic missile propulsion due to their simplicity, storage stability, high reliability, and high thrust-to-weight ratio. They consist of a propellant grain cast or pressed into a cylindrical casing, ignited at launch, and burned through to completion. There are no valves, pumps, or turbines.

    19.2.1 Propellant Formulations¶

    HTPB/AP/Aluminium (Composite Propellant)¶

    The most widely used tactical missile propellant is the HTPB/AP/Al system:

    • Hydroxyl-Terminated Polybutadiene (HTPB) binder: 12–16% by mass; provides structural integrity and acts as a supplementary fuel; cross-linked with isocyanate (MDI or IPDI) to form an elastomeric matrix.

    • Ammonium Perchlorate (AP), (NH\(_4\)ClO\(_4\)): 68–72% by mass; primary oxidiser; bimodal or trimodal particle size distribution (200 \(\mu\)m coarse + 20 \(\mu\)m fine) to maximise packing density.

    • Aluminium powder: 2–20% by mass; metallic fuel; increases \(T_{ad}\) by 300–500 K; increases \(I_{sp}\) by 15–25 s; produces Al\(_2\)O\(_3\) slag.

    • Additives: iron oxide (Fe\(_2\)O\(_3\)) burn rate catalyst 0.1–1%; bonding agents (HX-752, MAPO); stabilisers; plasticisers (DOA/TEGDN).

    Performance: \(I_{sp} = 250\)–\(270\) s (sea level), \(\rho = 1750\)–\(1870\) kg/m\(^3\), \(T_c \approx 3500\) K, \(r \approx 8\)–\(15\) mm/s at 7 MPa, \(n \approx 0.3\)–\(0.4\).

    NEPE (Nitrate Ester Plasticised Polyether)¶

    NEPE propellants use an energetic binder (polyethylene glycol or polyethylene-polyol plasticised with nitrate esters such as TMETN or BTTN) combined with HMX or RDX oxidiser/fuel:

    • Binder: polyether backbone with nitrate ester plasticiser (40% of binder)

    • HMX (cyclotetramethylenetetranitramine): 20–30% by mass

    • AP: 30–40%; Al: 15–20%

    • \(I_{sp} = 275\)–\(285\) s; density \(\rho = 1820\)–\(1870\) kg/m\(^3\)

    • Burn rate: 7–12 mm/s at 7 MPa; \(n = 0.35\)–\(0.45\)

    Used in the US Trident II D5 SLBM (first and second stages), achieving \(\Delta V\) capability for 7,400+ km range with MIRV payload. The higher energy density compared to HTPB/AP/Al allows significantly increased mass ratio or reduced motor size.

    ADN-Based Propellants¶

    Ammonium Dinitramide (ADN), NH\(_4\)N(NO\(_2\))\(_2\), is an alternative oxidiser offering:

    • Chlorine-free combustion: eliminates HCl in exhaust — reduces radar cross-section (RCS) of plume (HCl is strongly radar-absorbent at certain wavelengths)

    • \(I_{sp} \approx 255\)–\(265\) s (slightly below AP)

    • Higher solubility in binder systems

    • Lower sensitivity: more insensitive to shock and friction than AP

    • Hygroscopic: requires careful moisture control in processing

    ADN-based propellants are investigated for stealth missile applications where plume observability is critical (e.g., low-RCS cruise missiles, covert tactical rounds).

    HTPE Propellants¶

    Hydroxyl-Terminated Polyether (HTPE) replaces HTPB as the binder:

    • Improved low-temperature flexibility (\(T_g < -65°C\))

    • Lower sensitivity to shock and friction (important for Insensitive Munitions (IM) requirements MIL-STD-2105D)

    • Slower burn rate (must be compensated with catalyst)

    • Used in upgraded AIM-9X Sidewinder and AMRAAM motors

    19.2.2 Propellant Grain Geometry¶

    The geometry of the propellant grain determines the burn area \(A_b(t)\) as a function of time, which in turn controls the thrust profile. Six fundamental grain geometries are used:

    Table 19.2. Propellant Grain Geometries and Thrust Profiles
    Geometry Burn Profile \(A_b/A_{b0}\) Trend Typical Application
    End-burning (cigarette) Neutral/regressive Decreasing Long-burn sustain motors
    Internal cylinder (BATES) Neutral/progressive Constant then inc. Short-range AAM
    Star (5–7 point) Neutral \(\approx\) constant SAM, ICBM boost
    Finocyl (finned cylinder) Neutral/regressive Slight decrease ICBM 2nd stage, AAM
    Wagon-wheel Progressive Increasing High boost, short burn
    Slotted tube Progressive Increasing Quick boost applications

    The star grain is the most common for tactical missiles. With \(N\) points of internal star geometry, the burn area remains approximately constant (neutral burn) throughout most of the web, providing near-constant thrust. A 5-point star is typical for medium-calibre SAMs. A finocyl (finned cylindrical) grain achieves very neutral burning with higher volumetric loading than a star, at the cost of more complex tooling.

    19.2.3 Burn Rate and Internal Ballistics¶

    Vieille’s Law¶

    The propellant burn rate \(r\) (surface regression rate, mm/s) is governed by Vieille’s empirical law:

    \[r = a\,P_c^n\tag{19.12}\]

    where \(a\) is a pre-exponential coefficient (m m ( s M P a n )), \(P_c\) the chamber pressure in MPa, and \(n\) the pressure exponent (0.2–0.6 for stable burning). Values of \(n > 0.85\) indicate combustion instability risk; \(n = 1\) produces unstable runaway.

    Table 19.3. Typical Burn Rate Parameters for Composite Propellants
    Propellant \(a\) at 7 MPa \(n\) \(r\) at 7 MPa \(T_{ad}\) (K) \(\sigma_p\) (%/K)
    HTPB/AP/Al (18% Al) 2.1 0.33 10.2 3,500 0.10
    HTPB/AP/Al (2% Al) 2.5 0.30 10.8 3,100 0.12
    NEPE/HMX/Al 1.8 0.40 9.5 3,700 0.09
    HTPB/AP+Fe\(_2\)O\(_3\) 3.8 0.35 17.0 3,450 0.11
    Double-base (NC/NG) 1.2 0.28 7.5 2,800 0.18
    HTPE/AP/Al 1.9 0.32 9.8 3,480 0.10

    The temperature sensitivity coefficient \(\sigma_p\) describes the fractional change in burn rate per degree Kelvin change in initial propellant temperature at constant chamber pressure:

    \[\sigma_p = \frac{1}{r}\left(\frac{\partial r}{\partial T_i}\right)_{P_c}\tag{19.13}\]

    For \(\sigma_p = 0.10\%/\mathrm{K}\) and a temperature swing from \(-40°C\) to \(+70°C\) (\(\Delta T = 110~K\)), burn rate changes by \(\pm5.5\%\), requiring pressure vessel and nozzle throat sizing to accommodate maximum design pressure.

    Mass Flow and Chamber Pressure¶

    The mass flow rate generated by combustion is:

    \[\dot{m} = \rho_p\,r\,A_b = \rho_p\,a\,P_c^n\,A_b\tag{19.14}\]

    At quasi-steady state, this mass flow must equal the choked nozzle flow:

    \[\dot{m} = \frac{P_c\,A_t}{c^*}\tag{19.15}\]

    Equating Eqs. (19.14) and (19.15) and solving for the steady-state chamber pressure:

    \[P_c = \left(\frac{\rho_p\,c^*\,a\,A_b}{A_t}\right)^{1/(1-n)}\tag{19.16}\]

    This equation shows that \(P_c \propto (A_b/A_t)^{1/(1-n)}\). For \(n=0.35\) and a 10% increase in burn area (e.g., from propellant cracking), chamber pressure increases by \(\approx 15\%\) — highlighting the importance of grain integrity inspection.

    19.2.4 Combustion Instability¶

    Combustion instability is the coupling between the acoustic modes of the combustion chamber and the heat release rate. Two principal categories affect SRMs:

    L* Instability (Low-Frequency)¶

    \[L^* = \frac{V_c}{A_t}\tag{19.17}\]

    where \(V_c\) is the combustion chamber free volume. For \(L^* < L^*_{min} \approx 0.5\) m (propellant-specific), low-frequency pressure oscillations (chugging, 10–200 Hz) occur. Solution: increase \(L^*\) by enlarging chamber volume.

    Acoustic Mode Coupling (High-Frequency)¶

    Axial longitudinal acoustic modes at frequency:

    \[f_n = \frac{n\,c_{gas}}{2L}\tag{19.18}\]

    where \(c_{gas} = \sqrt{\gamma R T}\) is the speed of sound in combustion gases (\(\approx 1000\)–\(1200\) m/s) and \(L\) is the grain length. For \(L = 1.5\,\text{m}\) and \(c_{gas} = 1100\,\text{m s}\): first axial mode \(f_1 \approx 367\) Hz.

    Combustion response function \(R(f)\) describes the propellant’s amplification of oscillating pressure at frequency \(f\). If \(R > 1/\mathrm{damping}\), instability grows. Suppression methods include:

    • Iron oxide (Fe\(_2\)O\(_3\)) or zirconium oxide (ZrO\(_2\)) added at 0.1–0.5%: act as combustion stabilisers by modifying the solid-phase chemistry, reducing the response function magnitude

    • Resonance suppressor rods or aluminum wires (acoustic damping)

    • Propellant composition modification: reducing AP bimodal ratio

    • Chamber geometry modification: non-cylindrical port reduces modal purity

    19.2.5 Motor Design¶

    Web Thickness and Burn Time¶

    The web thickness \(W\) is the minimum distance from the initial burning surface to the nearest case wall:

    \[t_b = \frac{W}{\bar{r}}\tag{19.19}\]

    where \(\bar{r}\) is the mean burn rate over the operating pressure range. Volumetric loading fraction \(V_f = V_{propellant}/V_{case}\) is typically 0.88–0.94 for tactical motors; maximising \(V_f\) reduces inert motor mass.

    Dual-Pulse Motor¶

    A dual-pulse motor contains two propellant grains in a single casing separated by a bulkhead/barrier. The barrier is designed to rupture at a controlled pressure after the first grain burns out. This provides:

    • Programmable thrust: high initial thrust for kinematic advantage, then reignited for terminal phase

    • Increased average velocity (reduced time to target = reduced target manoeuvre time)

    • Example: MBDA Meteor uses a dual-pulse design integrated with a throttleable ramjet sustainer

    • Mass penalty: bulkhead mass \(\approx\) 3–5% of motor mass

    Boost-Sustain Configuration¶

    The boost-sustain SRM uses a single case with two grain types:

    • Booster grain (fast-burning, forward section): high \(a\), higher \(n\), burns first; provides 10–50\(g\) acceleration at launch; burns in 1–5 s

    • Sustainer grain (slow-burning, aft section): lower \(a\), lower \(n\); provides 3–10\(g\) for 10–60 s flight duration

    • Transition between grains: natural as booster burns through; slight pressure dip at transition

    • Example: AIM-120 AMRAAM uses a boost-sustain SRM with a shaped grain providing high initial thrust then a lower sustain level

    19.2.6 Motor Casing¶

    Modern tactical missile casings use filament-wound Carbon Fibre Reinforced Polymer (CFRP):

    • Fibre: T700S or T800H carbon fibre; tensile strength 4,900 MPa; modulus 294 GPa

    • Winding: wet filament winding with epoxy resin (hoop + helical layers); hoop-to-helical ratio optimised for biaxial stress state under \(P_c = 7\)–\(14\) MPa

    • Specific strength: \(\sigma_u/\rho \approx 2200\) kN\(\cdot\)m/kg; 3–5\(\times\) steel on weight basis

    • Structural factor \(PV/W\): CFRP cases achieve 40–55 km; steel cases 15–25 km

    • Liner: \(\approx\)3 m m HTPB or EPDM bonded to case interior; prevents debonding of grain from case under thermal cycling; tested per AIAA S-080B

    Older/cheaper designs (SCUD, SA-2) use D6AC or 300M high-strength steel: heavier but more resistant to manufacturing defects and easier to weld.

    19.2.7 Nozzle Design and Materials¶

    The SRM nozzle must withstand:

    • Stagnation temperatures: 3,000–3,700 K

    • Erosive Al\(_2\)O\(_3\) particles at 100–200 m/s

    • Short burn times: 1–60 s (tactical); 60–180 s (strategic)

    Throat insert: isostatically moulded graphite or carbon–carbon (C-C) composite. C-C throat erosion rate: 0.01–0.05 mm/s. Graphite: 0.05–0.15 mm/s. The throat diameter at burn end is larger than at start by \(\Delta d = 2\,r_{erosion}\,t_b\), causing \(P_c\) to decrease over burn time (must be accounted in design).

    Exit cone: C-C composite or silica-phenolic ablative. C-C preferred for high-performance (lower mass, higher \(\varepsilon\)); silica-phenolic for cost-sensitive applications.

    Thrust Vector Control (TVC) in SRMs¶

    Several TVC approaches are used:

    • Flex bearing: elastomeric ball-and-socket mount for nozzle; deflection \(\pm 7\)–\(12°\); actuated by dual linear actuators (electromechanical or electrohydraulic); torque requirement: \(M_{act} \approx k_s\,\delta\) where \(k_s = 10\)–\(100\) kN\(\cdot\)m/rad; no losses to exhaust; cleanest design

    • Jet vanes: four tungsten or graphite vanes inserted in exhaust plume; deflection \(\pm 20°\); 3–5% thrust loss at full deflection; vane erosion limits life to \(<60\) s; used on early V-2, Shahab-3

    • Liquid injection TVC (LITVC): injectant (water, NaClO\(_4\) solution) injected into nozzle divergent section; asymmetric shock creates side force; no moving nozzle parts; lower authority (\(\pm 4°\) equivalent); used on Minuteman III third stage

    19.2.8 Insulation and Thermal Protection¶

    The case internal insulation must protect the CFRP or steel case from hot gas impingement:

    • Material: EPDM (Ethylene Propylene Diene Monomer) loaded with silica/aramid fibres; \(\rho = 1200\) kg/m\(^3\); char rate 0.05–0.20 mm/s

    • Heat flux from hot gas: \(q = h\,(T_{gas} - T_{wall})\); for \(h = 1000\) W/m\(^2\)K and \(\Delta T = 3000\) K: \(q = 3\) MW/m\(^2\)

    • Insulation thickness \(\delta_{ins}\): sized so bondline temperature \(T_{bond} < T_{limit}\) (180°C for CFRP) during motor operation

    • Rule of thumb: \(\delta_{ins} = r_{char}\,t_b + \text{safety margin}\)

    19.2.9 Ignition Systems¶

    The ignition sequence must deliver sufficient energy to initiate and sustain combustion across the full grain surface before chamber pressure builds:

    1. Squib (initiator): zirconium/potassium perchlorate bridge wire; fires on 1 A current; output \(\approx 50\)–\(100\) J; response time \(<3\) ms

    2. Igniter booster: aluminium/iron oxide thermite or BKNO\(_3\) pellets; produces 2,000–3,000 K gases; ignites propellant grain surface

    3. Grain surface ignition: requires surface temperature to reach propellant ignition temperature (\(\approx 500\)–\(700°C\))

    4. Build-up: pressure rises from ambient to \(P_c\) in 20–200 ms; controlled by igniter mass and energy output vs grain surface area

    Safety requirements: no-fire current \(< 0.45\,I_{fire}\) (ESD protection); all-fire current \(> 2.0\,I_{fire}\); thermal barrier for cook-off resistance (UN Series 6 testing).

    19.3 Liquid Rocket Engines¶

    Liquid rocket engines (LREs) offer advantages over SRMs in restartability, throttleability, and (for some combinations) higher specific impulse. They are preferred for strategic ballistic missiles requiring extended range through efficient propellant utilisation, and for certain tactical missiles requiring multi-burn capability.

    19.3.1 Propellant Combinations¶

    LOX/LH₂ (Liquid Oxygen / Liquid Hydrogen)¶

    • \(I_{sp} = 450\) s (vacuum, \(\varepsilon = 77\)); \(T_{ad} = 3,570\) K; \(O/F = 6.0\) by mass

    • Mixture density: \(\rho_{mix} = 360\) kg/m\(^3\) (very low — large tank volume)

    • Boiling points: LH\(_2\) at \(-253°C\); LOX at \(-183°C\)

    • Cryogenic complexity: super-insulated tanks; active conditioning before launch; boil-off losses during extended storage

    • Applications: ICBM upper stages (Titan II LR-87-AJ-11), Space Shuttle main engines (SSME); extremely high performance but impractical for quick-reaction tactical missiles

    LOX/RP-1 (Liquid Oxygen / Rocket Propellant-1)¶

    • RP-1: highly refined kerosene; density 820 kg/m\(^3\); storable at ambient

    • \(I_{sp} = 338\) s (sea level), \(353\) s (vacuum) at \(O/F = 2.72\)

    • \(T_{ad} = 3,600\) K; LOX still cryogenic (but only one cryogen)

    • High density impulse (\(I_{sp} \times \rho_{mix}\)) advantages compact missiles

    • Example engines: RD-107/108 (R-7), RD-180 (Atlas V), SpaceX Merlin

    • ICBM application: R-7 Semyorka (first ICBM, 1957); limited by LOX load-before-launch procedure

    N₂O₄/UDMH (Nitrogen Tetroxide / Unsymmetrical Dimethylhydrazine)¶

    • Both propellants liquid at room temperature and pressure (storable)

    • Hypergolic: ignite spontaneously on contact — no igniter required; enhances reliability and restart capability

    • \(I_{sp} = 310\)–\(320\) s vacuum; \(O/F = 2.0\) by mass; \(T_{ad} = 3,150\) K

    • UDMH: extremely toxic (IARC carcinogen); vapour pressure 20 kPa at 25°C; requires SCAPE suits for handling

    • Soviet/Russian missiles: R-36M (SS-18 Satan, \(I_{sp} = 318\) s); Chinese DF-5, DF-21 (UDMH/N\(_2\)O\(_4\))

    • Advantages: can remain fuelled for years in silo; instant launch readiness

    N₂O₄/MMH (Monomethylhydrazine)¶

    • \(I_{sp} = 300\)–\(310\) s vacuum; hypergolic; storable

    • Less toxic than UDMH; better handling safety margin

    • Used widely in spacecraft propulsion (Shuttle RCS, Ariane 5 upper stage)

    • Tactical missile application: Exocet AM39, MICA (divert motors)

    IRFNA/Fuel Systems¶

    Inhibited Red Fuming Nitric Acid (IRFNA) is an older storable oxidiser:

    • HNO\(_3\) + 13–15% N\(_2\)O\(_4\) + 0.6–1.0% HF (inhibitor to reduce corrosion of stainless tanks)

    • \(I_{sp}\) with kerosene: 250–270 s; with UDMH: 280–290 s

    • Used: SCUD-B, SS-N-9 Siren, Chinese YJ-83 early versions

    • Replaced in modern designs by N\(_2\)O\(_4\) (better \(I_{sp}\), less corrosive vapours)

    19.3.2 Turbopump Systems¶

    For high thrust LREs, pressurising propellant tanks to drive flow through injectors (pressure-fed) requires very heavy tanks. A turbopump delivers propellant at high pressure from low-pressure tanks, dramatically reducing tank wall mass:

    \[P_{pump} = \frac{\dot{m}_{pump}\,\Delta P}{\rho\,\eta_p}\tag{19.20}\]

    where \(\eta_p = 0.70\)–\(0.85\) is the pump efficiency. A centrifugal pump with specific speed:

    \[N_s = \frac{N\,\sqrt{Q}}{\Delta H^{3/4}}\tag{19.21}\]

    drives propellants to chamber pressure (7–30 MPa for modern engines). The turbine driving the pump is powered by hot gas tapped from:

    • Gas generator (GG) cycle: small fraction of propellants burned in separate GG at low \(O/F\); turbine exhaust dumped overboard; 1–3% performance penalty; simplest (RD-107, F-1)

    • Staged combustion (SC): all propellant passes through preburner then main chamber; no dump loss; higher \(P_c\) achievable (20–30 MPa); most efficient (SSME, RD-180); most complex

    • Expander cycle: fuel heated in cooling jacket powers turbine; no combustion for turbine drive; limited to H\(_2\) fuel; clean and simple (RL-10 upper stage engine)

    19.3.3 Combustion Chamber and Injector¶

    The injector atomises and mixes propellants to achieve rapid and complete combustion in the shortest possible chamber length:

    • Like-on-like doublet impinging: LOX-LOX + Fuel-Fuel jets impinge; produces fans of mixed propellant; good for low \(O/F\) ratios

    • Unlike doublet (F-O-F triplet): LOX jet impacts between two fuel jets; efficient mixing; used in many US engines

    • Coaxial shear: central LOX jet surrounded by annular fuel jet; shear mixing; used in Russian and European H\(_2\) engines (Vulcain, RD-0120)

    • Pintle injector: central rod with radial holes; LOX through annulus; throttleable 10:1; used in Merlin 1D (SpaceX), descent engines

    Characteristic chamber length \(L^* = V_c/A_t\) (distinct from \(L^*\) instability parameter): 0.5–2.0 m for most propellant combinations; ensures sufficient residence time for complete combustion.

    Combustion efficiency \(\eta_c = T_{c,measured}/T_{c,theoretical}\): typically 0.94–0.99 for modern injector designs.

    19.3.4 Regenerative Cooling¶

    Chamber wall temperature limits: copper alloy (NARloy-Z) walls \(< 900°C\); nickel superalloy walls \(< 1,100°C\). Hot gas side heat flux at throat:

    \[q_w = h_g\,(T_{ad} - T_{wg})\tag{19.22}\]

    where \(h_g \approx 5,000\)–\(50,000\) W/m\(^2\)K and \(T_{wg} = 500\)–\(900°C\) for regeneratively cooled walls, giving \(q_w = 5\)–\(150\) MW/m\(^2\) at the throat.

    Regenerative cooling: propellant (usually fuel) flows through milled or brazed channels in the chamber/nozzle wall. Wall temperature governed by:

    \[q_w = \frac{T_{wg} - T_{wc}}{R_{wall}} = h_c\,(T_c^{cool} - T_{wc})\tag{19.23}\]

    Dittus–Boelter correlation for coolant-side heat transfer:

    \[h_c = 0.023\,\frac{k_f}{D}\,Re^{0.8}\,Pr^{0.4}\tag{19.24}\]

    where \(Re = \rho_c\,V_c\,D/\mu_c\) is the coolant Reynolds number and \(Pr = \mu_c\,c_p/k_f\) the Prandtl number of the coolant.

    19.3.6 Throttling and Restart¶

    Deep throttling requires special injector design:

    • Pintle injector: scales flow by adjusting radial gap; 10:1 throttle demonstrated; minimum flow limited by combustion stability

    • Multiple injection elements: shut down discrete element groups; coarser throttle steps

    • Cavitating venturi: pressure-drop device maintains constant mass flow ratio during throttling; simple but limits minimum flow to 50% nominal

    Restart: hypergolic propellants restart on valve opening (no igniter needed); LOX/H\(_2\) engines require spark or torch igniter for restart; LOX/RP-1 use TEA-TEB (triethylaluminium / triethylborane) pyrophoric igniter (Falcon 9).

    Propellant management in zero-\(g\):

    • Surface tension / capillary devices: screen channels or vanes collect propellant at tank outlet; passive; reliable for \(<10\) N thrust

    • Positive expulsion bladder: elastomeric bladder separates pressurant gas from propellant; zero-\(g\) operable; used in attitude control systems

    • Slosh baffles: reduce liquid motion during vehicle manoeuvres; ring baffles spaced \(\approx D/4\) apart in tank

    19.4 Air-Breathing Propulsion¶

    Air-breathing engines use atmospheric oxygen as the oxidiser, eliminating the need to carry it. This dramatically improves \(I_{sp}\) and range, but the engine only operates while the vehicle is within the atmosphere and at sufficient speed.

    19.4.1 Ramjet¶

    Operating Principle¶

    A ramjet has no rotating machinery. Inlet ram compression decelerates the incoming supersonic (or subsonic) flow, converting kinetic energy to pressure:

    \[\frac{P_{02}}{P_\infty} = \left(1 + \frac{\gamma-1}{2}M_\infty^2\right)^{\gamma/(\gamma-1)} \cdot \eta_{ram}\tag{19.25}\]

    where \(\eta_{ram}\) is the inlet pressure recovery (0.85–0.97 for well-designed inlets). At \(M = 3\): stagnation pressure ratio \(\approx 36\), providing adequate combustion chamber pressure without a mechanical compressor.

    Inlet Types¶

    • Pitot inlet (normal shock): simple; used for subsonic ramjets (\(M<1.5\)); high pressure loss above \(M=2\): \(\eta_{ram} \approx 0.65\) at \(M=3\)

    • Conical spike inlet: oblique shock from central spike compresses flow; then normal shock at throat; \(\eta_{ram} = 0.90\)–\(0.94\) at \(M = 2\)–\(4\); used on Kh-31, BrahMos

    • Isentropic spike (Oswatitsch): shaped spike for fully isentropic compression via series of infinitesimal oblique shocks; highest recovery but complex; variable geometry for off-design

    • Side inlets (2D wedge): flat wedge inlets either side of fuselage; SCALP/Storm Shadow; avoids nose spike; lower recovery than axisymmetric

    Ramjet Combustion¶

    Fuel is injected into the subsonic flow behind the inlet throat. Flame stabilisation requires a recirculation zone:

    • V-gutter flameholder: creates recirculation behind bluff body

    • Step/cavity: backward-facing step creates shear layer trapping hot products

    The ramjet combustion Mach number is 0.2–0.4 (subsonic). Equivalence ratio \(\phi = 0.5\)–\(0.9\) for lean combustion to avoid over-temperature at inlet wall.

    Combustion efficiency \(\eta_{comb}\): with modern fuel injectors, \(> 0.96\).

    Performance Calculations¶

    Ramjet specific impulse:

    \[I_{sp,ramjet} = \frac{F}{\dot{m}_f\,g_0}\tag{19.26}\]

    For a liquid ramjet burning kerosene at \(M_\infty = 3\):

    • \(I_{sp} \approx 1100\) s (compared to \(\approx 260\) s for SRM)

    • No static thrust: requires solid-rocket booster to reach \(M > 1.5\) minimum starting Mach for the particular design

    • Maximum \(T/W \approx 8\)–\(15\) at design Mach number

    Liquid Fuel Ramjet (LFRJ)¶

    Throttleable by varying fuel flow rate; fuel-to-air ratio control. Kerosene (JP-10, JP-7, RJ-4) is most common. Advantages: throttleable; long-duration flight; efficient cruise. Disadvantages: fuel system complexity; corrosion protection for long storage.

    Example: P-270 Moskit (SS-N-22 Sunburn) — liquid ramjet; \(M \approx 3\) at sea level; range 120 km; terminal phase \(M = 2.8\) manoeuvring.

    Solid Fuel Ramjet (SFRP)¶

    The combustion chamber contains a solid HTPB fuel grain with a central port. Air enters the port; erosive/pyrolytic combustion of HTPB surface releases fuel vapour that mixes with air and burns:

    \[r_{HTPB} = a_{ram}\,\dot{m}_{air}^n \cdot P^m\tag{19.27}\]

    \(I_{sp} = 500\)–\(700\) s; fuel-to-air ratio self-regulates through port area change; non-throttleable; simpler than LFRJ. Used: British Aerospace Sea Dart (UK RN carrier-borne SAM, retired 2012); development in ASRAAM/CAMM derivatives.

    Integral Rocket-Ramjet (IRR)¶

    The booster SRM charge is packaged within the ramjet combustion chamber:

    1. At launch, inlets are blocked (pop-out covers); SRM burns, accelerates missile to ramjet start speed (\(M \geq 1.8\)–\(2.2\))

    2. SRM burns out; inlet covers jettisoned; air flows into now-empty combustion chamber (former SRM case becomes ramjet combustor)

    3. Fuel injected; ramjet combustion begins

    Advantage: no separate booster stage — very compact. Used: MBDA Meteor BVRAAM; Kh-31 Krypton; SA-6 Gainful (with solid booster + liquid ramjet in separate sections).

    19.4.2 Scramjet¶

    Supersonic Combustion Fundamentals¶

    In a scramjet (supersonic combustion ramjet), the airflow through the combustor remains supersonic (\(M_{combustor} = 2\)–\(4\)). This avoids the severe temperature rise from decelerating hypersonic flow to subsonic speeds:

    At \(M = 8\): decelerating to subsonic gives \(T_{stagnation} \approx 3,800\) K — above dissociation temperature of molecular nitrogen, effectively destroying the propellant oxidiser before combustion. Instead, the scramjet operates at \(M_{inlet} \approx 5\)–\(8\), \(T_{static} \approx 1,500\)–\(2,000\) K — hot enough to initiate combustion without exceeding material limits.

    Operating Principles¶

    The air enters the inlet, is compressed (but stays supersonic), and fuel is injected perpendicular to the flow. The residence time in the combustor is:

    \[\tau_{res} = \frac{L_{comb}}{V_{air}} \approx \frac{0.5}{2000} = 0.25 \, \text{ms}\tag{19.28}\]

    Chemical reaction timescale for hydrogen at \(T = 1,500\) K: \(\tau_{chem} \approx 0.05\)–\(0.2\) ms — marginally achievable. For hydrocarbon fuel at \(M=8\): \(\tau_{chem} > \tau_{res}\), making combustion incomplete without active mixing enhancement.

    Fuel Injection and Mixing¶

    • Parallel wall injectors: fuel injected parallel to flow; slow mixing; low drag; insufficient for \(M > 6\) without cavity enhancement

    • Strut injectors: transverse struts in flow with fuel through holes; better penetration; structural and drag penalty; used in HyCause, Hyshot

    • Ramp injectors: angled ramps create streamwise vortices; enhance fuel–air mixing via counterrotating vortex pair (CVP)

    • Cavity flame holder: shallow recessed cavity (\(L/D = 4\)–\(8\)) traps hot products for flame anchoring; acts as both mixer and flame stabiliser

    Scramjet Performance¶

    \[I_{sp,scram} = \frac{F}{\dot{m}_f\,g_0} = \frac{\dot{m}_{air}\,(V_e - V_0) + (P_e - P_a)\,A_e}{\dot{m}_f\,g_0}\tag{19.29}\]

    For hydrogen fuel (\(Q_{react} = 120\) MJ/kg), theoretical \(I_{sp}\) at \(M=8\): \(\approx 1,500\)–\(2,000\) s. For hydrocarbon (JP-10, \(Q_{react} = 43\) MJ/kg): \(\approx 800\)–\(1,200\) s.

    Performance degrades sharply outside the design Mach range:

    • Below \(M \approx 5\): inlet un-starts; combustor pressure too low for ignition

    • Above \(M \approx 15\): hydrogen dissociation losses; structural limits

    • Optimum window: \(M = 8\)–\(12\) for sustained propulsion

    Experimental Programs and Flight Tests¶

    • NASA HyperX X-43A (2004): Mach 9.6 at 33 km; H\(_2\) fuel; 10-second burn; proved supersonic combustion in flight

    • US AF X-51A Waverider (2013): Mach 5.1; 240-second burn on JP-7 fuel; longest-duration scramjet flight at the time

    • HyCause (Australia): \(M = 7.6\); hydrogen fuel; strut injectors

    • Russian Kh-47M2 Kinzhal: officially described as a hypersonic aeroballistic missile (modified Iskander); classified propulsion details; some sources claim modified turbofan + terminal boost, not true scramjet

    Thermal Management Challenges¶

    Combustor wall heat flux for \(M = 8\) scramjet:

    \[q_w \approx St\,\rho_\infty V_\infty^3 / 2 \approx 5\,\text{M W m 2}\tag{19.30}\]

    Active cooling with endothermic fuel (cracked JP-7 or H\(_2\)) absorbs heat while providing fuel. Copper-alloy channels; ceramic matrix composite (CMC) walls.

    19.4.3 Turbojet and Turbofan for Missiles¶

    Williams F107-WR-402¶

    The Williams International F107 turbofan powers the BGM-109 Tomahawk:

    • Thrust: 3.1 kN (700 lbf); mass: 66 kg; length: 0.71 m; diameter: 0.31 m

    • Thrust-specific fuel consumption (TSFC): 0.682 kg/kN\(\cdot\)h

    • Bypass ratio: 1.0; max Mach: 0.90; altitude: 0–10,000 m

    • Range: 1,600 km+ at subsonic cruise (Mach 0.7–0.8)

    • Enables long loiter (TLAM Block IV: waypoint programming, BDA capability)

    The turbofan’s \(I_{sp} \approx 4,000\) s vastly exceeds any rocket system, enabling globally significant range from a compact standoff weapon. The price paid is subsonic speed (no supersonic capability), large minimum altitude (must maintain air intake), and vulnerability to modern air defences.

    SAFRAN/Microturbo TRI 60-3¶

    Powers the French ASMP (Air Sol Moyenne Portée) nuclear cruise missile:

    • Variable cycle: subsonic turbofan mode (\(M < 1\)) transitioning to turbojet + afterburner mode (\(M > 1\))

    • Performance: Mach 3 at altitude; range 300–500 km; turbine inlet temperature \(> 1,600°C\) at maximum afterburner

    • Mass: \(\approx\) 190 kg; thrust (AB): 15 kN

    Variable-Cycle Engines¶

    At \(M > 1.5\), the compressor of a conventional turbofan suffers from stagnation temperature rise exceeding blade material limits. Variable-cycle solutions:

    • Reduce bypass ratio to zero (turbojet) above \(M = 1.5\)

    • Increase turbine inlet temperature (\(> 1,800°C\) with CMC blades + TBC coating)

    • Use variable nozzle geometry to maintain optimal expansion ratio across Mach range

    19.5 Combined Cycle and Advanced Propulsion¶

    19.5.1 Turbine-Based Combined Cycle (TBCC)¶

    A TBCC system integrates a turbojet/turbofan (low-speed operation) with a ramjet/scramjet (high-speed) in a shared inlet/nozzle framework:

    • Turbojet operates \(M = 0\)–\(3\); ramjet takes over at \(M \approx 2.5\)–\(3\)

    • Mode transition requires careful inlet management: variable geometry diverts flow between turbojet and ramjet paths

    • Advantage: single-stage-to-Mach-8+ without expendable boosters

    • Disadvantage: weight, complexity, mode transition instability

    • Program: DARPA Vulcan program; Aerojet/Rocketdyne TBCC studies; MHI HYPR (Japan)

    19.5.2 Rocket-Based Combined Cycle (RBCC)¶

    An RBCC incorporates a rocket inside the air-breathing flowpath:

    1. Ejector mode (\(M = 0\)–\(1\)): rocket fires; entrained air augments thrust; \(I_{sp} = 400\)–\(600\) s

    2. Ramjet mode (\(M = 2\)–\(4\)): rocket off; air-breathing combustion

    3. Scramjet mode (\(M = 5\)–\(12\)): supersonic combustion

    4. Rocket mode (\(M > 12\)): pure rocket for exoatmospheric phase

    19.5.3 Rotating Detonation Engine (RDE)¶

    Thermodynamic analysis shows that constant-volume combustion (detonation) is inherently more efficient than constant-pressure combustion (deflagration):

    \[\eta_{th,RDE} = 1 - \frac{T_1}{T_4} \cdot \frac{\Delta s}{c_p}\tag{19.31}\]

    In an RDE, a detonation wave rotates continuously in an annular channel:

    • Detonation wave speed: \(V_D = V_{CJ} \approx 1,700\)–\(2,500\) m/s (for H\(_2\)/air)

    • Wave rotates at \(f_{RDE} = V_D / (\pi D_{channel}) \approx 5\)–\(10\) kHz for \(D = 100\) mm

    • Theoretical gain in cycle efficiency vs gas turbine: 5–15%

    • Applications under development: next-generation cruise missile engines, USAF AFRL programs

    19.5.4 Pulsed Detonation Engine (PDE)¶

    A PDE operates in repeated detonation cycles:

    1. Fill tube with fuel-air mixture

    2. Initiate detonation at closed end

    3. Detonation wave propagates to open end; impulse generated

    4. Purge and refill; repeat at 50–200 Hz

    Specific impulse: \(I_{sp} = 1,200\)–\(2,000\) s (JP-10/air); \(> I_{sp}\) of turbojet but pulse nature complicates integration. Demonstrated: General Electric PDE test vehicle (2008), \(M = 0\) to \(M = 0.4\) ground tests at 100 Hz.

    19.5.5 Nuclear Thermal Propulsion¶

    Nuclear thermal engines heat a propellant (H\(_2\)) using a fission reactor:

    \[I_{sp,NTP} = \frac{1}{g_0}\sqrt{\frac{2\,\gamma\,R_u\,T_{reactor}}{(\gamma-1)\,M_w}}\tag{19.32}\]

    For \(T_{reactor} = 3,000\) K and H\(_2\) (\(M_w = 2\) g/mol): \(I_{sp} \approx 900\) s — approximately \(3\times\) better than chemical rockets.

    Project Pluto / SLAM (1961–1964): US program for a nuclear-powered subsonic cruise missile using a nuclear ramjet (TORY-IIC reactor, \(\approx 500\) MW thermal; unshielded; designed for one-way use; programme cancelled due to arms control concerns and ICBM superiority). All nuclear-propelled missile concepts remain banned under the INF Treaty framework and are not deployed operationally by any signatory nation.

    19.6 Nozzle Design and Thrust Vector Control¶

    19.6.1 Bell Nozzle Contour Design¶

    The Rao optimum bell nozzle contour is derived using the Method of Characteristics (MOC):

    1. Specify \(\varepsilon = A_e/A_t\) and desired nozzle length as fraction of equivalent cone length

    2. Using a source-flow field at the throat with initial expansion angle \(\theta_i \approx 25°\)–\(45°\) (function of \(\varepsilon\))

    3. Propagate characteristic lines through the nozzle; reflect off wall

    4. Optimise wall contour to minimise flow non-uniformity at exit plane

    The exit flow divergence angle \(\theta_e\) for a Rao optimum bell is:

    \[\theta_e = \theta_i - \frac{\theta_i - \theta_{cone}}{\varepsilon^{0.4}}\tag{19.33}\]

    For 80% bell (\(L_n = 0.8 L_{cone}\)): \(\theta_e \approx 8\)–\(12°\); thrust efficiency: \(\lambda_{bell} > 0.99\) vs \(\lambda_{cone} \approx 0.983\) for \(\alpha = 15°\) cone.

    19.6.2 Extendible Exit Cone (EEC)¶

    For strategic missiles, the nozzle must be compact during storage but achieve high expansion ratio in flight. The extendible exit cone solves this:

    • Cone stowed around motor case during storage; deployed pneumatically or by spring on motor ignition

    • \(\varepsilon\) increase from 20 (stowed) to 80 (deployed) for Trident II D5

    • \(\Delta I_{sp} \approx +15\)–\(25\) s from higher expansion ratio

    • Deployment mechanism: pyrotechnic actuators; carbon-phenolic cone sections telescope or hinge out; must seal at joint to prevent hot gas leakage

    19.6.3 Thrust Vector Control Systems¶

    Gimballed Nozzle¶

    The most common TVC for large SRMs and LREs:

    • Ball-and-socket or trunnion bearing mount; full \(\pm 8°\)–\(15°\) pitch and yaw

    • Actuator force: \(F_{act} \approx P_c \cdot A_t \cdot 0.015\) per degree (rule of thumb for gas loads)

    • Bandwidth: 3–10 Hz for gimbal; actuator must exceed this

    • Used: Minuteman III 2nd stage (gimballed motor); Trident II (stages 1–3)

    Flexible Bearing (Flex Nozzle)¶

    The elastomeric flex bearing is the dominant TVC method for tactical SRMs:

    \[M_{flex} = k_s\,\delta + c_s\,\dot{\delta}\tag{19.34}\]

    where \(k_s\) is the bearing spring rate (k N m r a d), \(\delta\) the deflection angle, and \(c_s\) the viscous damping coefficient. Deflection range: \(\pm 7°\)–\(12°\). Material: alternating layers of silicone elastomer and spherical shells of carbon-filled rubber; vulcanised bond; life: 50–100 firings.

    Liquid Injection TVC (LITVC)¶

    \[F_{side} = C_{F,inj}\,\dot{m}_{inj}\,\sqrt{2\,\frac{P_c}{\rho_{inj}}}\tag{19.35}\]

    Injectant creates a local oblique shock on the injected side; asymmetric pressure distribution produces net side force and moment. Specific impulse of injectant: \(I_{sp,inj} = F_{side}/(\dot{m}_{inj}\,g_0) = 100\)–\(300\) s. Advantages: no moving nozzle; very high bandwidth (100 Hz+). Disadvantage: consumes additional mass; lower control authority than gimballed nozzle.

    19.7 Propulsion Testing and Evaluation¶

    19.7.1 Static Fire Testing¶

    Test Stand and Instrumentation¶

    Static fire tests measure motor or engine performance in ground conditions:

    • Thrust measurement: load cell (piezoelectric or strain-gauge); range: 100 N to 10 MN; accuracy: \(\pm0.1\%\) FS; must account for parasitic forces (pressure lines, umbilical cables, resonances)

    • Chamber pressure transducer: flush-mounted or with cooling standoff; Kistler piezoelectric; 0–70 MPa; 50 kHz bandwidth

    • Mass flow: Coriolis meter (liquids); orifice plate / venturi (gases); propellant tank weight-loss method

    • High-speed cameras: 2,000–100,000 fps; combustion imaging; plume observation; nozzle erosion measurement

    • Exhaust gas sampling: post-test chemical analysis; combustion efficiency verification; emission characterisation

    Performance Derivation¶

    From test data, key performance parameters:

    \[\begin{align*} c^*_{meas} &= \frac{P_c\,A_t}{\dot{m}} \\ C_{F,meas} &= \frac{F_{meas}}{P_c\,A_t} \\ I_{sp,meas} &= \frac{\int F\,dt}{\dot{m}_{total}\,g_0} \end{align*}\]

    Efficiency ratios: \(\eta_{c^*} = c^*_{meas}/c^*_{th}\) and \(\eta_{CF} = C_{F,meas}/C_{F,th}\) characterise combustion and nozzle efficiencies independently.

    19.7.2 Altitude Simulation¶

    To measure vacuum \(I_{sp}\), a jet exhaust diffuser simulates altitude back-pressure:

    • A second-throat diffuser after the nozzle captures exhaust and creates a low back-pressure environment

    • Minimum starting pressure ratio: \(P_{chamber}/P_{ambient} > 3\)–\(5\) for diffuser to start

    • Altitude simulation facilities: AFRL Test Cell 2 (Arnold Engineering Development Complex, AEDC); simulates \(> 70\) km altitude

    19.7.4 Uncertainty Analysis¶

    Measurement uncertainty in \(I_{sp}\) (per JANNAF Rocket Performance Standards):

    \[\frac{\delta I_{sp}}{I_{sp}} = \sqrt{\left(\frac{\delta F}{F}\right)^2 + \left(\frac{\delta \dot{m}}{\dot{m}}\right)^2}\tag{19.39}\]

    Typical individual uncertainties: \(\delta F/F = 0.1\%\); \(\delta\dot{m}/\dot{m} = 0.2\%\). Combined: \(\delta I_{sp}/I_{sp} \approx 0.22\%\). For \(I_{sp} = 265\) s, this gives \(\pm 0.6\) s uncertainty.

    19.8 Propulsion System Design Integration¶

    19.8.1 Motor Mass Budget¶

    The structural coefficient \(\varepsilon_s\) quantifies inert motor mass fraction:

    \[\varepsilon_s = \frac{m_{inert}}{m_{motor,total}} = \frac{m_{case}+m_{nozzle}+m_{igniter}}{m_{propellant}+m_{inert}}\tag{19.40}\]

    Typical values: tactical SRM with CFRP case: \(\varepsilon_s = 0.05\)–\(0.10\); strategic SRM: \(\varepsilon_s = 0.03\)–\(0.06\); steel case tactical SRM: \(\varepsilon_s = 0.15\)–\(0.20\).

    The impact on \(\Delta V\) is profound:

    \[\Delta V = I_{sp}\,g_0\,\ln\!\left(\frac{1 + m_{pay}/m_{motor}}{1 - (1-\varepsilon_s)(1 - m_{pay}/(m_{motor}+m_{pay}))}\right)\]

    Reducing \(\varepsilon_s\) from 0.10 to 0.05 for a missile with mass ratio 3:1 increases \(\Delta V\) by \(\approx 7\%\) or equivalently allows \(\approx 14\%\) more payload mass.

    19.8.2 Stage Optimisation¶

    For a multi-stage missile, the optimal staging to maximise \(\Delta V\) for a given payload requires:

    \[\frac{\Delta V_i}{\Delta V_{total}} = \frac{I_{sp,i}}{\sum_j I_{sp,j}} \text{ (equal mass ratios for equal $I_{sp}$)}\tag{19.42}\]

    For unequal \(I_{sp}\) stages (common in practical missiles), numerical optimisation using Lagrange multipliers determines optimal propellant mass allocation per stage.

    19.8.3 Volumetric Efficiency and Packaging¶

    Missile airframe diameter is often fixed by aerodynamic or launch system constraints. The volumetric specific impulse \(I_{sp,v}\) accounts for propellant density:

    \[I_{sp,v} = I_{sp}\,\rho_{prop}\,g_0\tag{19.43}\]

    Composite propellant (\(I_{sp} = 265\) s, \(\rho = 1,820\) kg/m\(^3\)): \(I_{sp,v} = 4.73 \times 10^6\) N\(\cdot\)s/m\(^3\). Liquid N\(_2\)O\(_4\)/UDMH (\(I_{sp} = 310\) s, \(\rho_{mix} = 1,050\) kg/m\(^3\)): \(I_{sp,v} = 3.19 \times 10^6\) N\(\cdot\)s/m\(^3\).

    Despite higher \(I_{sp}\), the liquid storable propellant has lower \(I_{sp,v}\) — liquid missiles require larger diameter or longer body for the same \(\Delta V\), partially explaining the dominance of solids in compact tactical designs.

    19.8.4 Thermal Environment and Insulation Design¶

    The motor thermal environment affects:

    • Propellant temperature at ignition (burn rate prediction)

    • Structural case temperatures during and after firing (cool-down period)

    • Adjacent warhead and electronics temperature limits

    Thermal analysis uses the transient heat conduction equation:

    \[\rho\,c_p\,\frac{\partial T}{\partial t} = \nabla \cdot (k\,\nabla T) + \dot{q}\tag{19.44}\]

    With appropriate boundary conditions:

    • Internal (combustion): \(q_{internal} = h_g(T_g - T_w) - \rho_{ins}\,r_{char}\,L_{char}\)

    • External (aerodynamic heating): \(q_{aero} = h_{aero}(T_{aw} - T_w)\) where \(T_{aw} = T_0(1 + r(\gamma-1)M^2/2)\) is the adiabatic wall temperature (\(r\) = recovery factor \(\approx 0.89\) for turbulent flow)

    • End faces: convection to ambient air + radiation

    Thermal protection between motor and warhead/guidance section: 10–25 mm of phenolic or ablative material; verified by thermocouple measurements during qualification testing per MIL-STD-331.

    Summary of Key Propulsion Design Equations:

    \[\begin{align*} F &= \dot{m}V_e + (P_e - P_a)A_e & &\text{(Thrust)} \\ I_{sp} &= F/(\dot{m}g_0) & &\text{(Specific Impulse)} \\ \Delta V &= I_{sp}g_0\ln(m_0/m_f) & &\text{(Tsiolkovsky)} \\ c^* &= P_c A_t / \dot{m} & &\text{(Char. Velocity)} \\ C_F &= F/(P_c A_t) & &\text{(Thrust Coeff.)} \\ P_c &= (\rho_p c^* a A_b / A_t)^{1/(1-n)} & &\text{(Chamber Pressure)} \\ r &= a P_c^n & &\text{(Burn Rate)} \\ t_b &= W/\bar{r} & &\text{(Burn Time)} \end{align*}\]

    These relations, combined with the thermochemical equilibrium calculations (NASA CEA or PROPEP codes) and structural finite-element analysis, form the complete toolset for solid and liquid missile propulsion design.
