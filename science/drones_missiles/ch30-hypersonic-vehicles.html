    Chapter 30: Hypersonic Vehicles¶

    30.1 Hypersonic Flight Fundamentals¶

    30.1.1 Definition and Classification¶

    Hypersonic flight is conventionally defined as flight at Mach numbers exceeding 5, although the boundary is not a sharp aerodynamic discontinuity but rather a regime where a cluster of physically distinct phenomena emerge together and dominate the aerothermodynamic environment. The regime is usefully subdivided as follows:

    Table 30.1. Hypersonic Mach Number Sub-regimes and Dominant Physical Phenomena
    Sub-regime Mach Range \(T_0\) (K) Dominant Effects
    Lower hypersonic \(5 \le M \le 8\) 1,500–7,000 Thin shock layer, real gas onset, viscous interaction
    High hypersonic \(8 \le M \le 12\) 7,000–20,000 O\(_2\) dissociation, strong viscous interaction, radiation
    Extreme \(12 \le M \le 20\) 20,000–50,000 N\(_2\) dissociation, ionisation, plasma sheath
    Near-orbital \(M > 20\) \(>50{,}000\) Radiative heating dominant, ablation required

    Five characteristic features distinguish hypersonic flow from supersonic flow:

    1. Thin shock layer: The normal shock density ratio \(\rho_2/\rho_1 = (\gamma+1)/(\gamma-1)\) approaches 6 for \(\gamma = 1.4\) but rises toward 11–12 at high enthalpy where \(\gamma \to 1.15\). The shock therefore lies very close to the body surface.

    2. Entropy layer: Strongly curved bow shock produces a gradient of total pressure along streamlines, generating an entropy layer that swallows the boundary layer at downstream stations.

    3. Viscous interaction: The hypersonic boundary layer grows rapidly and its displacement thickness \(\delta^*\) induces an effective body geometry that modifies the inviscid pressure field; this interaction is absent in supersonic flow.

    4. High-temperature real gas effects: Vibrational excitation, dissociation, and ionisation alter the thermodynamic properties so that the ideal gas assumption \(\gamma = 1.4\) fails badly.

    5. Low-density effects: At altitudes above \(\sim\)60 km the Knudsen number \(Kn = \lambda/L > 0.001\) so slip-flow and eventually free-molecular corrections are necessary.

    30.1.2 Stagnation Enthalpy and Thermal Environment¶

    The stagnation (total) enthalpy governs the thermal load on every hypersonic vehicle. For an ideal gas,

    \[H_0 = h + \frac{V^2}{2} = c_p T + \frac{V^2}{2}\tag{30.1}\]

    At the standard sea-level speed of sound \(a_0 = 340\) m/s and flight velocity \(V = M \cdot a(h)\), the kinetic contribution dominates at high Mach number. At Mach 10 at 30 km altitude where \(a \approx 300\) m/s:

    \[V = 10 \times 300 = 3{,}000 \text{ m/s}, \qquad H_0 \approx \frac{V^2}{2} = \frac{(3000)^2}{2} = 4.5 \text{ MJ/kg}\tag{30.2}\]

    At 70 km altitude (where \(a \approx 296\) m/s), Mach 20 re-entry gives:

    \[H_0 = \frac{(20 \times 296)^2}{2} \approx 35 \text{ MJ/kg}\tag{30.3}\]

    For ICBM re-entry at \(V = 7{,}000\) m/s (\(M \approx 23\)), \(H_0 \approx 24.5\) MJ/kg. If frozen equilibrium is assumed (no dissociation energy returned to the flow), the corresponding frozen stagnation temperature at Mach 10 from standard atmosphere is:

    \[T_0^{\text{frozen}} = T_\infty \left(1 + \frac{\gamma - 1}{2} M^2 \right) = 226 \left(1 + 0.2 \times 100 \right) \approx 4{,}746 \text{ K}\tag{30.4}\]

    Under equilibrium real-gas assumptions, the actual gas temperature is significantly lower because substantial enthalpy is absorbed by dissociation and ionisation, leaving temperatures of 6,000–8,000 K for re-entry conditions rather than the frozen 50,000 K implied by Eq. (30.4) at Mach 25.

    30.1.3 Real Gas Effects and Variable γ¶

    The ratio of specific heats \(\gamma = c_p/c_v = 1.4\) for diatomic gases at ambient temperature. As temperature rises:

    Table 30.2. Real Gas Excitation and Dissociation Thresholds for Air
    Temperature (K) Physical Process Effect on \(\gamma\)
    \(>800\) Vibrational excitation of O\(_2\) and N\(_2\) \(\gamma\) decreases below 1.4
    \(>1{,}500\) Vibrational modes fully excited \(\gamma \approx 1.286\)
    \(>2{,}500\) O\(_2\) dissociation begins \(\gamma \to 1.2\)
    \(>4{,}000\) N\(_2\) dissociation begins \(\gamma \to 1.15\)
    \(>9{,}000\) Ionisation; plasma formation \(\gamma\) further reduced
    \(>15{,}000\) Multiple ionisation states \(\gamma \approx 1.1\)

    The partition function for a diatomic gas with vibrational temperature \(\theta_v\) gives the modified specific heat:

    \[c_v = \frac{R}{M}\left(\frac{5}{2} + \frac{(\theta_v/T)^2 e^{\theta_v/T}}{(e^{\theta_v/T}-1)^2}\right)\tag{30.5}\]

    where \(\theta_v = 2{,}256\) K for O\(_2\) and \(3{,}374\) K for N\(_2\). These real-gas effects profoundly alter the shock standoff distance, the surface pressure distribution, and the heat flux predictions; codes assuming \(\gamma = 1.4\) can overpredict stagnation temperatures by factors of 2–4 at re-entry conditions.

    30.1.4 Mach Number Independence¶

    For \(M \to \infty\), the Mach number appears explicitly only in the factors \((1/M^2)\) in the Rankine–Hugoniot relations. The shock-layer flow becomes independent of \(M\) asymptotically, so that aerodynamic coefficients \(C_L\), \(C_D\), and \(C_M\) become nearly constant above \(M \approx 8\). This Mach number independence principle (Oswatitsch, 1951) states:

    \[C_p \to C_p^\infty \quad \text{as} \quad M \to \infty\tag{30.6}\]

    The Newtonian limit gives \(C_{p,\max} = 2\) (for \(M \to \infty\), \(\gamma \to 1\)), and practical departure from this asymptote is within 5% for \(M > 8\).

    30.1.5 Viscous Interaction Parameter¶

    The viscous interaction parameter \(\chi\) characterises the coupling between the boundary layer and the inviscid flow:

    \[\chi = \frac{M_\infty^3}{\sqrt{Re_x}} \sqrt{C}\tag{30.7}\]

    where \(C = \mu T_\infty / (\mu_\infty T)\) is the Chapman–Rubesin parameter (\(\approx 1\) for reference temperature method). For strong interaction (\(\chi \gg 1\)), the induced pressure is:

    \[\frac{p}{p_\infty} = 1 + \frac{4}{3}\chi + 0.466\chi^2\tag{30.8}\]

    For weak interaction (\(\chi \ll 1\)):

    \[\frac{p - p_\infty}{p_\infty} = 0.332 \sqrt{\frac{2C}{\pi}} \chi\tag{30.9}\]

    This induced pressure affects both the drag (up to 30% increment at \(M = 8\) for slender bodies) and the heating distribution along the surface.

    30.2 Hypersonic Aerodynamics¶

    30.2.1 Newtonian and Modified Newtonian Theory¶

    For \(M \to \infty\), Newton’s corpuscular theory gives an exact pressure coefficient for any surface inclined at angle \(\theta\) to the freestream:

    \[C_p = 2\sin^2\theta\tag{30.10}\]

    Equation (30.10) is the Newtonian formula. It assumes that flow momentum normal to the surface is destroyed and the tangential component is conserved. The stagnation pressure coefficient on a normal flat face is \(C_{p,\max} = 2\), underestimating the true value (which is \(\approx 1.86\) at \(M = 8\), \(\gamma = 1.4\) from Rayleigh Pitot formula).

    The modified Newtonian formula introduces \(C_{p,\max}\) from the actual normal-shock calculation:

    \[C_p = C_{p,\max} \sin^2\theta\tag{30.11}\]
    \[C_{p,\max} = \frac{2}{\gamma M_\infty^2}\left[\left(\frac{(\gamma+1)^2 M_\infty^2} {4\gamma M_\infty^2 - 2(\gamma-1)}\right)^{\gamma/(\gamma-1)} \cdot \frac{1-\gamma+2\gamma M_\infty^2}{\gamma+1} - 1\right]\tag{30.12}\]

    This is accurate within 5% for bodies at \(M > 4\) and provides an extremely fast method for aerodynamic estimation during vehicle shaping.

    30.2.2 Tangent Cone and Tangent Wedge Methods¶

    For conical bodies the tangent cone method replaces the local surface by a cone of the same local half-angle \(\theta_c\) and reads the surface pressure from cone tables. For wedge-like surfaces, the tangent wedge uses the oblique shock equations:

    \[\tan\theta = 2\cot\beta \frac{M_\infty^2 \sin^2\beta - 1}{M_\infty^2(\gamma + \cos 2\beta)+2}\tag{30.13}\]

    where \(\beta\) is the shock wave angle and \(\theta\) is the wedge half-angle. The attached shock condition requires \(\theta \le \theta_{\max}(\gamma, M)\); beyond this the shock detaches and a strong bow shock forms.

    30.2.3 Blunt Body Aerodynamics¶

    Re-entry vehicles and crew capsules intentionally use blunt geometries to exploit the bow shock standoff distance for heat management. The strong normal shock ahead of the blunt body raises gas temperature substantially, but the shock itself radiates energy away and the stagnation point heat flux is controlled by the nose radius \(R_N\):

    \[\dot{q}_s = k \sqrt{\frac{\rho_\infty}{R_N}} V_\infty^3\tag{30.14}\]

    (Sutton–Graves correlation, SI units: \(k = 1.83 \times 10^{-4}\) for Earth atmosphere.) Thus doubling the nose radius halves the peak heat flux—the physical basis for Apollo’s 4.7 m diameter heat shield.

    30.2.4 Waverider Configurations¶

    A waverider is a hypersonic lifting body derived such that the bow shock is everywhere attached to the leading edge. High-pressure gas is trapped under the lower surface, producing efficient lift. For a Mach 6 wedge-derived waverider, the inviscid L/D can reach 5–7; including friction effects it reduces to 3–4.

    The design procedure:

    1. Choose a generating flowfield (conical or wedge shock).

    2. Trace streamlines from leading-edge locus lying on the shock.

    3. Project these streamlines onto the upper surface (stream surface).

    4. The body is the volume enclosed between upper and lower stream surfaces.

    Viscous-optimised waveriders (Bowcutt, 1987) account for skin friction from the outset and achieve \(L/D \approx 4.5\) at \(M = 6\), \(Re = 10^7\). The X-51A Waverider used a wedge-derived shape tailored to the isolator inlet geometry.

    30.2.5 Aerodynamic Heating Correlations¶

    The Fay–Riddell stagnation point heat flux formula for equilibrium gas is:

    \[\dot{q}_s = 0.763 \, Pr^{-0.6} \left(\rho_w \mu_w\right)^{0.1} \left(\rho_e \mu_e\right)^{0.4} \left(\frac{du_e}{dx}\right)^{0.5}_{s} \left(h_{aw} - h_w\right) \left[1 + (Le^{0.52}-1)\frac{h_D}{h_{aw}}\right]\tag{30.15}\]

    where \(h_{aw}\) is the adiabatic wall enthalpy, \(h_w\) the wall enthalpy, \(h_D\) the dissociation enthalpy, and \(Le\) the Lewis number. For flat-plate laminar heating:

    \[\dot{q} = 0.332 \, k \, Re_x^{1/2} \, Pr^{-2/3} \frac{T_{aw} - T_w}{x}\tag{30.16}\]

    For turbulent flow (transition-to-turbulence typically at \(Re_x \sim 10^6\)–\(10^7\) in hypersonic flow due to cooling effects):

    \[\dot{q}_{\text{turb}} = 0.0296 \, k \, Re_x^{-0.2} \, Pr^{-2/3} \frac{T_{aw} - T_w}{x}\tag{30.17}\]

    30.2.6 Radiative Equilibrium Wall Temperature¶

    In the absence of active cooling, the surface temperature reaches radiative equilibrium when aerodynamic heating balances surface radiation:

    \[\dot{q}_{\text{aero}} = \varepsilon \sigma T_w^4\tag{30.18}\]
    \[T_w = \left(\frac{\dot{q}_{\text{aero}}}{\varepsilon \sigma}\right)^{1/4}\tag{30.19}\]

    For a leading edge at Mach 7 flight in the upper atmosphere with \(\dot{q} = 500\) kW/m\(^2\) and \(\varepsilon = 0.85\) (C-SiC):

    \[T_w = \left(\frac{500{,}000}{0.85 \times 5.67\times10^{-8}}\right)^{0.25} \approx 1{,}850 \text{ K}\tag{30.20}\]

    This is within the operating range of C-SiC composites (\(\le 1{,}900\) K), illustrating why they are used for control surfaces on vehicles such as the X-37B.

    30.3 Thermal Protection Systems¶

    30.3.1 Classification and Selection Criteria¶

    Thermal protection systems are selected based on the trajectory-integrated heat load \(Q\) (J/m\(^2\)), the peak heat flux \(\dot{q}_{\max}\) (W/m\(^2\)), and the allowable surface temperature. The three primary classes are:

    1. Ablative TPS: mass-loss cooling; highest heat-load capacity; single-use.

    2. Hot structure / Radiative: reusable; limited by material melting temperature.

    3. Actively cooled: mass penalty of coolant circuit; unlimited in principle.

    Table 30.3. Thermal Protection System Comparison
    TPS Type Max Temp (K) Heat Load Reuse Density (kg/m\(^3\)) Application
    PICA ablator 3,600 Very high (\(>\)300 MJ/m\(^2\)) No 270 Apollo, Orion heat shield
    Carbon-Carbon 3,300 Moderate Yes 1,700–2,000 Shuttle nose, leading edges
    HRSI tiles 1,533 Moderate Yes 352 Shuttle lower surface
    C-SiC CMC 2,100 Moderate Yes 2,100 X-37B, X-43A fins
    TUFROC 2,600 High Yes 290 Capsule/glider TPS
    Metallic (W, Re) 3,000 Low–moderate Yes 19,300 (W) Nozzle throats

    30.3.2 Ablative TPS: PICA and Phenolic Systems¶

    Ablation works by endothermic pyrolysis and mass injection that drives the boundary layer off the surface. The energy balance on the ablating surface is:

    \[\dot{q}_{\text{aero}} = \dot{m}” H_{\text{ablate}} + \varepsilon \sigma T_w^4\tag{30.21}\]

    where \(\dot{m}”\) is the ablation mass flux (kg/m\(^2\)s) and \(H_{\text{ablate}}\) is the effective ablation enthalpy (10–30 MJ/kg for PICA). The blowing parameter:

    \[B' = \frac{\dot{m}” c_{p,e}}{\dot{q}_{\text{aero}} / (h_{aw} - h_w)}\tag{30.22}\]

    For PICA, \(H_{\text{ablate}} \approx 20\) MJ/kg. At \(\dot{q} = 10\) MW/m\(^2\) (Apollo peak), the ablation rate is approximately 0.5 kg/m\(^2\)s. PICA (Phenolic Impregnated Carbon Ablator), developed at NASA Ames, consists of carbon fibre felt impregnated with phenolic resin at \(\sim\)9% by volume. It is the primary heat shield material for Orion and SpaceX Dragon re-entry capsules.

    The char layer that forms acts as a thermal insulator. One-dimensional char regression is:

    \[\frac{ds}{dt} = \frac{\dot{m}”}{\rho_v}\tag{30.23}\]

    where \(s\) is the char depth and \(\rho_v\) is the virgin material density (270 kg/m\(^3\) for PICA).

    30.3.3 Carbon-Carbon Composites¶

    Reinforced Carbon-Carbon (RCC) uses a woven carbon fibre preform densified with pyrolytic carbon via chemical vapour infiltration (CVI) to achieve densities of 1,700–2,000 kg/m\(^3\). Its sublimation point is \(\approx\)3,600 K, making it the highest-temperature structural material in hypersonic use. Oxidation in air above 450°C is the critical failure mode, addressed by:

    • Silicon carbide conversion coating (reacts with C to form SiC at surface).

    • Tetraethyl orthosilicate (TEOS) impregnation for pore sealing.

    • Tantalum hafnium carbide (Ta\(_4\)HfC\(_5\)) additions for highest-temperature protection.

    30.3.4 Ceramic Tile Systems¶

    The Space Shuttle’s High-temperature Reusable Surface Insulation (HRSI) used tiles of LI-2200 alumina silica fibres bonded with colloidal silica at density 352 kg/m\(^3\). Thermal conductivity at 1,000 K is \(\approx 0.6\) W/m·K. The one-dimensional transient conduction equation governing heat penetration through the tile:

    \[\rho c_p \frac{\partial T}{\partial t} = \frac{\partial}{\partial x} \left(k(T) \frac{\partial T}{\partial x}\right) + \dot{q}_{\text{int}}\tag{30.24}\]

    Boundary conditions: outer surface from Eq. (30.18); inner surface bonded to aluminium structure limited to 450 K. Finite element solution with 2 mm element size resolves the spatial gradient adequately. Shuttle tiles reduced structure temperature to \(\le 175\)°C during nominal re-entry.

    30.3.5 C-SiC Ceramic Matrix Composites¶

    Carbon fibre–reinforced silicon carbide (C/SiC) CMCs combine the toughness of carbon fibres with the oxidation resistance of the SiC matrix. Fabricated by liquid silicon infiltration (LSI) or CVI at densities of 2,000–2,200 kg/m\(^3\). Key properties:

    • Flexural strength: 300–500 MPa.

    • Fracture toughness: 12–20 MPa·m\(^{1/2}\).

    • Maximum service temperature: 1,600°C in oxidising atmosphere; 1,850°C in inert.

    • Thermal conductivity: 7–20 W/m·K depending on fibre orientation.

    C/SiC is used for control surface leading edges and body flaps on the X-37B, X-43A, and European IXV re-entry demonstrator.

    30.3.6 Actively Cooled Structures¶

    For scramjet combustors and nozzles with sustained high heat flux (\(>5\) MW/m\(^2\) for minutes), passive TPS cannot survive. Regenerative cooling circulates cryogenic hydrogen fuel through channels machined into the combustor wall before injection:

    \[\dot{q}_{\text{wall}} = \dot{m}_{\text{fuel}} c_{p,H_2} (T_{\text{out}} - T_{\text{in}}) / A_{\text{cooled}}\tag{30.25}\]

    Hydrogen’s specific heat (\(c_p \approx 14{,}300\) J/kg·K) makes it an ideal coolant. Wall temperatures are maintained below the structural limit (\(\le 900\) K for nickel superalloys) even in the combustor. The X-51A and HyFly programmes used JP-7 fuel for endothermic cooling.

    30.4 Hypersonic Vehicle Types¶

    30.4.1 Ballistic Re-entry Vehicles¶

    The simplest hypersonic vehicle is the conical or biconic re-entry vehicle (RV) carried by an ICBM or SLBM. The trajectory is Keplerian (ballistic) through most of the flight; hypersonic aerodynamics matters only in the terminal phase (\(h < 60\) km). Key design parameters:

    • Ballistic coefficient: \(\beta = m / (C_D \cdot A)\); high-\(\beta\) (1,000–10,000 kg/m\(^2\)) for deep penetration.

    • Nose radius: \(R_N \le 50\) mm for high \(\beta\) (smaller \(R_N\) raises heating but reduces drag).

    • Body half-angle: \(10°\)–\(20°\) for conical shapes; matched to aeroballistic stability.

    The manoeuvreable re-entry vehicle (MaRV) adds aerodynamic lift to defeat ballistic missile defences. Terminal manoeuvre authority is \(\pm3–5\) km lateral displacement at 50 km altitude from a 3-second thrust burst.

    30.4.2 Boost-Glide Hypersonic Glide Vehicles¶

    The Hypersonic Glide Vehicle (HGV) is boosted to altitude by a ballistic missile, then released to glide unpowered at hypersonic speed through the upper atmosphere. Advantages include:

    1. Trajectory depressed below radar-detectable ballistic arc: ground radar first acquires at \(\le 1{,}000\) km versus \(\ge 3{,}000\) km for ICBM.

    2. Sustained manoeuvre capability: defeat both midcourse and terminal interceptors.

    3. Global reach from a single launch point.

    The approximate glide range is:

    \[R_{\text{glide}} \approx \frac{L}{D} \cdot H_{\text{entry}}\tag{30.26}\]

    A more precise expression accounting for exponential atmosphere decay:

    \[R_{\text{glide}} = \frac{L}{D} \cdot \frac{1}{\beta_{\text{atm}}} \ln\left(\frac{\rho_{\text{entry}}}{\rho_{\text{terminal}}}\right)\tag{30.27}\]

    where \(\beta_{\text{atm}} = 1/H_s\) is the inverse scale height (\(H_s \approx 7{,}000\) m). With \(L/D = 3\) and entry from 80 km to 30 km descent to terminal, range \(\sim 5{,}000\) km is achievable.

    Known programmes: China’s DF-17 (HGV on DF-ZF boost vehicle; \(\sim\)2,000 km range; operational 2019), Russia’s Avangard (claimed Ma 27, boost on UR-100N UTTH/RS-28 Sarmat), US Common Hypersonic Glide Body (C-HGB; Conventional Prompt Strike CPS).

    30.4.3 Hypersonic Cruise Missiles¶

    The Hypersonic Cruise Missile (HCM) is air-breathing, sustaining Mach 5–8 under scramjet power over ranges of 500–2,000 km. Key system elements:

    • Scramjet: dual-mode ramjet/scramjet (DMRJ) operating at Ma 4 (ramjet) through Ma 8+ (scramjet).

    • Booster: solid rocket accelerates to scramjet light-off at Ma \(\ge\) 3.5–4.

    • TPS: C-SiC or TUFROC for skin; regenerative cooling for combustor.

    • Guidance: INS + GPS (before blackout) + terminal seeker.

    The AGM-183A ARRW (Air-Launched Rapid Response Weapon) is a US USAF programme; after multiple test delays a boosted glide vehicle (not scramjet) was demonstrated successfully in 2023.

    30.4.4 Fractional Orbital Bombardment System¶

    FOBS places a weapon in low Earth orbit for a partial revolution (\(<0.9\) orbit) before de-orbiting onto the target. This circumvents early-warning radars oriented toward ballistic trajectories from known threat azimuths. The de-orbit phase is hypersonic re-entry. First deployed by Soviet Union (1968–83); revived conceptually in China’s 2021 fractional-orbit test (HGV payload in FOBS-like trajectory).

    30.5 Scramjet Propulsion¶

    30.5.1 Thermodynamic Cycle¶

    The scramjet is a ramjet variant in which combustion occurs in supersonic flow (Mach 2–5 within the combustor), avoiding the thermal choking that would result if the flow were decelerated to subsonic speeds at Mach \(>\) 5. The ideal specific impulse:

    \[I_{sp} = \frac{F}{\dot{m}_f g_0} = \frac{V_e - V_\infty + \Delta V_{\text{pressure}}} {\dot{f} \cdot g_0}\tag{30.28}\]

    where \(\dot{f}\) is the fuel-to-air ratio and \(V_e\) is nozzle exit velocity. Hydrogen-fuelled scramjets achieve \(I_{sp}\) of 1,000–3,000 s at Mach 6–12 (versus \(\approx 450\) s for turbojets).

    30.5.2 Inlet Design¶

    The scramjet inlet must compress the freestream from \(p_\infty\) to the combustor entry pressure \(p_2\) via external oblique shocks and possibly an internal normal shock (in dual-mode operation). The compression ratio needed for efficient combustion:

    \[\frac{p_2}{p_\infty} = \prod_{i=1}^{n} \frac{p_{i+1}}{p_i}\tag{30.29}\]

    For Mach 8 flight, total pressure ratio of \(\sim 50\)–100 is needed. A two-ramp inlet with \(\theta_1 = 7°\), \(\theta_2 = 12°\) achieves \(p_2/p_\infty \approx 40\) with \(\eta_{\text{KE}} = 0.97\). The kinetic energy efficiency:

    \[\eta_{KE} = \frac{V_2^2}{V_2^2 + 2 (h_2 - h_{2s})}\tag{30.30}\]

    30.5.3 Isolator¶

    The isolator is a constant-area duct between the inlet throat and combustor that accommodates the pre-combustion shock train. It prevents inlet unstart when combustor backpressure rises. Required isolator length:

    \[\frac{L_{\text{iso}}}{d_h} \approx \frac{50 \, \theta^*}{\sqrt{Re_\theta}} \quad \text{(Waltrup-Billig correlation)}\tag{30.31}\]

    where \(\theta^*\) is the momentum thickness and \(Re_\theta\) is the Reynolds number based on \(\theta^*\). Practical isolator \(L/d_h \approx 3\)–10 at \(M_2 = 2\)–4.

    30.5.4 Combustor: Fuel Injection and Mixing¶

    Hydrogen or hydrocarbon fuel is injected as a transverse jet into the supersonic cross-flow. Jet penetration height:

    \[h_{\text{jet}} = 1.23 \sqrt{J} \, d_j\tag{30.32}\]

    where \(J = (\rho_j V_j^2)/(\rho_\infty V_\infty^2)\) is the jet momentum flux ratio and \(d_j\) is the injector diameter. Strut injectors (mid-stream) improve mixing at the cost of increased drag and structural complexity. Cavity flameholders sustain ignition at low equivalence ratios.

    The heat release constraint from Rayleigh flow (thermal choking):

    \[\frac{T_{0,3}}{T_{0,2}} \le \left(\frac{1 + \gamma M_2^2}{1 + \gamma M_3^2}\right)^2 \cdot \frac{M_3^2}{M_2^2}\tag{30.33}\]

    At \(M_2 = 2\) the maximum heat addition before thermal choking (\(M_3 = 1\)) is:

    \[\frac{\Delta T_0}{T_{0,2}} = \frac{T_{0,\max}}{T_{0,2}} - 1 = \frac{9}{1} - 1 = \text{factor of } 8\tag{30.34}\]

    This large margin shrinks at \(M_2 = 1.2\) (near-sonic combustor entry in dual-mode).

    30.5.5 Nozzle and Thrust Calculation¶

    The nozzle expands combustor products to ambient pressure (ideally) using the lower vehicle forebody as an integrated nozzle. Net thrust:

    \[F_{\text{net}} = \dot{m}_{\text{air}}(V_e - V_\infty) + (p_e - p_\infty)A_e + \dot{m}_f V_\infty\tag{30.35}\]

    Specific impulse degradation at both ends: at low \(M\) the cycle approaches thermal choking (ramjet limit); at very high \(M\) the combustor temperature exceeds dissociation, returning energy to freestream chemistry and reducing net thrust. The practical operating range for hydrogen is \(M = 5\)–15.

    30.5.6 Inlet Unstart¶

    Unstart occurs when backpressure from the combustor is sufficient to expel the shock train past the inlet throat, causing a sudden and severe loss of pressure recovery. The shock is expelled supersonically outward in \(<1\) ms. Detection relies on rapid pressure sensing (\(<100\) Hz bandwidth inadequate; \(>1\) kHz needed). Recovery requires reducing fuel flow to re-establish shock train. The X-51A suffered an unstart event during Flight 2 (2011), causing vehicle loss.

    30.6 Guidance and Control for Hypersonic Vehicles¶

    30.6.1 Challenges Unique to the Hypersonic Regime¶

    Guidance, navigation, and control (GN&C) of hypersonic vehicles faces a set of challenges that do not occur in any other flight regime:

    1. Structural flexibility: Aerothermoelastic deformation of slender bodies at Mach 6–10 alters the aerodynamic moment arms and shifts the centre of pressure, requiring real-time model adaptation.

    2. Plasma blackout: At wall temperatures above \(\sim 9{,}000\) K or dynamic pressures producing ionisation, the electron density \(n_e\) in the plasma sheath rises above the critical value \(n_{e,\text{crit}} = m_e \omega^2 / e^2 \approx 10^{17}\) m\(^{-3}\) at 1 GHz, absorbing RF.

    3. Very low control authority: Dynamic pressure \(q = \frac{1}{2}\rho V^2\) can exceed 500 kPa at \(M = 8\), 30 km; a fin of 0.05 m\(^2\) produces 25 kN per degree of deflection. Mechanical stops of \(\pm 2°\) are typical.

    4. Time-varying aerodynamic model: Surface ablation changes geometry; reaction of the control system must account for this drift.

    30.6.2 Plasma Blackout¶

    The communication blackout during re-entry lasts from approximately Mach 15 descent at 70 km to Mach 8 at 45 km altitude (\(\approx 2\)–12 min for ICBM warheads). Strategies:

    • Pre-programmed terminal guidance (most re-entry vehicles).

    • Aerospike injection of electrophilic gas (SF\(_6\)) to reduce electron density.

    • Higher-frequency (EHF, 60 GHz atmospheric window) or optical (laser) communications.

    • Trailing-wire antenna kept out of plasma: effective below 60 km.

    30.6.3 Navigation During Blackout¶

    An Inertial Navigation System (INS) integrates accelerometer outputs to maintain position and velocity knowledge through the blackout. The Schuler loop period (\(T_S = 2\pi\sqrt{R_E/g} \approx 84\) min) means that short blackouts \(\ll T_S\) introduce negligible oscillatory error. The Kalman filter mechanisation for position, velocity, attitude (PVA) states:

    \[\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{k|k} = \hat{\mathbf{x}}_{k|k-1} + K_k \left( \mathbf{z}_k - H_k \hat{\mathbf{x}}_{k|k-1}\right)\tag{30.36}\]
    \[K_k = P_{k|k-1} H_k^T \left(H_k P_{k|k-1} H_k^T + R_k\right)^{-1}\tag{30.37}\]

    where \(\mathbf{z}_k\) are measurements (star tracker angles, when available) and \(R_k\) is the measurement noise covariance. Star tracker updates prior to blackout reduce INS drift to \(\le 30\) m over a 10-minute blackout.

    30.6.4 Terminal Guidance¶

    Upon blackout exit, a millimetre-wave radar (Ka-band, 35 GHz or 94 GHz) or an imaging infrared (IIR) seeker can acquire and track a ground aimpoint at distances of 5–50 km. For an HGV closing at 2,000 m/s with seeker noise \(\sigma_\theta = 0.1\) mrad:

    \[\sigma_{\text{miss}} = \sigma_\theta \cdot R_{\text{terminal}} = 10^{-4} \times 5000 = 0.5 \text{ m}\tag{30.38}\]

    This theoretical accuracy is degraded by latency, actuator bandwidth, and aeroelastic resonance to a practical CEP of 5–30 m for current systems.

    30.6.5 Trajectory Optimisation¶

    Skip-glide trajectories oscillate between the dense and rarefied atmosphere to extend range while managing thermal loads. Each skip peak reduces the heating rate; each trough provides lift. The optimal trajectory is found by solving the constrained optimal control problem:

    \[\min \int_0^{t_f} \dot{q}_s \, dt \quad \text{subject to} \quad \begin{cases} \dot{V} = -D/m - g\sin\gamma \\ \dot{\gamma} = (L/m - g\cos\gamma)/V \\ T_w \le T_{w,\max} \end{cases}\tag{30.39}\]

    Pseudo-spectral collocation (Legendre–Gauss–Lobatto nodes) is commonly used to discretise and solve Eq. (30.39) on-board or pre-loaded as a reference trajectory.

    30.7 Materials for Hypersonic Vehicles¶

    30.7.1 Ultra-High Temperature Ceramics¶

    Ultra-High Temperature Ceramics (UHTCs) are a class of transition metal borides, carbides, and nitrides with melting points above 3,000°C. The leading UHTC candidates for hypersonic leading edges:

    • Hafnium diboride (HfB\(_2\)): \(T_m = 3{,}380\)°C; density 11.2 g/cm\(^3\); thermal conductivity 104 W/m·K (which assists heat spreading). Oxidation resistance limited above 1,600°C without 20% SiC addition (forms SiO\(_2\)/HfO\(_2\) protective scale).

    • Zirconium diboride (ZrB\(_2\)): \(T_m = 3{,}245\)°C; density 6.1 g/cm\(^3\); less dense than HfB\(_2\); 80% ZrB\(_2\)–20% SiC composites withstand 2,200°C in air for 10 min.

    • Tantalum carbide (TaC): \(T_m = 3{,}880\)°C (highest of any binary compound); density 14.3 g/cm\(^3\); brittle; mixed Ta\(_4\)HfC\(_5\) achieves \(T_m = 3{,}942\)°C.

    30.7.2 Refractory Metals¶

    Tungsten (W), rhenium (Re), molybdenum (Mo), and niobium (Nb) are used in the highest heat-flux zones (nozzle throats, combustor walls). Pure W has \(T_m = 3{,}422\)°C but is brittle below the ductile-to-brittle transition temperature (DBTT \(\approx 400\)°C). W-Re alloys (5–26% Re) lower the DBTT to room temperature and improve ductility. Iridium coating on rhenium components provides oxidation resistance to \(\ge 2{,}200\)°C.

    30.7.3 Aerogel Insulation¶

    Silica aerogel (SiO\(_2\)) panels with density 80–100 kg/m\(^3\) and thermal conductivity \(k = 0.015\)–\(0.04\) W/m·K at 1 bar are used as sub-TPS insulation layers between the hot outer skin and the cool structure. At the radiative equilibrium surface temperature, the aerogel reduces the temperature on the structural side by:

    \[\Delta T = \frac{\dot{q} \cdot t}{k_{\text{aerogel}}}\tag{30.40}\]

    For \(\dot{q} = 50\) kW/m\(^2\), \(t = 25\) mm, \(k = 0.02\) W/m·K: \(\Delta T = 62{,}500\) K·m/W \(\times\) 0.025 m \(\times\) W/m·K \(= 1{,}562\) K — indicating that even thick aerogel layers must be supplemented by primary TPS at high heat flux.

    30.8.2 Shock Tunnels¶

    Shock tunnels use a diaphragm to initiate a strong shock wave that reflects off the end wall, producing a high-enthalpy reservoir at stagnation conditions. The reservoir gas then expands through a convergent-divergent nozzle to the desired Mach number. Test time is limited to the arrival of the expansion fan from the driver end (typically 2–20 ms).     • HIEST (JAXA): 25 MJ/kg; \(M = 7\)–15.

    • JF-12 (China): 3.5 km long; Mach 5–9; 100 ms test time (extended via shock tunnel modification).

    • HEG (DLR Germany): reflected shock + free-piston driver; 12 MJ/kg.

    30.8.3 Free-Flight Scramjet Tests¶

    Free-flight tests circumvent facility limitations by flying the actual vehicle. The NASA/DARPA Hyper-X programme flew two vehicles:

    • X-43A Flight 2 (March 2004): Ma \(\approx 7\); 11 s combustion; Pegasus booster; hydrocarbon fuel excluded, used H\(_2\).

    • X-43A Flight 3 (November 2004): Ma \(\approx 9.6\); 10–11 s combustion; highest sustained air-breathing flight speed.

    30.8.4 Computational Validation¶

    The Direct Simulation Monte Carlo (DSMC) method resolves rarefied gas dynamics at Knudsen numbers \(Kn > 0.01\) by tracking simulated molecular collisions. For continuum high-enthalpy flow (\(Kn < 10^{-3}\)), the reacting Navier–Stokes equations with finite-rate chemistry are solved by unstructured FVM codes (e.g., US3D, DPLR, FUN3D). Key validation metrics: stagnation heat flux within \(\pm 10\)%, surface pressure within \(\pm 5\)%, and wake structure topology.

    30.9 Ballistic Missile Defence Against Hypersonic Threats¶

    30.9.1 Detection Architecture¶

    Detection of hypersonic vehicles is more difficult than for ballistic missiles:

    • Trajectory: HGVs fly at 40–80 km altitude, below the SBIRS early-warning satellite sensor geometric horizon at short ranges; detectable at \(\le\) 800 km range from ground.

    • Radar cross section: manoeuvreable shape with potential RAM coating reduces detection range.

    • Emission: Low IR signature compared to ballistic missile boost phase; plume is small or absent after boost burnout.

    The Space-Based Infrared System (SBIRS) GEO satellites detect the boost phase reliably. The Hypersonic and Ballistic Tracking Space Sensor (HBTSS) constellation, planned for low Earth orbit (\(\sim\)400–1,000 km), provides persistent track of the glide phase with \(\le 10\) m CEP track accuracy.

    30.9.2 The Intercept Gap¶

    Current US BMD systems face an intercept gap against HGVs:

    • SM-3 Block IIA: designed for midcourse intercept at 1,000 km altitude; HGVs at 40–80 km are far below the SM-3 engagement envelope.

    • THAAD: maximum intercept altitude \(\approx 150\) km at steep angles; lateral manoeuvre of HGV defeats intercept at stand-down range.

    • Patriot PAC-3 MSE: terminal at \(<\)40 km; only effective in last seconds; closing speed \(\ge 7\) km/s makes guidance extremely demanding.

    The Glide Phase Interceptor (GPI) programme aims to fill this gap with a hit-to-kill vehicle capable of engaging at 40–80 km altitude with endoatmospheric manoeuvrability.

    30.9.3 Directed Energy Countermeasures¶

    High-energy lasers (HEL) theoretically offer unlimited magazine depth and near-speed-of-light intercept. Practical challenges against hypersonic targets:

    \[P_{\text{req}} = \frac{\pi \left(\frac{D_a}{2}\right)^2 I_{\text{kill}} \lambda^2 R^2} {D_a^2 (1 - \text{SR})} \cdot f_{\text{atm}}\tag{30.41}\]

    where \(I_{\text{kill}}\) is the kill intensity (W/m\(^2\)), \(R\) is slant range, and \(f_{\text{atm}}\) accounts for atmospheric turbulence and absorption. For a C/C target requiring \(I_{\text{kill}} = 10^7\) W/m\(^2\) at 200 km range with a 2 m aperture, required power \(\sim\) 5 MW — beyond current mobile HEL capability. Additionally, the plasma sheath around hypersonic re-entry bodies itself provides protective absorption of laser energy at wavelengths below the plasma frequency.

    30.11 Design Case Study: Boost-Glide Vehicle Trajectory Analysis¶

    30.11.1 Equations of Motion¶

    Consider a symmetric HGV lifting body with \(m = 900\) kg, \(A_{\text{ref}} = 0.5\) m\(^2\), \(C_L = 0.25\), \(C_D = 0.08\) (\(L/D = 3.125\)). The planar equations of motion in flight path coordinates:

    \[\dot{V} = -\frac{D}{m} - g\sin\gamma\tag{30.42}\]
    \[\dot{\gamma} = \frac{L}{mV} - \frac{g\cos\gamma}{V} + \frac{V\cos\gamma}{R_E + h}\tag{30.43}\]
    \[\dot{h} = V\sin\gamma\tag{30.44}\]
    \[\dot{R} = \frac{V\cos\gamma \cdot R_E}{R_E + h}\tag{30.45}\]

    where \(R\) is the ground range, \(R_E = 6{,}371\) km, and atmospheric density follows the exponential model \(\rho(h) = \rho_0 \exp(-h/H_s)\) with \(H_s = 7{,}100\) m, \(\rho_0 = 1.225\) kg/m\(^3\).

    30.11.2 Energy Management¶

    The vehicle’s specific mechanical energy is:

    \[E = \frac{V^2}{2} - \frac{\mu}{R_E + h}\tag{30.46}\]

    Rate of energy dissipation:

    \[\dot{E} = -\frac{D \cdot V}{m}\tag{30.47}\]

    The ratio of range to energy dissipated (\(L/D\) relationship):

    \[\frac{dR}{dE} = -\frac{L}{D} \cdot \frac{1}{g}\tag{30.48}\]

    Integrating from entry energy \(E_0\) to terminal energy \(E_f\):

    \[R = \frac{L}{D} \cdot \frac{E_0 - E_f}{g}\tag{30.49}\]

    For entry at \(V_0 = 5{,}000\) m/s, \(h_0 = 70\) km and terminal at \(V_f = 1{,}500\) m/s, \(h_f = 20\) km:

    \[\begin{align*} E_0 &= \frac{5000^2}{2} - 9.81 \times 70{,}000 = 12.5 \times 10^6 - 0.686 \times 10^6 = 11.81 \text{ MJ/kg} \notag \\ E_f &= \frac{1500^2}{2} - 9.81 \times 20{,}000 = 1.125 \times 10^6 - 0.196 \times 10^6 = 0.929 \text{ MJ/kg} \notag \\ R &= 3.125 \times \frac{(11.81 - 0.929) \times 10^6}{9.81} \approx 3{,}470 \text{ km} \end{align*}\]

    This simple analytical estimate agrees within 15% of 6-DOF numerical integration for the same vehicle.

    30.11.3 Heating Constraint¶

    Peak stagnation heat flux during the glide (Sutton–Graves):

    \[\dot{q}_s = k \sqrt{\frac{\rho(h)}{R_N}} V^3\tag{30.51}\]

    Differentiating and setting \(d\dot{q}_s/dt = 0\) along the glide path, the peak occurs near:

    \[h^* = H_s \ln\left(\frac{\rho_0 k^2 V^6}{2 \dot{q}_{s,\max}^2 R_N}\right)\tag{30.52}\]

    Design specification: \(\dot{q}_{s,\max} = 10\) MW/m\(^2\); this drives the minimum nose radius \(R_N \ge 40\) mm for the peak entry speed profile.

    30.12 Future Directions in Hypersonic Technology¶

    30.12.1 Combined Cycle Propulsion¶

    Turbine-Based Combined Cycle (TBCC) uses a turbojet for Mach 0–4, transitioning to a ramjet/scramjet for Mach 4–12, enabling horizontal take-off, sustained cruise, and recovery. Key transition challenges: mode switch from turbojet to ramjet at Mach 3.5–4 without thrust interruption; inlet re-geometry. SABRE (Synergistic Air-Breathing Rocket Engine, Reaction Engines Ltd) uses a heat-exchanger precooler to enable turbo-ramjet operation to Mach 5+ before switching to rocket mode for orbital insertion.

    30.12.2 Detonation-Based Propulsion¶

    Rotating Detonation Engine (RDE) offers potential thermodynamic efficiency gains over constant-pressure deflagration combustion. Applied to scramjets: detonation wave traverses annular combustor at supersonic speed; near-constant-volume heat addition increases cycle efficiency. AFRL and multiple university programmes are investigating RDE integration.

    30.12.3 Autonomous Hypersonic Vehicles¶

    Artificial intelligence for real-time trajectory replanning in response to detected threats or navigation errors represents a key research frontier. Challenges: latency constraints (\(<50\) ms for 10-g manoeuvre), radiation-hardened compute at \(>100\)°C internal temperature, training data scarcity for rare hypersonic flight regimes.

    30.12.4 Reusable Hypersonic Platforms¶

    The X-37B Orbital Test Vehicle (OTV), operated by USAF Space Force, routinely re-enters at orbital velocity (Mach 25+) and lands on a runway. Its C/SiC and tile TPS system has survived multiple missions (OTV-6: 908 days on-orbit). This demonstrates reusability of ceramic TPS for orbital class vehicles. Extension to hypersonic cruise vehicles requires demonstration of TPS robustness for 10–100 re-uses at Mach 8–12 entry conditions.

    Summary¶

    Hypersonic vehicles occupy the forefront of aerospace technology due to the simultaneous demands they place on aerodynamics, materials, propulsion, and guidance. Key quantitative relationships:

    • Stagnation enthalpy scales as \(V^2/2\); at Mach 10 this is \(\sim 4\)–12 MJ/kg depending on altitude.

    • Stagnation heat flux scales as \(\rho^{0.5} V^3 / R_N^{0.5}\); nose radius is the primary design variable for heat management.

    • Scramjet specific impulse (\(I_{sp} \sim 1{,}000\)–\(3{,}000\) s) greatly exceeds rockets for air-breathing flight, enabling sustained hypersonic cruise.

    • Glide range scales as \((L/D) \times \Delta E / g\); current HGVs with \(L/D = 3\)–4 achieve ranges of 2,000–5,000 km.

    • No current BMD system reliably intercepts manoeuvring HGVs at 40–80 km altitude; the intercept gap drives next-generation programmes (GPI, HBTSS).

    Mastery of hypersonic design requires integration of thermochemistry, high-enthalpy gas dynamics, materials science, and advanced guidance algorithms — making it one of the most demanding and strategically significant fields in contemporary aerospace engineering.
