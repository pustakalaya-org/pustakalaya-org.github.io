    Chapter 18: Missile Aerodynamics¶

    18.1 Introduction to Missile Aerodynamics¶

    Missile aerodynamics sits at the intersection of classical fluid mechanics, structural dynamics, and flight-control engineering. Unlike aircraft aerodynamics—which is dominated by near-subsonic, quasi-steady regimes with well-characterised stall behaviour—missiles must operate reliably across Mach 0.3 to Mach 25, spanning subsonic, transonic, supersonic, and hypersonic regimes in a single flight trajectory. Each regime introduces qualitatively different flow physics: separated low-speed laminar flow, transonic shock formation, oblique shock–expansion fan systems, and chemically reacting real-gas hypersonic flows.

    The aerodynamic force and moment system on a missile uses a body-fixed coordinate frame: axial force \(X\) (along body axis, positive rearward), normal force \(Z\) (positive upward in pitch plane), and side force \(Y\) (positive starboard). Corresponding moments: pitching moment \(M\) (positive nose-up), yawing moment \(N\) (positive nose-right), rolling moment \(L\) (positive starboard-wing-down). Non-dimensional coefficients are formed with dynamic pressure \(q_\infty = \frac{1}{2}\rho_\infty V_\infty^2\), reference area \(S_{ref} = \pi D^2/4\) (body cross-section), and reference length \(D\) (body diameter) for moments:

    \[\begin{align*} C_N &= \frac{Z}{q_\infty S_{ref}} & C_A &= \frac{X}{q_\infty S_{ref}} & C_m &= \frac{M}{q_\infty S_{ref} D} \end{align*}\]

    18.2 Missile Body Aerodynamics¶

    18.2.1 Nose Geometry¶

    The nose cone geometry determines zero-lift wave drag and the nose contribution to normal force. Principal nose shapes used in missile design:

    Conical Nose¶

    A cone of half-angle \(\theta_c\) has wave-drag coefficient at supersonic speeds (linearised theory):

    \[C_{D,cone} = \frac{2\theta_c^2}{\sqrt{Ma^2 - 1}}\bigl(1 + \text{correction terms}\bigr)\tag{18.2}\]

    Conical noses are simple to manufacture and have low wave drag at Mach \(>\)3 but higher drag than optimised shapes at Mach 1–3.

    Ogive Nose¶

    The tangent ogive is the circular-arc profile whose arc is tangent to the body cylinder at the shoulder. Nose radius \(R_{ogive}\), calibre \(D\), and nose length \(L_n\) are related by:

    \[R_{ogive} = \frac{(L_n/D)^2 + 0.25}{1}\cdot\frac{D}{2}\tag{18.3}\]

    The cross-sectional radius at axial station \(x\) (measured from nose tip):

    \[r(x) = \sqrt{R_{ogive}^2 - (L_n - x)^2} - (R_{ogive} - D/2)\tag{18.4}\]

    Optimum fineness ratio \(L_n/D \approx 3\)–\(4\) for minimum wave drag at Mach 1.5–3; increasing \(L_n/D\) beyond 5 gives diminishing drag reduction while reducing internal volume.

    Von Kármán (LD-Haack) Nose¶

    Derived by minimising wave drag for a fixed enclosed volume (von Kármán, 1947):

    \[r(x) = \frac{R}{\sqrt{\pi}}\bigl[\theta - \tfrac{1}{2}\sin(2\theta)\bigr]^{1/2}, \quad \theta = \arccos\!\left(1 - \frac{2x}{L_n}\right)\tag{18.5}\]

    where \(R\) is the base radius and \(L_n\) is nose length. The LD-Haack profile has 20–30% lower wave drag than a tangent ogive of equal fineness ratio at Mach 1.5–3.

    Parabolic Nose¶

    A parabolic profile \(r(x) = R(x/L_n)^n\) where \(n = 0.5\)–\(0.75\); simple to define; drag intermediate between conical and von Kármán.

    18.2.2 Normal Force on the Body¶

    Slender-body theory (Allen and Perkins, 1951) separates body normal force into a potential (linear) term and a crossflow viscous (nonlinear) term:

    \[C_{N,body} = C_{N,body}^{linear}(\alpha) + C_{N,body}^{nonlinear}(\alpha)\tag{18.6}\]

    The linear (potential flow) term from slender-body theory:

    \[\frac{dC_N}{d\alpha}\bigg|_{linear} = \frac{2}{D}\left(\frac{A_{base}}{S_{ref}} - \frac{A_{nose}}{S_{ref}}\right)\tag{18.7}\]

    For a body of revolution with no nose cut-off: \(dC_N/d\alpha \approx 2 \times A_{base}/S_{ref} = 2\) (per radian) where \(S_{ref} = A_{base}\).

    The nonlinear crossflow term at angle of attack \(\alpha > 10°\):

    \[C_{N,body}^{NL} = \eta\,C_{d,cf}\,\frac{A_{plan}}{S_{ref}}\sin^2\alpha\tag{18.8}\]

    where \(C_{d,cf} \approx 1.2\) is the crossflow drag coefficient of a cylinder, \(A_{plan}\) is the planform area of the body (projected side area), and \(\eta \approx 0.8\)–\(1.0\) is an empirical correction factor. The centre of pressure of the crossflow term is located at mid-body; this moves \(x_{cp}\) aft as \(\alpha\) increases, potentially destabilising a marginally stable missile.

    18.2.3 Body Drag Components¶

    Total zero-lift drag coefficient of a missile body:

    \[C_{D0,body} = C_{D,friction} + C_{D,wave} + C_{D,base}\tag{18.9}\]

    Skin friction.

    For turbulent flow (transition at \(Re_x \approx 5\times10^5\)), the compressible turbulent skin friction using the van Driest II method:

    \[C_f = \frac{0.455}{(\log_{10} Re_L)^{2.58} (1 + 0.21 Ma_\infty^2)^{0.467}}\tag{18.10}\]

    Wetted area correction: \(C_{D,friction} = C_f \cdot (S_{wet}/S_{ref})\). For \(Ma = 2\), \(Re_L = 10^7\): \(C_f \approx 0.0027\); \(S_{wet}/S_{ref} \approx 4 L/D\); \(C_{D,friction} \approx 0.054\) for \(L/D = 5\).

    Wave drag.

    At supersonic speeds, linear wave drag of a smooth body of revolution (linearised theory):

    \[C_{D,wave} = \frac{4}{\pi S_{ref}}\int_0^L\!\int_0^L \frac{d^2A}{dx^2}\frac{d^2A}{d\xi^2}\ln|x-\xi|\,dx\,d\xi\tag{18.11}\]

    For the Sears–Haack body (minimum wave drag for fixed volume \(V\) and length \(L\)):

    \[C_{D,wave,min} = \frac{24\pi V}{L^2 S_{ref}} = \frac{9\pi^2}{2}\left(\frac{A_{max}}{L^2}\right)\tag{18.12}\]

    Base drag.

    Base drag arises from the low-pressure region aft of a blunt base. The empirical Platou formula:

    \[C_{D,base} = \frac{0.25}{1 + 0.4\,Ma_\infty^2}\cdot\frac{A_{base}}{S_{ref}}\tag{18.13}\]

    Base drag is reduced by a boattail (reducing \(A_{base}\)), a nozzle plume (which acts as an aerodynamic boat-tail), or a base-bleed device. At Mach 2, a boattail of 10° half-angle reducing base area by 50% cuts base drag by \(\approx\)60%.

    18.2.4 Centre of Pressure of the Body¶

    From slender-body theory, the axial location of the body CP (linear term only):

    \[x_{cp,body} = \frac{\int_0^L x\,\frac{d(dC_N/dx)}{dx}\,dx}{\int_0^L \frac{d(dC_N/dx)}{dx}\,dx} = x_{centroid,A(x)}\tag{18.14}\]

    For a tangent ogive + cylinder configuration, \(x_{cp,body} \approx 0.67\,L_n\) (located within the ogive).

    18.3 Fin Aerodynamics¶

    18.3.1 Fin Planform Selection¶

    Fin planforms in order of decreasing subsonic normal-force slope (per unit aspect ratio): rectangular \(>\) trapezoidal \(>\) delta \(\approx\) cropped delta. The fin aspect ratio: \(AR = b^2/S_{fin}\) where \(b\) is fin span and \(S_{fin}\) is fin area. For a delta fin: \(AR = 4/(C_{root}/b + C_{tip}/b) = 4/\bar{c}/b\) where \(\bar{c}\) is mean chord.

    18.3.2 Fin Normal-Force Slope¶

    For a single fin at angle of attack \(\alpha\), the subsonic normal-force slope per unit \(\alpha\) (Prandtl–Glauert corrected):

    \[C_{N\alpha,fin} = \frac{2\pi AR}{2 + \sqrt{4 + AR^2(1 + \tan^2\Lambda_{c/2}/\beta^2)}}\tag{18.15}\]

    where \(\beta = \sqrt{1 - Ma^2}\) (subsonic), \(\Lambda_{c/2}\) is quarter-chord sweep. For \(AR = 2\), \(Ma = 0.5\), \(\Lambda_{c/2} = 45°\): \(C_{N\alpha,fin} \approx 2.1\) rad\(^{-1}\).

    At supersonic speeds (Ackeret 2D linear theory, thin fin):

    \[C_{N\alpha,fin,sup} = \frac{4}{\sqrt{Ma^2 - 1}}\tag{18.16}\]

    For 3D fins a correction factor \(K\) (typically 0.8–0.95 for practical AR) applies.

    18.3.3 Body–Fin Interference: Carryover Factors¶

    The total normal-force coefficient of a body–fin combination exceeds the sum of isolated components due to mutual interference. The Nielsen interference factors:

    \[\begin{align*} K_{W(B)} &= \text{ratio of fin lift in presence of body to isolated fin lift} \\ K_{B(W)} &= \text{ratio of body lift (carryover from fin) to isolated fin lift} \end{align*}\]

    These are functions of \(d/(d+s)\) where \(d\) is body diameter and \(s\) is fin semi-span. For \(d/(d+s) = 0.5\): \(K_{W(B)} \approx 1.37\); \(K_{B(W)} \approx 0.87\). Total fin + carryover contribution:

    \[C_{N,fins+carryover} = n_{fins} \cdot [K_{W(B)} + K_{B(W)}]\,C_{N\alpha,fin}\,\alpha\,\frac{S_{fin}}{S_{ref}}\tag{18.19}\]

    where \(n_{fins}\) is the number of fins (typically 3 or 4).

    18.3.4 Fin Flutter¶

    Panel flutter velocity (simplified Theodorsen approach for a rectangular panel):

    \[V_F = \sqrt{\frac{2\pi E t^3 \beta_F}{3(1-\nu^2) \rho_\infty c^4}}\tag{18.20}\]

    where \(E\) is panel Young’s modulus, \(t\) is panel thickness, \(\nu\) is Poisson’s ratio, \(c\) is chord, and \(\beta_F\) is a non-dimensional flutter parameter (\(\approx 0.07\) for an aspect-ratio-1 panel). Structural damping ratio \(\zeta > 0.01\) increases \(V_F\) by 10–15%. Titanium alloy fins (Ti-6Al-4V: \(E = 114\) GPa, \(\rho = 4{,}430\) kg/m\(^3\)) vs. aluminium 7075 (\(E = 72\) GPa, \(\rho = 2{,}810\) kg/m\(^3\)): Ti provides \(\approx 2.0\times\) higher specific stiffness, increasing \(V_F\) by \(\approx 40\%\) at the same geometry.

    18.3.5 Roll Stabilisation and Spin Rate¶

    Fin cant angle \(\phi_{cant}\) induces a rolling moment:

    \[C_{l,cant} = n_{fins}\,C_{N\alpha,fin}\,\phi_{cant}\,\frac{S_{fin}}{S_{ref}}\,\frac{y_{fin}}{D}\tag{18.21}\]

    Steady-state roll rate for a free-rolling missile:

    \[p = \frac{C_{l,cant}\,q_\infty\,S_{ref}\,D}{I_{xx}\,|C_{l\delta}|\,q_\infty\,S_{ref}\,D\,D/(2V_m)}\tag{18.22}\]

    Spinning stabilisation is used in unguided rockets; guided missiles typically require low roll rate (\(<\)5 rad/s) to prevent seeker gimbal saturation and cross-coupling between pitch and yaw axes. Roll rate induced by cant angle of 1° (\(\phi_{cant} \approx 0.0175\) rad) on a Mach 2 missile at sea level (HIMARS GMLRS geometry): \(p \approx 12\) rad/s.

    18.4 Aerodynamic Coefficient Assembly¶

    18.4.1 Normal Force Coefficient¶

    The total normal force coefficient by additive interference method:

    \[C_N = C_{N,body} + C_{N,fins+carryover} + C_{N,canard}\bigl(\text{if present}\bigr)\tag{18.23}\]

    For a tail-controlled missile at \(\alpha = 5°\), \(Ma = 2\), \(n = 4\) fins of \(S_{fin}/S_{ref} = 0.8\):

    • \(C_{N,body} = 2.0\,\alpha_{rad} = 2.0 \times 0.0873 = 0.175\)

    • \(K_{W(B)} + K_{B(W)} = 2.0\) (typical)

    • \(C_{N\alpha,fin} = 4/\sqrt{4-1} = 2.31\) rad\(^{-1}\)

    • \(C_{N,fins} = 4 \times 2.0 \times 2.31 \times 0.0873 \times 0.8 = 1.29\)

    • \(C_N = 0.175 + 1.29 = 1.47\)

    18.4.2 Pitching Moment Coefficient and Stability¶

    Pitching moment coefficient about the nose (reference point \(x_{ref} = 0\)):

    \[C_m = C_{m,body} + C_{m,fins} = -C_{N,body}\frac{x_{cp,body}}{D} - C_{N,fins}\frac{x_{cp,fins}}{D}\tag{18.24}\]

    Pitching moment slope about the CG:

    \[C_{m\alpha} = C_{N\alpha}\,\frac{x_{CG} - x_{CP}}{D}\tag{18.25}\]

    Static stability criterion: \(C_{m\alpha} < 0\) (restoring moment for positive AoA). This requires \(x_{CG} < x_{CP}\) (CG ahead of CP), i.e. positive static margin:

    \[SM = \frac{x_{CP} - x_{CG}}{D} > 0\tag{18.26}\]

    Typical design target: \(SM \approx 0.5\)–\(2.0\) calibres. Insufficient SM \(\to\) instability; excessive SM \(\to\) sluggish response, high control surface deflections required, aerodynamic heating of tail concentrated.

    18.4.3 Centre of Pressure Calculation¶

    Weighted-average CP location:

    \[x_{CP} = \frac{\sum_i C_{N\alpha,i}\,x_{CP,i}}{\sum_i C_{N\alpha,i}}\tag{18.27}\]

    where the sum is over: nose, body expansion, body crossflow, fins + carryover. This is the Barrowman method (extended by missile DATCOM), applicable up to \(\alpha \approx 10°\) in the linear regime. Above \(\alpha = 10°\), nonlinear crossflow terms must be added per Eq. ((18.8)).

    18.4.4 Axial Force Coefficient¶

    \[C_A = C_{A,friction} + C_{A,pressure,nose} + C_{A,base} + C_{A,fins}\tag{18.28}\]

    At lift-off (subsonic, \(Ma < 0.5\)): \(C_A \approx 0.3\)–\(0.5\) (friction + base dominated). At Mach 2 (supersonic): \(C_A \approx 0.6\)–\(1.2\) (wave drag now dominant, combined with friction). The total drag \(D = q_\infty S_{ref}(C_A\cos\alpha + C_N\sin\alpha)\) and lift \(L = q_\infty S_{ref}(C_N\cos\alpha - C_A\sin\alpha)\).

    18.4.5 Magnus Effect on Spinning Missiles¶

    A spinning missile with body roll rate \(p\) experiences a side force perpendicular to both the spin axis and the velocity vector due to the Magnus effect. Magnus side-force coefficient:

    \[C_{Y,Magnus} = C_{Y,p\alpha}\,\frac{p\,D}{2V}\,\alpha\tag{18.29}\]

    where \(C_{Y,p\alpha}\) is the Magnus coefficient derivative (\(\approx\)0.01–0.1 per rad\(^2\)). For high spin rates (\(p D/2V > 0.1\)), Magnus deflection can exceed guidance accuracy budgets; guided missiles minimise spin rate.

    18.5 Transonic Aerodynamics¶

    18.5.1 Wave Drag Rise and Critical Mach Number¶

    As free-stream Mach number \(Ma_\infty\) increases toward unity, local supersonic regions first appear above convex body surfaces when the local Mach number \(Ma_{local} = 1.0\). This defines the critical Mach number \(Ma_{cr}\):

    \[C_p^{cr} = \frac{2}{\gamma Ma_\infty^2}\!\left[\left(\frac{2 + (\gamma-1)Ma_\infty^2}{\gamma+1}\right)^{\gamma/(\gamma-1)} - 1\right]\tag{18.30}\]

    Beyond \(Ma_{cr}\), wave drag rises sharply (Prandtl–Glauert singularity):

    \[C_L = \frac{C_{L,incomp}}{\sqrt{1 - Ma_\infty^2}} \quad (Ma < Ma_{cr})\tag{18.31}\]

    This relation breaks down at \(Ma_{cr}\) and above as transonic nonlinearities dominate.

    18.5.2 Whitcomb Area Rule¶

    The transonic wave drag of a body depends only on its axial cross-sectional area distribution \(A(x)\), not on the detailed shape. Minimum wave drag in transonic flight is achieved when the \(A(x)\) distribution follows the Sears–Haack profile. Area ruling (Whitcomb, 1952) requires the total cross-section (body + fins + any protuberances) to follow a smooth Sears–Haack-like curve:

    \[A_{SR}(x) = \frac{4V}{3L}\sin^3\phi, \quad \phi = \arccos\!\left(1 - \frac{2x}{L}\right)\tag{18.32}\]

    Fins located at the aft end of the missile body satisfy this naturally (fins add area at the widest body point); interference with the boattail region requires careful integration.

    18.5.3 Drag Coefficient vs. Mach Number¶

    Table 18.1. Typical Missile \(C_{D0}\) Variation with Mach Number (tangent-ogive body, 4 delta fins)
    \(Ma_\infty\) 0.3 0.6 0.85 0.95 1.0 1.2 2.0 3.0
    \(C_{D0}\) 0.32 0.34 0.40 0.58 0.80 0.72 0.55 0.48
    \(C_{D,wave}\) 0 0 0.05 0.22 0.44 0.37 0.20 0.13
    \(C_{D,base}\) 0.18 0.19 0.21 0.22 0.22 0.19 0.12 0.09
    \(C_{D,frict}\) 0.14 0.15 0.14 0.14 0.14 0.16 0.23 0.26

    The peak drag occurs at Mach \(\approx\)1.0 due to strong bow shock formation and high base pressure differential. Drag in the Mach 0.9–1.1 “transonic bucket” must be computed with CFD or wind tunnel data; linear theory is invalid.

    18.5.4 Shock–Boundary-Layer Interaction (SBLI)¶

    At transonic and supersonic speeds, impingement of an oblique shock on a turbulent boundary layer causes flow separation, unsteady pressure loads, and heat flux augmentation. The interaction length \(L_{int}\) scales with:

    \[L_{int} \approx 6\,\delta\,\sqrt{\frac{p_2/p_1 - 1}{C_f}}\tag{18.33}\]

    where \(\delta\) is boundary layer thickness, \(p_2/p_1\) is shock pressure ratio, and \(C_f\) is local skin-friction coefficient. SBLI-induced pressure fluctuations can excite structural resonances (buffet) at frequencies of 50–500 Hz in the transonic regime; this must be assessed in aeroelastic analyses for thin-walled missile structures.

    18.6 Supersonic Aerodynamics¶

    18.6.1 Linear Wave Drag Theory (Ackeret)¶

    For a 2D thin profile at angle of attack \(\alpha\) in supersonic flow (\(Ma > 1\)), Ackeret’s linearised theory gives:

    \[C_{D,wave,2D} = \frac{4\alpha^2}{\sqrt{Ma^2 - 1}} + \frac{4(t/c)^2}{\sqrt{Ma^2 - 1}}\tag{18.34}\]
    \[C_{L,2D} = \frac{4\alpha}{\sqrt{Ma^2 - 1}}\tag{18.35}\]

    where \(t/c\) is thickness-to-chord ratio. These equations are valid for \(0.5 < \alpha < 8°\) and \(Ma > 1.3\) (outside the transonic regime).

    18.6.2 Oblique Shock Relations¶

    An oblique shock wave at shock angle \(\beta\) relative to the incoming flow deflects the flow by turning angle \(\theta\). The \(\theta\)–\(\beta\)–\(M\) relation:

    \[\tan\theta = \frac{2\cot\beta(Ma_1^2\sin^2\beta - 1)}{Ma_1^2(\gamma + \cos 2\beta) + 2}\tag{18.36}\]

    Post-shock Mach number:

    \[Ma_2^2 = \frac{Ma_1^2\sin^2\beta(\gamma-1)/2 + 1}{\sin^2(\beta-\theta)[\gamma Ma_1^2\sin^2\beta - (\gamma-1)/2]}\tag{18.37}\]

    Post-shock pressure ratio:

    \[\frac{p_2}{p_1} = 1 + \frac{2\gamma}{\gamma+1}(Ma_1^2\sin^2\beta - 1)\tag{18.38}\]

    For a 15° cone at Mach 3: \(\beta \approx 32°\) from cone chart; \(p_2/p_1 \approx 2.8\); this pressure acts on the windward side of control fins.

    18.6.3 Prandtl–Meyer Expansion¶

    Flow accelerates around a convex corner through a Prandtl–Meyer expansion fan. The Prandtl–Meyer function:

    \[\nu(Ma) = \sqrt{\frac{\gamma+1}{\gamma-1}}\arctan\sqrt{\frac{\gamma-1}{\gamma+1}(Ma^2-1)} - \arctan\sqrt{Ma^2-1}\tag{18.39}\]

    Flow turning angle \(\Delta\theta = \nu(Ma_2) - \nu(Ma_1)\). The expansion fan is isentropic; pressure drops below the incident pressure, producing suction on the leeward surface. Combined oblique shock (windward) + expansion fan (leeward) gives the total pressure force on a surface at AoA, providing a more accurate supersonic force prediction than linear theory for \(\alpha > 5°\).

    18.6.4 Supersonic Fin Normal Force — Diederich Method¶

    For low-aspect-ratio fins (\(AR < 3\)) at supersonic speeds:

    \[C_{N\alpha,fin}^{3D} = C_{N\alpha,fin}^{2D} \cdot \frac{AR}{AR + 2\cos\Lambda_{LE}/\sqrt{Ma^2-1}}\tag{18.40}\]

    where \(\Lambda_{LE}\) is the leading-edge sweep angle. For a delta fin with \(AR = 1\), \(\Lambda_{LE} = 60°\), \(Ma = 2\): \(C_{N\alpha,2D} = 4/\sqrt{3} = 2.31\); correction factor \(= 1/(1 + 2 \times 0.5/1.732) = 0.634\); \(C_{N\alpha,3D} = 1.46\) rad\(^{-1}\).

    18.6.5 Blunt vs. Sharp Nose: Aerodynamic Trade¶

    A blunt nose (hemisphere or large-radius sphere) generates a strong detached bow shock at supersonic speeds, resulting in:

    • High nose wave drag: \(C_{D,blunt} \approx 0.85\)–\(1.0\) (reference area = nose frontal area) vs. \(C_{D,sharp} \approx 0.15\)–\(0.25\).

    • Low aerodynamic heating at the stagnation point (larger \(R_N \to\) lower \(\dot{q}_s\) per Eq. (17.6)).

    • Forward centre of pressure \(x_{CP,nose}\) (blunt nose CP at \(\approx 0.25 D\) from tip vs. \(0.67 L_n\) for tangent ogive).

    In ballistic re-entry vehicle design, a blunt nose is deliberately chosen to reduce heat flux at the expense of drag; in an aerodynamic cruise missile, a sharp nose is preferred for range efficiency.

    18.7 Hypersonic Aerodynamics¶

    18.7.1 Flow Regime Definitions¶

    Hypersonic flow (\(Ma > 5\)) is characterised by:

    1. Thin shock layers (shock stand-off distance decreases as \(1/Ma^2\)).

    2. Merged shock and boundary layer at very high Mach numbers.

    3. High-temperature real-gas effects: vibrational excitation (\(T > 800\) K), oxygen dissociation (\(T > 2{,}500\) K), nitrogen dissociation (\(T > 4{,}000\) K), ionisation (\(T > 9{,}000\) K).

    4. Entropy layer: strong vorticity in the hot gas between the bow shock and the body.

    5. Viscous interaction: thick boundary layer modifies the external inviscid flow.

    Table 18.2. Thermochemical Excitation Regimes in Hypersonic Flow (Air)

    800–2,500 Vibrational excitation; \(\gamma \to 1.3\) Ma 4–8 \(C_m\), \(C_N\) deviation \(<\)10%
    2,500–4,000 O\(_2\) dissociation; \(\gamma \to 1.2\) Ma 8–12 \(C_D\) up 15%; shock layer thicker
    4,000–9,000 N\(_2\) dissociation + NO formation; \(\gamma \to 1.15\) Ma 12–18 Significant \(C_m\) shift

    18.7.2 Newtonian Impact Theory¶

    In the limit \(Ma \to \infty\), the pressure coefficient on a surface inclined at angle \(\delta\) to the free-stream:

    \[C_p = 2\sin^2\delta\tag{18.41}\]

    Modified Newtonian theory (Lees, 1955) replaces the factor 2 with the stagnation-point value \(C_{p,max}\):

    \[C_p = C_{p,max}\sin^2\delta\tag{18.42}\]

    where:

    \[C_{p,max} = \frac{2}{\gamma Ma_\infty^2}\left[\left(\frac{(\gamma+1)^2 Ma_\infty^2}{4\gamma Ma_\infty^2 - 2(\gamma-1)}\right)^{\gamma/(\gamma-1)}\frac{1-\gamma+2\gamma Ma_\infty^2}{\gamma+1} - 1\right]\tag{18.43}\]

    For \(Ma = 20\), \(\gamma = 1.4\): \(C_{p,max} = 1.84\). Modified Newtonian gives \(<\)15% error in normal force for most missile geometries at \(Ma > 7\).

    18.7.3 Mach Number Independence¶

    At \(Ma \gg 1\), aerodynamic force coefficients approach limiting Mach-independent values. The governing parameter is the hypersonic similarity parameter \(K = Ma\,\sin\alpha\); coefficients expressed as functions of \(K\) collapse to a single curve. This is the basis of the hypersonic equivalence principle. Practically, for \(Ma > 10\)–\(15\), \(C_N\) and \(C_m\) change by \(<\)5% with further Mach increase; aerodynamic databases need not extend beyond Mach 15–20.

    18.7.4 Aerodynamic Heating — Stagnation Point¶

    The Sutton–Graves correlation for stagnation heat flux (Eq. (17.6)) applies for \(Ma < 20\) in a standard atmosphere. For a sharper nose (small \(R_N\)), the Fay–Riddell correlation provides higher fidelity:

    \[\dot{q}_{FR} = 0.763\,\Pr^{-0.6}\left(\rho_w\mu_w\right)^{0.1}\left(\rho_e\mu_e\right)^{0.4}\sqrt{\frac{du_e}{dx}}\,(h_{aw} - h_w)\tag{18.44}\]

    where \(h_{aw} = h_e + r\,V_e^2/2\) is the adiabatic-wall enthalpy (\(r \approx Pr^{0.5}\) for turbulent flow), and the velocity gradient \(du_e/dx \approx \sqrt{2(p_s - p_\infty)/(\rho_s R_N^2)}\).

    For a sharp cone half-angle \(\theta_c\) (Lees’ formula):

    \[\frac{\dot{q}_{cone}}{\dot{q}_{stag}} = \left(\frac{R_{N,sphere}}{R_{cone}}\right)^{0.5}\left(\frac{\sin^2\theta_c}{1 - \cos^3\theta_c}\right)^{0.5}\tag{18.45}\]

    A sharp nose reduces stagnation heating but increases total heat load over the frustum.

    18.7.5 Flat-Face and Leading-Edge Heating¶

    On a flat-face RV (blunt re-entry), the total heat-load integral:

    \[Q_{total} = \int_0^{t_f} \dot{q}_s \cdot A_{stag} \cdot dt\tag{18.46}\]

    For a Mk-12A RV (approx.): \(\dot{q}_{peak} \approx 10\) MW/m\(^2\) at 60 km altitude; integrated \(Q \approx 200\) MJ/m\(^2\) over 50 s. An ablative phenolic-nylon TPS at \(\Delta H_{abl} = 20\) MJ/kg ablates at \(\dot{m}_{abl} = \dot{q}_s/\Delta H_{abl} = 0.5\) kg/(m\(^2\cdot\)s) at peak heating; total mass loss \(\approx 25\) kg/m\(^2\) over the trajectory, equivalent to 25 mm thickness reduction.

    18.8 Control Surface Aerodynamics¶

    18.8.1 Fin Deflection and Hinge Moment¶

    When a control fin is deflected by angle \(\delta\), it generates additional normal force and a hinge moment about the pivot. Control normal-force increment:

    \[\Delta C_N = C_{N\delta}\,\delta = \frac{\partial C_N}{\partial\delta}\,\delta\tag{18.47}\]

    The hinge moment coefficient about the fin pivot:

    \[C_H = C_{H\alpha}\,\alpha + C_{H\delta}\,\delta\tag{18.48}\]

    where \(C_{H\alpha}\) and \(C_{H\delta}\) are the hinge-moment derivatives with AoA and deflection. Both terms are typically negative (\(C_{H\alpha} < 0\), \(C_{H\delta} < 0\)), meaning the aerodynamic moment resists the commanded deflection. Actuator sizing must provide torque at least 3\(\times\) the peak hinge moment (including safety margins and structural flexibility effects).

    18.8.2 Control Effectiveness and Authority¶

    Control effectiveness degrades in the transonic regime (Mach 0.8–1.2) due to shock formation on the fin and shock-induced separation. The effective control derivative:

    \[\left(\frac{\partial C_m}{\partial\delta}\right)_{eff} = \frac{\partial C_m}{\partial\delta}\cdot \eta_{eff}(Ma)\tag{18.49}\]

    where \(\eta_{eff}(Ma)\) drops to \(\approx\)0.6–0.7 in the transonic region and recovers to \(\approx\)0.85–0.95 at Mach 1.5+. At high AoA (\(\alpha > 20°\)), vortex shedding from the forebody can produce asymmetric rolling moments exceeding the control authority; cross-coupling corrections are required in the autopilot.

    18.8.3 Canard vs. Tail Configuration¶

    Canard control.

    Canards deflect ahead of the CG; their downwash impinges on the tail fins, reducing tail-fin effectiveness. Net pitching moment is:

    \[C_m = C_{N,canard}\,\frac{x_{CG} - x_{canard}}{D} - C_{N,tail}\,\frac{x_{tail} - x_{CG}}{D}\tag{18.50}\]

    Canard advantage: positive lift increment (canard + tail both contribute to \(L\)); faster pitch response. Canard disadvantage: downwash reduces tail effectiveness; roll–yaw cross-coupling; difficult to balance at high AoA.

    Tail control.

    Deflecting aft tail fins generates a pitching moment about the CG; the fin itself creates negative lift (sign convention dependent). Tail-controlled missiles have better roll symmetry and are easier to stabilise; most medium-range and long-range missiles use tail control.

    All-moveable tail (AMT).

    The entire fin pivots (no fixed surface + flap); control effectiveness is \(\approx\)2–3\(\times\) higher than a plain flap on a fixed fin for the same deflection angle because the full fin area develops normal force in the commanded direction rather than just the flap portion. Hinge moments are also lower for AMT.

    18.8.4 Thrust Vector Control (TVC) and Lateral Jets¶

    When a missile is at low dynamic pressure (shortly after launch, or post-burnout), aerodynamic control surfaces are ineffective. Two alternative control actuators:

    1. Thrust Vector Control (TVC): deflects the nozzle using a gimballed nozzle mechanism, jet vanes, or liquid injection into the nozzle. Control force proportional to thrust: \(F_{TVC} = T\sin(\delta_{TVC}) \approx T\delta_{TVC}\). Jet vanes (tungsten, molybdenum) immersed in the exhaust plume produce side force but add \(\approx\)3–8% thrust loss.

    2. Reaction Control System (RCS) / Lateral Jets: Cold-gas or hot-gas thruster perpendicular to the missile axis; pure torque (no velocity dependence); used on KKVs and on short-burn missiles. Interaction force between lateral jet and free-stream creates a jet-in-crossflow amplification factor \(K_{jet} \approx 1.5\)–\(4.0\):

      \[F_{total} = F_{jet}\,(1 + K_{jet})\,(q_\infty/q_{jet})^{k}\tag{18.51}\]

    18.9 Aerodynamic Databases and Computational Methods¶

    18.9.1 DATCOM and Missile DATCOM¶

    The USAF Digital DATCOM (Data Compendium) is a semi-empirical handbook method for rapid aerodynamic estimation of conventional aircraft and missile configurations. Missile DATCOM (McDonnell Douglas, 1979; AFRL update 2005) extends the method explicitly for:

    • Body-of-revolution geometries (ogive, cone, cylinder, boattail, flare).

    • Fin/wing components (rectangular, trapezoidal, delta; 3 or 4 fin sets).

    • Mach 0.0–5.0; angle of attack 0–180° (in some modules).

    • Force/moment coefficients, stability derivatives, hinge moments, roll damping.

    Accuracy: \(\pm 15\)% on \(C_N\), \(\pm 10\)% on \(x_{CP}\) for conventional configurations; less accurate for manoeuvring bodies at \(\alpha > 15°\). Run time: seconds on a workstation; invaluable for parametric trade studies during preliminary design.

    18.9.2 Euler CFD¶

    Euler methods solve the inviscid form of the Navier–Stokes equations, capturing wave drag, pressure distributions, and shock patterns without resolving the boundary layer. Typical specification:

    • Grid: 2–10 million cells (structured or unstructured).

    • Solver: RANS without viscous terms (inviscid flux only); density-based; Roe scheme.

    • Time-to-solution: 1–4 hours on 32 CPU cores for a supersonic missile at fixed \(\alpha\).

    • Accuracy: wave drag \(\pm 5\)%, \(C_N \pm 8\)%, \(x_{CP} \pm 3\)% relative to Euler-accurate solution; viscous effects (friction drag, separation) not captured.

    18.9.3 RANS CFD¶

    Reynolds-Averaged Navier–Stokes (RANS) with \(k\)–\(\omega\) SST turbulence model provides full aerodynamic coefficient prediction including viscous effects. Specification:

    • Grid: 10–50 million cells; wall \(y^+ \approx 1\) for SST; prism layers 20–30 cells deep.

    • Solver: segregated pressure-based (low-speed); density-based coupled (supersonic).

    • Time-to-solution: 12–96 hours on 256 CPU cores for a complete missile geometry at one flight condition; full Mach/AoA polar requires \(\approx\)500 CPU-hours.

    • Accuracy: \(C_D \pm 5\)%, \(C_N \pm 5\)%, \(C_m \pm 8\)% vs. wind tunnel for attached flow; degrades for massively separated flows (\(\alpha > 20°\)).

    18.9.4 DSMC for Rarefied Flow¶

    At altitudes above \(\approx\)80 km (free-molecular and transitional regime), continuum CFD fails. The Direct Simulation Monte Carlo (DSMC) method tracks representative molecular populations:

    \[f(x, v, t) \to \text{stochastic particle collisions} \quad \text{(Bird, 1994)}\tag{18.52}\]

    DSMC is essential for KKV homing at 150+ km altitude where mean free path \(\lambda_f > 1\) mm and Knudsen number \(Kn = \lambda_f/L > 0.01\). At 200 km altitude, \(\rho \approx 2.5\times10^{-10}\) kg/m\(^3\) and aerodynamic forces are 6 orders of magnitude lower than at sea level; DACS thrusters provide essentially all control authority.

    18.9.5 Wind Tunnel Testing¶

    Wind-tunnel testing remains the authoritative validation method for missile aerodynamic databases. Relevant facility types:

    • Transonic blow-down tunnel (0.5 \(< Ma <\) 1.5): schlieren photography for shock visualisation; 6-component strain-gauge balance for forces and moments; Mach 0.85–1.3 most valuable; AEDC 16T, ONERA S1MA.

    • Supersonic blow-down tunnel (1.5 \(< Ma <\) 6): NASA 9\(\times\)7 Foot Supersonic Wind Tunnel; model scale 1:5 to 1:15; \(Re\) matching to \(\pm\)20%.

    • Ballistic range: missile model fired through instrumented range; multiple laser shadowgraphs at Mach 2–10; free-flight dynamics; no sting interference; AEDC G-Range.

    • Shock tunnel (Mach 8–25, \(T_{stag}\) to 20,000 K; \(\tau_{test} = 1\)–\(50\) ms): Caltech T5, DLR HEG; heat-flux measurement with thin-film gauges; DSMC validation.

    18.10 Aerodynamic Heating and Thermal Protection¶

    18.10.1 Heat Flux Distribution¶

    At high Mach numbers, heat flux varies strongly over the missile surface. At the stagnation point (Eq. (17.6)); on the conical frustum (Eckert’s reference enthalpy method for turbulent flat plate, mapped to cone surface):

    \[\dot{q}_{cone} = C_f^*\,\frac{\rho^* V_e^3}{2}\tag{18.53}\]

    where the starred quantities are evaluated at a reference temperature \(T^* = 0.5T_w + 0.22T_{aw} + 0.28T_e\). At leading edges of fins (radius \(R_{LE}\)):

    \[\dot{q}_{LE} = \dot{q}_{stag}\sqrt{\frac{R_N}{R_{LE}}}\tag{18.54}\]

    For a 1 mm leading-edge radius vs. a 20 mm nose radius: \(\dot{q}_{LE} = \dot{q}_{stag}\sqrt{20/1} = 4.47\,\dot{q}_{stag}\). Fin leading edges are the most thermally critical structural elements at Mach \(>\)4.

    18.10.3 Ablation Physics¶

    Ablative TPS materials cool by pyrolysis (polymer decomposition), sublimation, and char formation. The mass loss rate:

    \[\dot{m}_{abl} = \frac{\dot{q}_s - \dot{q}_{cond} - \sigma\varepsilon T_w^4}{\Delta H_{eff}}\tag{18.55}\]

    where \(\dot{q}_{cond}\) is heat conducted into the base structure, \(\sigma\varepsilon T_w^4\) is radiation re-emission, and \(\Delta H_{eff}\) is the effective enthalpy of ablation (pyrolysis + latent heat; \(\approx 5\)–\(30\) MJ/kg depending on material). The char layer formed by pyrolysis has very low thermal conductivity (\(k_{char} \approx 0.1\)–\(0.5\) W/(m\(\cdot\)K)) compared to the virgin material, providing additional insulation.

    18.10.4 Seeker Window Thermal Management¶

    The seeker dome (radome or optical window) must simultaneously:

    • Transmit electromagnetic (RF) or optical (IR/EO) radiation in the seeker band.

    • Withstand aerodynamic heating without melting, cracking, or losing transmissivity.

    • Maintain dimensional accuracy (\(<\)0.1 mm) to avoid aberrations in the seeker focal path.

    Sapphire dome at Mach 4 (\(V \approx 1{,}300\) m/s, 20 km altitude): surface temperature \(T_w \approx 500\)–\(800\)°C. Transmissivity in the MWIR (3–5 \(\mu\)m) band decreases from \(\approx\)84% at 25°C to \(\approx\)65% at 600°C. Active cooling (coolant circulation through the seeker dome housing) maintains \(T_w < 300\)°C at Mach 4. Silicon nitride dome at Mach 5: \(T_w \approx 1{,}100\)°C; transmissivity loss in Ku-band (13–18 GHz) RF seeker remains \(<\)2 dB up to 1,500°C.

    18.11 Flight Envelope and Manoeuvrability¶

    18.11.1 Load Factor and Structural Limit¶

    The aerodynamic load factor:

    \[n = \frac{L}{W} = \frac{q_\infty S_{ref} C_N}{W}\tag{18.56}\]

    Maximum structural load factor \(n_{max}\) is set by the airframe structural limit (typically 20–50\(g\) for SAMs, 10–25\(g\) for air-launched missiles, 5–10\(g\) for heavy land-attack cruise missiles). For a SAM with \(W = 300\) kg, \(S_{ref} = 0.05\) m\(^2\), \(C_{N,max} = 20\), \(q_\infty = 50\) kPa (Mach 4 at 12 km):

    \[n_{max} = \frac{50{,}000 \times 0.05 \times 20}{300 \times 9.81} = 17.0\,g\tag{18.57}\]

    18.11.2 Turn Radius¶

    The minimum turn radius at load factor \(n\):

    \[R_{turn} = \frac{V^2}{g\sqrt{n^2 - 1}}\tag{18.58}\]

    For \(V = 1{,}000\) m/s, \(n = 20\): \(R_{turn} = 10^6/(9.81\sqrt{399}) = 5{,}105\) m. This sets the intercept geometry: a target at \(R < R_{turn}\) cannot be reached without the missile executing a spiralling engagement.

    18.11.3 Maximum Lateral Acceleration¶

    Maximum lateral acceleration:

    \[a_{lat,max} = n_{max}\,g\tag{18.59}\]

    Typical values: MANPADS (\(20g\)); SHORAD SAM (\(30g\)); advanced SAM Aster 30 (\(50g\) with divert thrusters); ATGM (\(10g\)). The required lateral acceleration for a proportional navigation intercept of a \(5g\) manoeuvring target at \(t_{go} = 1\) s before intercept:

    \[a_{req} = N' \cdot a_{target} \cdot (N'-1) \cdot \frac{t_{go,0}}{t_{go}} \approx 4 \times 5g \approx 20g\tag{18.60}\]

    18.11.4 Dynamic Pressure Variation with Altitude¶

    Dynamic pressure at a given Mach number varies with altitude through air density:

    \[q_\infty = \frac{1}{2}\gamma p_\infty Ma^2 = \frac{1}{2}\gamma p_0\left(1 + \frac{\gamma-1}{2}Ma^2\right)^{-\gamma/(\gamma-1)}Ma^2\tag{18.61}\]

    At Mach 4:

    • Sea level: \(q = 0.5 \times 1.225 \times (4\times 343.3)^2 = 488\) kPa

    • 10 km: \(q = 0.5 \times 0.413 \times (4\times299.5)^2 = 148\) kPa

    • 20 km: \(q = 0.5 \times 0.088 \times (4\times295.1)^2 = 30.7\) kPa

    Available lateral acceleration at Mach 4 drops by \(148/488 = 0.30\) going from sea level to 10 km at the same Mach number (since \(a_{lat} \propto q_\infty\)). This is the fundamental reason why high-altitude engagements are harder for aerodynamically controlled interceptors; RCS/divert thrusters provide velocity-independent control.

    18.11.5 Aeroelastic Effects and Structural Coupling¶

    Bending of the missile body under aerodynamic load alters the seeker line-of-sight angle and the control surface effectiveness. The body bending angle for a free–free beam under distributed aerodynamic load:

    \[\theta_{bend}(x) = \int_0^x \frac{M_{bend}(\xi)}{EI(\xi)}\,d\xi\tag{18.62}\]

    For a seeker at the nose and rate gyros mid-body, body bending introduces a spurious guidance signal:

    \[\dot{\sigma}_{spurious} = \frac{d\theta_{bend}}{dt}\bigg|_{nose-gyro}\tag{18.63}\]

    This can destabilise the guidance loop if the structural bending frequency \(\omega_n\) is within one decade of the guidance bandwidth \(\omega_g\). Structural notch filters at \(\omega_n\) are standard in missile autopilot design.

    18.12 Summary: Aerodynamic Design Process¶

    The missile aerodynamic design process follows a structured progression:

    1. Mission analysis: define speed, altitude, range, and manoeuvre envelope \(\to\) flight conditions database.

    2. Preliminary sizing: Missile DATCOM to establish nose geometry, \(L/D\), fin configuration, and initial \(SM\) estimate.

    3. Stability analysis: verify \(C_{m\alpha} < 0\) across \(Ma\)-\(\alpha\) envelope; check \(SM\) vs. propellant burn-off CG travel.

    4. Control sizing: control surface \(C_{N\delta}\), hinge moment, actuator torque requirement, and effectiveness at transonic.

    5. Euler CFD: wave drag, shock impingement, fin pressure distribution at key Mach numbers (0.8, 1.2, 2, 4).

    6. RANS CFD: full aerodynamic database including friction drag, separated-flow AoA effects, actuator-on configurations.

    7. Thermal analysis: heat flux distribution from RANS; TPS thickness sizing; seeker dome temperature budget.

    8. Wind tunnel: validation of CFD at critical conditions; force and moment balance; schlieren shock visualisation; transition fixing.

    9. Aeroelastic assessment: structural mode frequencies vs. guidance bandwidth; notch filter design.

    10. Database integration: tabulated \(C_N\), \(C_A\), \(C_m\), \(C_{N\delta}\), \(C_{l\delta}\), etc. vs. (\(Ma\), \(\alpha\), \(\delta\), altitude) for 6-DoF simulation.

    The key aerodynamic parameters and their design targets for a notional medium-range SAM are summarised in Table 18.5.

    Table 18.5. Aerodynamic Design Parameter Summary — Notional Medium-Range SAM
    Parameter Symbol Design Target Rationale
    Static margin at Mach 2 \(SM\) 0.5–2.0 cal. Stable but responsive
    Pitching moment slope \(C_{m\alpha}\) \(< -0.5\) rad\(^{-1}\) Restoring
    Max normal force coefficient \(C_{N,max}\) 12–20 \(20g\) intercept
