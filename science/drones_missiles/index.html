    Preface¶

    “The history of aerial warfare is, in large measure, the history
    of the unceasing effort to remove the pilot from danger—
    and ultimately to remove the pilot altogether.”
    — Attributed to Theodore von Kármán, 1954

    Scope of This Book¶

    This reference volume grew from a straightforward observation: no single engineering text covers unmanned aerial vehicles and guided missiles with the depth, mathematical rigour, and cross-disciplinary breadth that practising engineers actually require. Monographs on UAV aerodynamics exist alongside separate treatments of missile guidance, propulsion system handbooks, and avionics references—but the practitioner who needs to understand how a loitering munition differs from a cruise missile, why the square-cube law constrains nano-drone battery endurance, or how a radar seeker trades range against cross-range resolution must presently assemble that understanding from dozens of disparate sources.

    This book attempts to provide that single, authoritative source. It covers the full technical breadth of unmanned systems—from sub-100-gram micro-air vehicles to intercontinental ballistic missiles—with consistent mathematical notation, SI units throughout, and engineering derivations carried to the level of detail needed for design work. Every major subsystem is treated: aerodynamics and flight mechanics, propulsion (chemical rocket, air-breathing, electric), guidance and navigation, control theory, seekers and sensors, warheads and fuzing, structures and materials, manufacturing, testing and evaluation, and the supporting digital infrastructure of ground control, communications, and autonomy.

    The scope is deliberately both historical and prospective. Understanding why the V-1 buzz bomb used an Argus pulsejet rather than a turbojet, or why early anti-radiation missiles homed on continuous-wave emitters, is not mere antiquarianism: it illuminates the engineering trade-offs that remain just as relevant today. Equally, chapters on swarm autonomy, hypersonic glide vehicles, and directed-energy warheads are included because these are the design spaces in which the next generation of engineers will work.

    The book does not address operational employment, tactics, or the legal and ethical dimensions of autonomous weapons, except insofar as regulatory frameworks constrain design choices. Those subjects deserve dedicated treatment by specialists in military doctrine, international humanitarian law, and strategic studies.

    Audience¶

    This reference is written for four overlapping communities:

    • Practising engineers and system designers who need a reliable, mathematically complete source from which to extract design equations, material properties, and subsystem specifications without returning to primary literature for every calculation.

    • Researchers in aeronautics, control engineering, robotics, materials science, and related disciplines who require precise cross-disciplinary context for their specialisations.

    • Defence and government professionals—programme managers, test directors, acquisition officers, intelligence analysts—who need sufficient technical depth to evaluate claims, interpret test data, and manage complex engineering programmes.

    • Graduate and advanced undergraduate students in aerospace, mechanical, and electrical engineering who are entering the field and need a comprehensive technical foundation alongside their course texts.

    The assumed background is a bachelor’s degree in engineering or physical science, including calculus through ordinary differential equations, undergraduate dynamics, thermodynamics, and introductory electronics. More advanced mathematical tools (Laplace transforms, state-space control theory, stochastic estimation) are introduced where needed with sufficient background that a diligent reader can follow the derivations.

    Organisation of Chapters¶

    The book is divided into eight parts.

    Part I: Foundations (Chapters 1–3) provides the historical, definitional, and physical foundations common to both drones and missiles. Chapter 1 (this chapter) surveys the history and taxonomy of unmanned systems. Chapter 2 reviews the aerodynamics and flight mechanics relevant to both domains. Chapter 3 covers the propulsion landscape from electric motors to solid-rocket motors to scramjets.

    Part II: Guidance, Navigation, and Control (Chapters 4–7) addresses inertial navigation, GNSS, seeker technologies (electro-optical, infrared, radar, laser), guidance laws from proportional navigation to optimal and AI-derived methods, and autopilot and flight-control system design.

    Part III: Drone Systems (Chapters 8–14) treats fixed-wing, rotary-wing, multi-rotor, hybrid VTOL, and unconventional drone configurations; power systems including batteries, fuel cells, and solar arrays; avionics and communication architectures; payloads; and software and autonomy including AI/ML approaches to mission planning, perception, and swarming.

    Part IV: Missile Systems (Chapters 15–22) covers the major missile categories—ballistic, cruise, hypersonic, surface-to-air, air-to-air, anti-tank, anti-ship, anti-satellite, and loitering munitions—with individual chapters on rocket propulsion, warheads, fuzing, and thermal protection for atmospheric re-entry.

    Part V: Structures, Materials, and Manufacturing (Chapters 23–26) addresses airframe structural design, composite materials and manufacturing, stealth shaping and materials, and the emerging role of additive manufacturing.

    Part VI: Sensors and Electronics (Chapters 27–30) covers radar, electro-optical and infrared sensors, electronic warfare, and embedded computing and data links.

    Part VII: Ground Systems and Integration (Chapters 31–34) treats ground control stations, launch and recovery systems, mission planning, and system integration and test.

    Part VIII: Testing, Evaluation, and Certification (Chapters 35–38) addresses flight test methodology, ground test facilities, airworthiness certification for civil UAVs, and military qualification standards.

    Appendices provide physical constants and conversion factors, standard atmospheres, material property tables, a glossary of abbreviations, and a bibliography organised by subject.

    How to Use This Reference¶

    Readers with a specific design problem should begin with the relevant chapter and follow cross-references to subsystem chapters as needed. The index is comprehensive and includes equation numbers for key relationships so that a formula can be located without reading surrounding prose.

    Those approaching the field for the first time should read Part I in sequence before selecting topics of interest. The mathematical notation is established in Chapter 2 and used consistently throughout; a notation summary is provided at the front of the book.

    Each chapter opens with a summary of key results and closes with worked examples and problem sets suitable for graduate courses. Equations are numbered within chapters (e.g., Eq. 3.17 refers to the seventeenth numbered equation in Chapter 3). Tables and figures are similarly numbered.

    Conventions, Notation, and Units¶

    Units. The International System of Units (SI) is used throughout. US customary equivalents are given in parentheses where they commonly appear in the literature (e.g., thrust in lbf, range in nautical miles, altitude in feet). Conversions are exact where the SI definition permits; elsewhere, four significant figures are retained.

    Notation. Scalars are set in italic roman type (\(m\), \(V\), \(T\)). Vectors are bold italic (\(\boldsymbol{r}\), \(\boldsymbol{v}\), \(\boldsymbol{F}\)). Matrices are bold upright (\(\mathbf{A}\), \(\mathbf{Q}\), \(\mathbf{R}\)). Unit vectors carry a hat (\(\hat{\boldsymbol{e}}_x\)). Time derivatives are written with Newton’s dot notation (\(\dot{x}\), \(\ddot{x}\)) for ordinary differential equations and with \(\partial/\partial t\) for partial derivatives. The nabla operator is \(\nabla\). Dimensional quantities are always given with their units; dimensionless coefficients (\(C_L\), \(C_D\), \(M\)) are identified explicitly.

    Coordinate frames. Body, wind, stability, North-East-Down (NED), and Earth-Centred Earth-Fixed (ECEF) frames are defined formally in Chapter 2 and used consistently. Rotations follow the right-hand rule; Euler angles follow the aerospace sequence: yaw (\(\psi\)), pitch (\(\theta\)), roll (\(\phi\)).

    Abbreviations. A full glossary appears in Appendix E. Common acronyms (UAV, GPS, INS, TVC) are defined at first use in each chapter so that chapters may be read independently.

    Significant figures. Derived results are generally quoted to three or four significant figures consistent with the accuracy of the underlying models. Physical constants are quoted to the precision of the 2018 CODATA recommended values.
