    Chapter 28: Missile Manufacturing¶

    28.1 Missile Manufacturing Overview¶

    Missile manufacturing occupies a unique niche in the broader aerospace and defence manufacturing landscape. Unlike commercial aircraft production, which is governed primarily by structural airworthiness and schedule-driven economics, missile production integrates energetic materials, classified electronic payloads, and precision guidance assemblies under a unified, tightly regulated quality regime. The convergence of explosive components with advanced composites, microelectronics, and precision machining creates a manufacturing environment found nowhere else in industry.

    28.1.1 Fundamental Differences from Aircraft Manufacturing¶

    Several characteristics sharply distinguish missile manufacturing from conventional aerospace production.

    Energetic materials. Solid-propellant rocket motors and warheads contain high explosives (HE) and deflagrating propellants that are present throughout much of the assembly sequence. This imposes continuous electrostatic-discharge (ESD) control, no-lone-zone procedures, and personnel limits in process cells. Aircraft fuelling and armament loading are transient events; in a missile factory they are central, long-duration processes.

    HERO and HERF hazards. Hazards of Electromagnetic Radiation to Ordnance (HERO) and Hazards of Electromagnetic Radiation to Fuel (HERF) require that radio-frequency emitters—including portable radios, radar, and cellular devices—be excluded from explosive areas. MIL-STD-464C establishes susceptibility limits; missile factories implement controlled electromagnetic environments (CEME) with building shielding, door interlocks, and RF-absorbing panels.

    Classified processes. Guidance-system assembly, propellant formulations, and seeker algorithms are classified at various levels (CUI, SECRET, or above). Facilities must hold the appropriate facility security clearances (FSC) and implement SCIF-level protections for the most sensitive operations.

    Build sequence dependency. The order of assembly is dictated as much by explosive-safety logic as by mechanical assembly logic. Propellant is loaded before the warhead is integrated; the fuze is armed only in the final stage. This means production cannot be freely re-sequenced to optimise flow as is common in lean aircraft manufacturing.

    28.1.2 Facility Requirements¶

    Facilities that manufacture or handle explosives in the United States must be licensed by the Bureau of Alcohol, Tobacco, Firearms and Explosives (ATF) and comply with the DoD Explosives Safety Board (DDESB) standard DoD 6055.09-STD. Key physical requirements include:

    • Blast-resistant construction: Explosive-process buildings use reinforced-concrete walls (typically 200–400 mm) or earth-bermed structures with frangible roofs designed to vent overpressure upward.

    • Grounding and bonding: All surfaces, personnel, and tooling in explosive areas must be bonded to a common ground. Floor resistivity is maintained at \(10^{4}\)–\(10^{8}\,\Omega\) per MIL-STD-1495.

    • Quantity-distance (QD) siting: Buildings are sited so that the maximum credible event (MCE) yield does not damage inhabited structures. The inhibited weight of explosives (IWE) drives minimum inter-magazine distances per DDESB Table 6-3.

    • Environmental controls: Temperature and relative humidity must be controlled during propellant mixing, casting, and curing (\(\pm 2\,^\circ\)C, \(\pm 5\%\) RH) to ensure reproducible mechanical properties in the cured grain.

    • Ventilation: Explosive vapour concentrations must remain below 25% of the lower explosive limit (LEL) measured continuously by catalytic-bead or photoionisation detectors.

    28.1.3 Quality Management and Regulatory Compliance¶

    AS9100 Rev D is the aerospace quality management standard applied to missile manufacturing. It extends ISO 9001 with requirements for configuration management, risk management, and first-article inspection (FAI) per AS9102. Military contracts typically invoke MIL-Q-9858A (superseded but still referenced in legacy contracts) or AQAP-2110 for NATO programs.

    ITAR compliance. The International Traffic in Arms Regulations (22 CFR Parts 120–130) control the export of defence articles listed on the US Munitions List (USML). Missile systems, components, technical data, and manufacturing equipment are typically USML Category IV. Foreign nationals may not access ITAR data without a Department of State licence or applicable exemption (e.g., 22 CFR §126.18 for bona fide employees). Violations carry criminal penalties up to $1 million per violation and 20 years imprisonment.

    Government-owned, contractor-operated (GOCO) facilities. Because the capital investment for explosive-loading and large-motor-test facilities is enormous and the demand is episodic, the US government owns many propellant-production and warhead-filling facilities while contractors operate them under management and operating (M&O) contracts. Examples include the Holston Army Ammunition Plant (RDX and HMX production), the Radford Army Ammunition Plant (nitrocellulose and double- base propellant), and the McAlester Army Ammunition Plant (warhead assembly).

    28.1.4 Build Sequence¶

    A representative build sequence for a tactical air-to-ground missile is:

    1. Motor case fabrication (composite winding or metal forging)

    2. Liner application and propellant casting

    3. Propellant cure and motor inspection

    4. Nozzle assembly and attachment

    5. Aft section assembly (fins, control actuators)

    6. Warhead loading (explosive fill, fuze pocket machining)

    7. Guidance section assembly (IMU, electronics, seeker)

    8. Mid-body integration (warhead to guidance section)

    9. Forward section mating (seeker dome, radome, or window)

    10. Final assembly (motor + warhead + guidance)

    11. Acceptance testing (electrical, BIT, mass properties)

    12. Packaging, proof of acceptance, transfer to storage or fleet

    28.2 Airframe Manufacturing¶

    28.2.1 Metal Airframe Fabrication¶

    Aluminium alloys (7075-T6, 2024-T3), titanium alloys (Ti-6Al-4V), and high-strength steels (4340, 17-4PH) are the primary metallic airframe materials. Material selection is driven by the thermal environment, structural loads, and compatibility with propellants and explosives.

    Spin-forming of nose cones. Ogival and conical nose cones are produced by power spinning (shear spinning) aluminium or titanium blanks over a mandrel. Wall thickness tolerance is \(\pm 0.15\,\text{mm}\); the forming operation work-hardens the alloy approximately 15–20% beyond the base temper. Post-forming heat treatment restores the design temper. CNC-controlled spinning machines apply forces up to 200 kN at feed rates of 0.1–0.5 mm/rev.

    Machined rings and bulkheads. Structural ring frames that join body sections are turned from forged billets. Key dimensions:

    \[\sigma_\text{hoop} = \frac{p \cdot r}{t}\tag{28.1}\]

    where \(p\) is internal pressure (motor burnout), \(r\) is mean radius, and \(t\) is wall thickness. For a 150 mm diameter motor case at 10 MPa with \(t = 3\) mm:

    \[\sigma_\text{hoop} = \frac{10 \times 10^6 \times 0.075}{0.003} = 250\,\text{MPa}\tag{28.2}\]

    which is within the yield strength of 7075-T6 (\(\sigma_y = 503\,\text{MPa}\)) with a factor of safety of 2.0.

    Tolerances. Critical dimensional tolerances for missile airframe components are much tighter than those for aircraft structures due to the need for aerodynamic consistency and inter-lot interchangeability:

    Table 28.1. Representative airframe dimensional tolerances
    Feature Tolerance Method
    Airframe OD \(\pm 0.10\,\text{mm}\) CMM / air gauge
    Fin attachment slot \(\pm 0.05\,\text{mm}\) CMM
    Nozzle bore \(\pm 0.025\,\text{mm}\) Air plug gauge
    Nose cone concentricity \(0.05\,\text{mm TIR}\) CMM
    Section joint face flatness \(0.025\,\text{mm}\) Surface plate

    28.2.2 Composite Airframe Fabrication¶

    Composite missile bodies offer significant weight savings (15–30% versus aluminium) and can be tailored for aeroelastic stiffness. Carbon-fibre-reinforced polymer (CFRP) and glass-CFRP hybrids are typical.

    Filament winding. A CNC winding machine deposits resin-impregnated (wet winding) or pre-impregnated (dry towpreg) fibres over a mandrel at prescribed angles. The winding angle \(\alpha\) controls the axial-to-hoop stiffness ratio. For a closed-end pressure vessel the netting-analysis optimum winding angle is:

    \[\tan^2\alpha = \frac{N_\phi}{N_\theta}\tag{28.3}\]

    where \(N_\phi\) is the meridional stress resultant and \(N_\theta\) is the hoop stress resultant. For a cylindrical section under axial and pressure loads, \(\alpha = 54.7°\) (the “magic angle”).

    Prepreg layup. Flat and complex-curved panels are built from unidirectional or woven prepreg plies placed in matched-metal or composite tooling. Ply sequence is typically \([0/\pm 45/90]_s\) to achieve quasi-isotropic stiffness. Autoclave cure at \(121°\)C/585 kPa (180°C/700 kPa for bismaleimide systems) achieves void contents below 1%.

    Surface finish and coating. Post-cure surfaces are lightly sanded to \(R_a = 0.8\)–\(1.6\,\mu\text{m}\) and primed with a MIL-PRF-23377 epoxy primer before application of a MIL-PRF-85285 polyurethane topcoat. The topcoat provides UV resistance, chemical resistance, and a controlled emissivity for thermal management.

    28.2.3 Joining Processes¶

    Electron beam welding (EBW). Thin-wall titanium sections (1–3 mm) are joined by EBW in vacuum (\(10^{-4}\) Pa). The narrow heat-affected zone (HAZ) (\(\le 1\,\text{mm}\)) preserves the base metal temper. Weld penetration depth \(d\) scales with beam power \(P\) and travel speed \(v\):

    \[d \propto \frac{P}{v \cdot \sqrt{k \rho c_p}}\tag{28.4}\]

    where \(k\) is thermal conductivity, \(\rho\) is density, and \(c_p\) is specific heat. Welds are 100% inspected by X-ray and dye-penetrant (PT) per AWS D17.1.

    TIG welding. Gas tungsten arc welding (GTAW/TIG) is used for thicker steel sections. Trailing gas shields protect titanium welds from oxidation. Preheat (150–200°C) is required for 4340 steel to prevent hydrogen embrittlement.

    28.3 Solid Propellant Manufacture¶

    28.3.1 Propellant Formulation and Ingredients¶

    Modern composite solid propellants are physically and chemically complex systems. The primary ingredients of a typical HTPB (hydroxyl-terminated polybutadiene) propellant are:

    Table 28.2. Representative HTPB propellant formulation
    Ingredient Function Mass fraction (%)
    HTPB (R-45M) Binder / fuel 10–14
    Isocyanate (IPDI) Cross-linking agent 1–3
    Ammonium perchlorate (AP) Oxidiser (coarse + fine) 65–72
    Aluminium powder (30–400 extmum) Metal fuel 16–20
    Bonding agent (MAPO/HX-880) AP–binder adhesion 0.1–0.5
    Stabiliser (MNA) Thermal stability 0.1–0.3
    Plasticiser (DOA) Flexibility at low-T 0–3
    Burn-rate catalyst (Fe2O3) Rate modifier 0–0.5

    The specific impulse \(I_{sp}\) of an HTPB/AP/Al propellant at optimum mixture ratio is approximately 260–275 s (sea level) and 280–295 s (vacuum). Theoretical specific impulse is computed from:

    \[I_{sp} = \frac{1}{g_0} \sqrt{\frac{2\gamma}{\gamma-1} \cdot \frac{R_u T_c}{M_w} \left[1 - \left(\frac{p_e}{p_c}\right)^{(\gamma-1)/\gamma}\right]}\tag{28.5}\]

    where \(\gamma\) is the product-gas ratio of specific heats (\(\approx 1.2\)), \(R_u\) is the universal gas constant, \(T_c\) is combustion temperature (typically 3,100–3,600 K), \(M_w\) is the mean molecular weight of combustion products, and \(p_e/p_c\) is the nozzle expansion ratio.

    28.3.2 Mixing Operations¶

    Propellant mixing is conducted in vertical-sigma-blade (VSB) or planetary mixers with vacuum capability. Typical production mixer sizes range from 11 litres (development) to 2,300 litres (production). Key process parameters:

    • Mixing sequence: Binder components (HTPB, plasticiser, bonding agent) are premixed; then coarse AP is added; then fine AP; aluminium is added last to minimise ignition risk.

    • Temperature: Maintained at 25–35°C; elevated temperature reduces viscosity but shortens pot life.

    • Vacuum: Vacuum (\(\le 10\,\text{kPa}\)) applied during mixing to remove entrapped air and residual moisture.

    • Pot life: The time from addition of isocyanate (cross-linker) to the point at which viscosity exceeds the castable limit is 4–8 hours. Pot life \(t_{pot}\) follows Arrhenius kinetics:

    \[t_{pot} = A \cdot \exp\!\left(\frac{E_a}{R_u T}\right)\tag{28.6}\]

    where \(E_a\) is the activation energy for gel formation (\(\approx 60\)–80 kJ/mol) and \(A\) is a pre-exponential factor. Reducing temperature from 35°C to 25°C approximately doubles pot life.

    28.3.3 Casting and Cure¶

    Propellant is transferred from the mixer to a casting vessel and cast into the motor case (which has been pre-lined and pre-heated to 40°C) via gravity or vacuum-assisted casting. A central mandrel displaces material to form the internal grain geometry (star, finocyl, etc.).

    The degree of cure \(\alpha_c\) is related to the isocyanate/hydroxyl (NCO/OH) ratio \(r\):

    \[\alpha_c = 1 - \exp(-k \cdot r \cdot t)\tag{28.7}\]

    Cure takes place in a temperature-controlled oven at 50–60°C for 3–7 days. The cross-linking reaction increases storage modulus \(E'\) from approximately 0.2 MPa (uncured) to 1–5 MPa (cured). Final Shore A hardness target is 30–60, depending on the grain design.

    The stress relaxation modulus is characterised by:

    \[E_r(t) = \frac{\sigma(t)}{\varepsilon_0}\tag{28.8}\]

    where \(\sigma(t)\) is the decaying stress at a fixed imposed strain \(\varepsilon_0\), measured in a relaxation test. This function determines the structural response of the grain to thermal cycling and pressure loading throughout storage life.

    28.3.4 Quality Control of Propellant¶

    X-ray radiography. 100% of production motors are X-rayed using linear accelerators or industrial X-ray sources to detect voids, cracks, and AP agglomerates. Minimum detectable void size is approximately 1.5 mm diameter. Acceptance criteria per drawing callout typically limit voids to \(\le 1.5\,\text{mm}\) in any dimension with no more than five per 300 mm length.

    Ultrasonic inspection. Pulse-echo or through-transmission ultrasonics detects liner/propellant bond-line disbonds. A disbond larger than 6 mm in any direction is a cause for rejection.

    Mechanical property coupons. Each mix is sampled; uniaxial tensile coupons (dogbone, per JANNAF test protocol) are cured alongside the production grain and tested at \(-40°C\), \(+25°C\), and \(+65°C\). Minimum required elongation is typically \(>25\%\) and tensile strength \(>0.8\,\text{MPa}\).

    Burn rate is measured on strand burner samples. The empirical Vieille burn-rate law:

    \[\dot{r} = a \cdot p^n\tag{28.9}\]

    gives burn rate \(\dot{r}\) (mm/s) as a function of chamber pressure \(p\) (MPa) and empirical constants \(a\) and \(n\) (pressure exponent, typically 0.3–0.4 for HTPB/AP).

    28.3.5 Minimum Signature Propellants¶

    Minimum-signature (reduced-smoke) propellants substitute RDX or HMX for part of the AP oxidiser and eliminate or reduce the aluminium fuel content. This produces a nearly transparent plume at the cost of lower \(I_{sp}\) (240–260 s). Double-base propellants (nitrocellulose + nitroglycerine) produce essentially no smoke but have lower \(I_{sp}\) and poorer mechanical properties. For infrared countermeasure reduction the plume temperature is also reduced.

    28.3.6 Explosive Safety in Propellant Manufacturing¶

    All propellant-manufacturing facilities are sited per DDESB 6055.09 quantity-distance (QD) requirements. The inhabited building distance (IBD) for exposed personnel is:

    \[D_{IBD} = K_D \cdot W^{1/3}\tag{28.10}\]

    where \(W\) is the net explosive weight (NEW) in kg, and \(K_D\) is the QD constant (18 for TNT-equivalent for the IBD in the US customary form, or 44.4 m/kg\(^{1/3}\) for high-explosive process buildings). Lightning protection is mandatory; a grounding grid with \(\le 10\,\Omega\) impedance must be demonstrated by earth-resistance testing.

    28.4 Warhead Manufacture¶

    28.4.1 Explosive Loading Processes¶

    Three primary processes are used to load explosive fills into warhead cases.

    Melt-pour loading. TNT and TNT-based compositions (Composition B, Octol) are melted at 82–95°C in steam-jacketed kettles and poured into the pre-warmed warhead case. Vibration during solidification (frequency 50–120 Hz) drives out trapped gas. Slow cooling (1–2°C/hr) prevents differential thermal cracking. Void acceptance criteria are defined per drawing; typical rejection limit is any void \(>3\,\text{mm}\) diameter revealed by X-ray.

    Press loading. Insensitive high explosives (IHE) such as TATB (triaminotrinitrobenzene) and PBXN formulations cannot be melt-poured; they are pressed isostatically at 50–200 MPa in rubber tooling (cold isostatic pressing, CIP). Pressed density \(\rho_p\) relative to theoretical maximum density (TMD) is a key quality parameter:

    \[\rho_\% = \frac{\rho_p}{\rho_{TMD}} \times 100\%\tag{28.11}\]

    Acceptance requires \(\rho_\% \ge 98\%\) TMD. Any cracks detected by X-ray or dye-penetrant constitute automatic rejection.

    Slurry casting. Water-slurry casting (used for PBXW formulations) disperses the explosive in an aqueous binder solution that is cast into the case and dried. This process allows very dense explosive charges at reduced ignition sensitivity.

    28.4.2 Shaped Charge Liner Manufacture¶

    The liner of a shaped charge controls the jet formation. Liner materials include electrolytic tough-pitch (ETP) copper, OFHC copper, tantalum, and steel. The liner profile (conical, bi-conical, hemispherical, trumpet) is machined to very tight tolerances:

    • Wall thickness uniformity: \(\pm 0.05\,\text{mm}\) (electroformed copper)

    • Surface roughness: \(R_a \le 0.4\,\mu\text{m}\) (turned); \(R_a \le 0.8\,\mu\text{m}\) (electroformed)

    • Apex eccentricity: \(\le 0.1\,\text{mm}\) TIR

    The penetration capability \(P\) of a shaped-charge jet into rolled homogeneous armour (RHA) is approximated by the Birkhoff formula:

    \[P = L_j \sqrt{\frac{\rho_j}{\rho_t}}\tag{28.12}\]

    where \(L_j\) is effective jet length (\(\approx 5\)–8 calibre for optimised designs), \(\rho_j\) is jet material density, and \(\rho_t\) is target density (\(7,850\,\text{kg/m}^3\) for RHA). For a copper jet (\(\rho_j = 8,960\,\text{kg/m}^3\)) penetrating steel, \(\sqrt{\rho_j/\rho_t} \approx 1.07\).

    Table 28.3. NDT methods applied to warhead assemblies
    Method Defects Detected Notes
    Industrial X-ray Voids, cracks, liner contact 100% of production units
    Gamma-ray densitometry Explosive density variation Collimated source; \(\pm 0.5\%\) density resolution

    28.4.4 Sensitivity Testing of Explosives¶

    Before and after loading, explosive materials are characterised by standard sensitiveness tests:

    • Drop-weight impact: Height at which 50% of samples initiate (DH50) per STANAG 4489. For TATB, DH50 \(> 320\,\text{cm}\) (32 J), versus \(< 20\,\text{cm}\) for primary explosives.

    • Friction test: Sliding friction apparatus; \(P_{50}\) is the load at which 50% of trials initiate.

    • Electrostatic spark: Energy at which 50% of trials ignite (ES50 in mJ).

    • Thermal stability (DSC/DTA): Onset temperature of exotherm; minimum required decomposition onset \(> 180\)°C for IHE.

    Fuze and warhead integration is performed only at the final assembly stage; safe-arm devices are verified functional (mechanically and electrically safe) before warhead attachment.

    28.5 Guidance and Electronics Manufacture¶

    28.5.1 Inertial Measurement Unit Assembly¶

    The IMU is the most precision-sensitive subassembly in the missile. Assembly takes place in ISO Class 7 (FED-STD-209E Class 10,000) or better cleanrooms to prevent particulate contamination of gyroscopes and accelerometers.

    Key alignment requirements:

    • Input axis misalignment: \(\le 0.01°\) (\(\le 175\,\mu\text{rad}\))

    • Scale factor error: \(\le 50\,\text{ppm}\)

    • Bias (accelerometer): \(\le 100\,\mu g\) (1 \(\sigma\))

    • Bias (gyroscope): \(\le 0.01°/\text{hr}\) (tactical), \(\le 0.001°/\text{hr}\) (strategic)

    The navigation error arising from gyro bias \(\omega_b\) in position grows as:

    \[\delta x \approx \frac{1}{2} g \cdot \omega_b \cdot t^2\tag{28.13}\]

    For a 60-second flight with \(\omega_b = 1°/\text{hr} = 4.85 \times 10^{-6}\,\text{rad/s}\): \(\delta x \approx \frac{1}{2} \times 9.81 \times 4.85 \times 10^{-6} \times 3600 = 0.085\,\text{m}\).

    28.5.2 Electronic Assembly¶

    Printed circuit boards. Missile electronics boards are manufactured to IPC-6012 Class 3 (most reliable) using polyimide (PI) substrates, which retain dielectric properties to 300°C. High-density interconnect (HDI) boards use laser-drilled microvias (\(< 150\,\mu\text{m}\)). Solder alloy is SAC305 or indium-based for extreme thermal cycling.

    Military-grade components. All active ICs are specified to Military or Industrial temperature ranges (\(-55°\)C to \(+125°\)C). Strategic systems use radiation-hardened (rad-hard) ASICs and FPGAs (total ionising dose \(> 100\) krad). Components are sourced to MIL-PRF-38535 (integrated circuits) or commercial equivalents with DLA-approved qualification.

    Conformal coating. After assembly and test, boards are coated with acrylic, silicone, or polyurethane conformal coat (IPC-CC-830) to 25–75 extmum depth to resist moisture, salt, and vibration-induced fretting corrosion.

    28.5.3 Seeker Assembly and Calibration¶

    Seeker subassemblies (imaging infrared, semi-active laser, active radar) are assembled in specialised environments:

    • Focal plane arrays (FPA) are bonded and wire-bonded under nitrogen purge at \(< 50\) ppm moisture.

    • Optical alignment: boresight is aligned to the missile axis to \(\le 0.1\,\text{mrad}\).

    • Gimbal balance: static unbalance \(\le 1\,\text{g-mm}\) to prevent vibration-induced bias.

    • Radiometric calibration: blackbody source at multiple temperatures; non-uniformity correction (NUC) coefficients stored in EEPROM.

    28.5.4 Cable Harness and Connector Practice¶

    Cable harnesses comply with MIL-DTL-22759 (wire) and MIL-DTL-38999 (circular connectors). All signal harnesses use shielded twisted pairs to achieve \(\ge 60\,\text{dB}\) common-mode rejection. Connector torque is specified per manufacturer data and verified by a calibrated torque wrench. Every harness undergoes 100% continuity, insulation resistance (\(> 100\,\text{M}\Omega\) at 500 V DC), and hipot testing (1,500 V AC for 60 s).

    28.5.5 Environmental Stress Screening¶

    ESS is applied to 100% of production units to precipitate latent defects (infant mortality). A HALT-derived ESS profile for missile electronics typically consists of:

    1. Thermal cycling: \(-40°\)C to \(+85°\)C; 5 cycles; ramp rate 5–10°C/min

    2. Random vibration: \(0.04\,\text{g}^2/\text{Hz}\) PSD; 20–2,000 Hz; 30 min per axis

    3. Combined temperature/vibration simultaneously

    4. Burn-in at elevated temperature (if required by contract)

    The ESS screening effectiveness \(SE\) is related to the screening strength \(SS\):

    \[SE = 1 - \exp(-SS)\tag{28.14}\]

    A \(SS = 2.0\) achieves \(SE = 0.865\) (86.5% of latent defects precipitated).

    28.5.6 Acceptance Sampling¶

    After ESS, lot acceptance sampling per MIL-STD-105 (now ANSI/ASQ Z1.4) is applied to non-100%-inspectable characteristics. At Acceptable Quality Level (AQL) 1.0, a lot of 90 units uses a sample size of 13; the accept number is 0, reject number is 1. The producer’s risk (Type I error) \(\alpha\) and consumer’s risk (Type II error) \(\beta\) are:

    \[P_{accept}(p) = \sum_{k=0}^{c} \binom{n}{k} p^k (1-p)^{n-k}\tag{28.15}\]

    where \(c\) is the acceptance number, \(n\) is sample size, and \(p\) is the true fraction defective.

    28.6 Composite Motor Case Manufacture¶

    28.6.1 Mandrel Preparation and Liner¶

    The motor case mandrel may be a steel or aluminium wash-out mandrel, a segmented steel mandrel, or a sacrificial mandrel (plaster, salt) for complex shapes. Before winding:

    1. Mandrel surfaces are grit-blasted and release agent applied (carnauba wax or silicone).

    2. An elastomeric liner (EPDM, HTPB-based, or filled EPDM) 1–5 mm thick is applied to the interior by casting or calendering. The liner bonds to both the case and the propellant, acting as a thermal insulator and barrier.

    3. The liner is cured partially (B-stage) so that final propellant casting completes the bond.

    28.6.2 Filament Winding Process¶

    CNC filament winding machines with up to 6 axes control the fibre placement angle, bandwidth, tension, and speed. Key parameters:

    \[t_{case} = \frac{p \cdot r}{2 \sigma_{f} \cdot V_f \cdot \cos^2\!\alpha}\tag{28.16}\]

    where \(\sigma_f\) is fibre tensile strength, \(V_f\) is fibre volume fraction (\(\approx 0.6\)), and \(\alpha\) is winding angle. Fibre tension during winding is controlled to \(\pm 5\%\) to ensure uniform compaction. Typical composite motor cases use T700 or T1000 carbon fibre in an epoxy matrix.

    For geodesic (isotensoid) dome winding, the winding angle at radius \(r\) from the axis is governed by:

    \[r \cdot \sin\alpha = r_0 \cdot \sin\alpha_0 = \text{constant}\tag{28.17}\]

    where \(r_0\) is the polar boss radius and \(\alpha_0\) is the winding angle at the equator. This ensures equal fibre tension throughout the dome, preventing fibre slippage.

    28.6.3 Autoclave Cure and Post-Processing¶

    Wound cases are cured in an autoclave or oven:

    • Temperature: 120–180°C depending on resin system

    • Pressure: 3–7 bar

    • Hold time: 2–4 hours

    • Cool rate: \(\le 1\)°C/min to minimise residual thermal stress

    Post-cure, the mandrel is extracted; the case is trimmed to length; and boss fittings are machined. A hydrostatic proof pressure test is conducted at 1.1 times the maximum expected operating pressure (MEOP):

    \[p_{proof} = 1.1 \times p_{MEOP}\tag{28.18}\]

    Burst pressure must exceed \(1.5 \times p_{MEOP}\) (safety factor). X-ray computed tomography (CT) scanning is used for critical applications to detect delaminations and dry spots.

    The fibre-dominated burst pressure of the cylinder is:

    \[p_{burst} = \frac{2 \sigma_f V_f t}{D/2}\tag{28.19}\]

    28.7 Nozzle Manufacture¶

    28.7.1 Throat Insert¶

    The throat insert experiences the most severe thermochemical environment in a rocket motor: stagnation temperatures of 3,000–3,600 K and hot-gas velocities approaching Mach 1. Graphite (ATJ grade, isostatically pressed) is the workhorse material. CNC turning achieves the nozzle contour to \(\pm 0.025\,\text{mm}\); surface finish \(R_a \le 0.4\,\mu\text{m}\) minimises roughness-induced boundary layer transition and ablation asymmetry.

    The throat area \(A_t\) and its relationship to motor thrust \(F\) and chamber pressure \(p_c\):

    \[F = C_F \cdot p_c \cdot A_t\tag{28.20}\]

    where the thrust coefficient \(C_F\) is:

    \[C_F = \sqrt{\frac{2\gamma^2}{\gamma-1}\left(\frac{2}{\gamma+1}\right)^{(\gamma+1)/(\gamma-1)} \left[1-\left(\frac{p_e}{p_c}\right)^{(\gamma-1)/\gamma}\right]} + \frac{p_e - p_a}{p_c} \cdot \frac{A_e}{A_t}\tag{28.21}\]

    The erosion rate of the graphite throat \(\dot{\varepsilon}\) is empirically:

    \[\dot{\varepsilon} \approx \frac{\dot{r}_s \cdot A_s}{A_t}\tag{28.22}\]

    where \(\dot{r}_s\) is the surface regression rate (typically 0.1–0.5 mm/s for filled propellants on graphite).

    28.7.2 Exit Cone¶

    Carbon-carbon (C/C) composites are used for exit cones in high-performance motors due to their exceptional strength retention at temperatures above 2,000°C. The manufacturing process:

    1. Preform fabrication: 3D woven, needle-punched, or filament-wound carbon fibre preform shaped to the exit cone geometry.

    2. CVI densification: Chemical vapour infiltration deposits pyrolytic carbon from a hydrocarbon gas (methane or propane) at 900–1,100°C and 1–10 kPa. Each cycle fills pores; multiple cycles (100+ hours total) build up density from 0.3 to 1.8–1.95 g/cm\(^3\).

    3. Oxidation protection: Silicon carbide (SiC) coating by CVD or pack cementation protects against oxidation in air up to 1,700°C.

    4. Final machining: Diamond tooling; tight tolerance on exit diameter (determines area ratio \(\varepsilon = A_e/A_t\) and hence \(I_{sp}\)).

    28.7.3 Flex Bearing (TVC)¶

    The flex bearing (flexible bearing) enables thrust vector control by tilting the nozzle up to \(\pm 8°\). It consists of alternating layers of high-strength steel shims (1–2 mm) and elastomer (natural rubber or silicone; 2–5 mm layers), bonded by heat and pressure. Manufacturing requirements:

    • Shim flatness: \(\le 0.025\,\text{mm}\) per shim

    • Elastomer bond strength: \(> 14\,\text{MPa}\) (lap shear)

    • Proof test: hydrostatic to 1.25 × MEOP; deflection through full range

    • Lateral stiffness: characterised; must remain \(< K_{max}\) to not limit actuator

    The restoring moment \(M\) of the flex bearing at deflection angle \(\theta\):

    \[M = k_\theta \cdot \theta\tag{28.23}\]

    where the rotational stiffness \(k_\theta\) (N-m/rad) is calculated from the geometry and elastomer shear modulus \(G\) via laminated-bearing theory.

    28.8 Final Assembly and Testing¶

    28.8.1 Assembly Sequence and Integration¶

    Final assembly of a surface-to-air missile (SAM) follows a sequence that enforces the explosive-safety logic while also managing the alignment and electrical verification steps:

    1. Aft section build-up: fins attached and torqued; actuators installed; aft connector checked.

    2. Motor mated to aft section: O-ring seals; torqued bolts to specified preload.

    3. Mid-section attached to motor: explosive interface (warhead forward bulkhead to motor aft bulkhead) completed under explosive-safe conditions.

    4. Guidance section pre-checked: self-test passed; alignment verified by laser tracker before mating.

    5. Fuze installed (armed only in final step before delivery).

    6. Seeker dome or radome mated; seeker bore-sight re-verified.

    7. Full missile electrical continuity: all harnesses connected; pin-for-pin verified with wiring diagram.

    28.8.2 Mass Properties Measurement¶

    Mass properties are measured on a dedicated mass properties instrument (Space Electronics, Raptor series, or equivalent):

    Table 28.4. Mass properties measurement methods and tolerances
    Property Method Accuracy Typical Value
    Total mass Load cell \(\pm 0.1\,\text{kg}\) 150–2,000 kg
    CG (axial) Roll fixture balance \(\pm 1\,\text{mm}\) Design-specific
    CG (lateral) Balance table \(\pm 0.5\,\text{mm}\) \(< 2\,\text{mm}\) offset spec
    \(I_{yy}\), \(I_{zz}\) Swing pendulum \(\pm 0.5\%\) Design-specific
    \(I_{xx}\) (roll) Torsion pendulum \(\pm 1\%\) Design-specific
    Imbalance Dynamic balance \(\pm 10\,\text{g-cm}\) \(< 50\,\text{g-cm}\) typical

    The natural period of the swing pendulum gives the moment of inertia:

    \[I = \frac{m g L}{(2\pi/T)^2} - m L^2\tag{28.24}\]

    where \(L\) is the distance from pivot to CG, \(T\) is the oscillation period, and \(m\) is total mass.

    28.8.3 Built-in Test and Acceptance¶

    Built-in test (BIT) initiates at power-on and verifies:

    • IMU bias and scale factor within limits (self-calibration)

    • Seeker: acquisition and tracking of internal reference target

    • Flight control: actuator step response; bandwidth \(> 30\,\text{Hz}\)

    • Fuze: arming circuit continuity; safe-arm state verified

    • Power: bus voltages within \(\pm 5\%\); thermal management operational

    A captive-carry test with the delivery platform verifies all umbilical and MIL-STD-1760 digital interfaces before the first free-flight test.

    28.9 Quality and Reliability¶

    28.9.1 Acceptance Test Procedures¶

    The Acceptance Test Procedure (ATP) is a contractually mandated document specifying the sequence, equipment, and pass/fail criteria for every parameter to be verified on each production unit. ATP content includes:

    • Reference drawings and specifications (with revision letters)

    • Test equipment calibration requirements (MIL-STD-45662)

    • Environmental conditions (temperature, humidity, vibration during test)

    • Pass/fail limits for each parameter (numerically stated)

    • Required signatures and data recording format

    28.9.2 Reliability Prediction and Allocation¶

    System reliability is predicted using MIL-HDBK-217F (electronics failure rates) and NPRD-2016 (non-electronic part reliability data). The system failure rate:

    \[\lambda_{system} = \sum_{i=1}^{N} \lambda_i\tag{28.25}\]

    Mean time between failures (MTBF):

    \[MTBF = \frac{1}{\lambda_{system}}\tag{28.26}\]

    For a missile guidance section with \(N = 1{,}200\) components, \(\lambda_{system}\) may be \(1.2 \times 10^{-4}\) failures/hour, giving \(MTBF = 8{,}333\) hours. However, for a single-shot missile the relevant metric is the probability of success \(P_s\) over the engagement duration \(t_m\) (typically 20–300 seconds):

    \[P_s = \exp(-\lambda_{system} \cdot t_m)\tag{28.27}\]

    For \(t_m = 60\,\text{s}\) and \(\lambda = 1.2 \times 10^{-4}\,\text{hr}^{-1}\): \(P_s = \exp(-1.2\times10^{-4}/3600 \times 60) = \exp(-2\times10^{-6}) \approx 0.999998\).

    28.9.3 Reliability Growth¶

    During development testing, the AMSAA (Army Materiel Systems Analysis Activity) reliability growth model tracks cumulative failures \(N(t)\) versus cumulative test time \(T\):

    \[N(T) = \lambda_0 \cdot T^\beta\tag{28.28}\]

    The growth parameter \(\beta < 1\) indicates improving reliability. The current (instantaneous) failure rate is:

    \[\lambda(T) = \lambda_0 \cdot \beta \cdot T^{\beta-1}\tag{28.29}\]

    A Duane plot (\(\log\) cumulative failure rate vs. \(\log\) cumulative time) reveals whether the program is on track to achieve the reliability requirement by the start of production.

    28.9.4 FRACAS¶

    Failure Reporting, Analysis, and Corrective Action System (FRACAS) is a closed-loop failure management process mandatory on all US DoD missile development programs (per MIL-HDBK-2155). Every anomaly is:

    1. Identified and classified (hardware, software, test-induced, operator error)

    2. Analysed for root cause (fault tree, 5-Why, Ishikawa diagram)

    3. Corrected (design change, process change, specification revision)

    4. Verified by retest

    5. Closed with documented evidence

    28.9.5 Shelf Life Determination¶

    Missile shelf life is assessed using the Arrhenius acceleration model. At an elevated storage temperature \(T_H\), the acceleration factor \(AF\) relative to the rated storage temperature \(T_L\):

    \[AF = \exp\!\left[\frac{E_a}{R_u}\left(\frac{1}{T_L} - \frac{1}{T_H}\right)\right]\tag{28.30}\]

    For a propellant with \(E_a = 80\,\text{kJ/mol}\) and storage temperatures \(T_L = 298\,\text{K}\) (\(25°\)C) and \(T_H = 328\,\text{K}\) (\(55°\)C):

    \[AF = \exp\!\left[\frac{80{,}000}{8.314}\left(\frac{1}{298} - \frac{1}{328}\right)\right] = \exp(9{,}621 \times 9.14 \times 10^{-4}) \approx e^{8.79} \approx 6.6\tag{28.31}\]

    That is, one year at 55°C degrades the propellant as much as 6.6 years at 25°C, enabling accelerated life testing programmes.

    28.10 Production Economics¶

    28.10.1 Learning Curve Theory¶

    As cumulative production quantity doubles, unit cost falls by a consistent fraction. The Wright (unit) learning curve model:

    \[C_n = C_1 \cdot n^{\log_2 p}\tag{28.32}\]

    where \(C_n\) is the cost of the \(n\)th unit, \(C_1\) is the cost of the first unit, and \(p\) is the learning rate (progress ratio). For \(p = 0.80\) (80% learning curve): \(\log_2(0.80) = -0.322\). The cumulative average cost model (Crawford model) gives:

    \[\bar{C}_n = C_1 \cdot n^{\log_2 p}\tag{28.33}\]

    where \(\bar{C}_n\) is the average cost of all \(n\) units. Both models are reconciled via the “T1” first-unit cost and the “slope” parameter used by DoD cost analysts in the AFCAA Cost Analysis Handbook.

    Typical cost allocation for a medium-range tactical missile at production maturity:

    28.10.3 Multi-Year Procurement¶

    Multi-year procurement (MYP) authority, authorised by 10 USC 3501, allows DoD to contract for up to 5 years of production in a single contract action. Savings of 10–15% over annual procurement result from:

    • Long-lead material purchasing at locked-in prices

    • Stable production rates enabling supplier optimisation

    • Reduction in programme management and re-procurement overhead

    • Assured volume enabling capital investment by suppliers

    28.10.4 Foreign Military Sales and Offsets¶

    Foreign military sales (FMS) are conducted through the Defense Security Cooperation Agency (DSCA). The FMS case process involves Letter of Request (LOR), Letter of Offer and Acceptance (LOA), and contract award. Technology transfer is controlled by ITAR; country-unique variants may be approved for export when national security implications have been reviewed.

    Offset agreements (industrial participation, IP) require the prime contractor to generate economic benefit for the purchasing nation equivalent to a fraction (50–100%) of the contract value. Offsets may be direct (licensed production of missile components in-country) or indirect (investment in unrelated industries).

    28.10.5 Surge Capacity and Bottlenecks¶

    Peacetime missile production rates are designed for political palatability and budget constraints, not for wartime replenishment. Key surge bottlenecks include:

    • Explosive fill capacity: Government-owned explosive-loading facilities cannot be rapidly expanded; new cells require 2–5 years for DDESB approval and construction.

    • Seeker and guidance microelectronics: Advanced infrared focal plane arrays (FPA) and radiation-hardened ASICs are produced by a handful of suppliers with limited surge capacity and long qualification lead times.

    • Specialty materials: Carbon-carbon nozzles (CVI process) require 100+ hours per article; isostatic graphite for throats is produced by a very limited industrial base.

    • Skilled labour: Explosive-process operators, propellant chemists, and NDT Level III inspectors require years of training and clearance.

    The DoD has responded with the Organic Industrial Base (OIB) modernisation programme, Advanced Manufacturing of Energetics (AME) initiatives, and National Defence Industrial Association (NDIA) engagement with the defence industrial base.
