    Chapter 32: Directed Energy Weapons¶

    32.1 Introduction to Directed Energy Weapons¶

    Directed energy weapons (DEWs) constitute a class of armaments that defeat targets by delivering energy in the form of electromagnetic radiation, particle beams, or plasma rather than by propelling a mass at high velocity. Unlike kinetic interceptors, a DEW engages its target at or near the speed of light, eliminating the need to compute a lead angle for all but the most extreme slant ranges. The phrase directed energy differentiates these weapons from nuclear or chemical weapons that release energy omnidirectionally; in a DEW the energy is coherently directed along a defined propagation path toward a specific aim point.

    Three primary technology families define the DEW landscape:

    1. High Energy Laser (HEL) — coherent electromagnetic radiation in the optical or near-infrared spectrum, concentrated into a narrow beam that delivers thermal energy sufficient to heat, melt, ignite, or structurally weaken a target.

    2. High Power Microwave (HPM) — high-power radiofrequency pulses, typically in the 1–300 GHz range, that couple energy into electronic systems through antenna ports (front-door coupling) or structural apertures (back-door coupling), causing latch-up, burnout, or logic upset.

    3. Particle Beam Weapons (PBW) — streams of accelerated charged or neutralised particles whose kinetic and ionising energy interact directly with target material; primarily relevant in exo-atmospheric or near-space applications.

    32.1.1 Speed-of-Light Delivery and Engagement Geometry¶

    At tactical ranges of 1–20 km the propagation time for a laser or microwave beam is:

    \[t_{\text{prop}} = \frac{R}{c} \approx \frac{10{,}000}{3 \times 10^8} \approx 33\;\mu\text{s}\tag{32.1}\]

    This is negligible compared to any mechanical or electronic system latency, which typically lies in the millisecond regime. Consequently, no ballistic lead angle is required. For a target moving at \(V_T = 300\;\text{m/s}\) at range \(R = 5\;\text{km}\), the kinetic weapon would require a lead distance \(\Delta x = V_T \cdot t_{\text{flight}}\), where \(t_{\text{flight}}\) may be 10–20 s for a gun round. A DEW requires only accurate current pointing, dramatically simplifying the fire-control problem.

    32.1.2 Advantages and Disadvantages of DEWs¶

    Advantages:

    • Deep magazine: limited only by electrical power supply; a ship’s power plant or a vehicle generator can supply thousands of engagements;

    • Low per-shot cost: a \(5\,\text{USD}\) electricity cost per engagement for HEL, versus thousands to millions of dollars for kinetic interceptors;

    • Scalable effects: power and dwell time can be modulated — dazzle, damage, or destroy;

    • Precision: beam can be held on a specific sub-component (e.g., warhead fuze or optical seeker);

    • Near-zero collateral fragment hazard: no shrapnel.

    Disadvantages:

    • Atmospheric propagation loss: molecular absorption, aerosol scattering, and thermal blooming attenuate the beam;

    • Thermal blooming: high-power beams heat the air, creating a refractive lens that defocuses the beam;

    • Beam jitter: platform vibration and atmospheric turbulence widen the effective spot;

    • Power supply mass and volume: multi-MW power conditioning plant required;

    • Dwell time requirement: target must be held in the beam for 1–10 s for thermal kill, requiring stable tracking.

    32.2 High Energy Laser Fundamentals¶

    32.2.1 Laser Physics¶

    A laser (Light Amplification by Stimulated Emission of Radiation) produces coherent, near-monochromatic electromagnetic radiation through the process of stimulated emission. When a photon of energy \(h\nu\) encounters an atom already in an excited state, it stimulates the emission of an identical photon — same frequency, phase, and direction — returning the atom to its ground state. Three conditions are necessary for laser operation:

    1. Population inversion: more atoms in the upper energy level \(N_2\) than the lower \(N_1\); required for net amplification rather than absorption.

    2. Gain medium: solid, liquid, gas, or semiconductor that supports population inversion; examples include Yb-doped silica glass fibre (1070 nm), Nd:YAG crystal (1064 nm), CO\(_2\) gas (10,600 nm), and chemical oxygen–iodine (1315 nm).

    3. Optical resonator (cavity): two mirrors (one partially transmissive) that recirculate photons through the gain medium, building coherent intensity; threshold condition requires round-trip gain to exceed round-trip loss.

    The small-signal gain coefficient \(g_0\) of the medium is:

    \[g_0 = \sigma_{21}\left(N_2 - \frac{g_2}{g_1}N_1\right)\tag{32.2}\]

    where \(\sigma_{21}\) is the stimulated-emission cross-section, \(g_1\) and \(g_2\) are degeneracy factors, and \(N_1\), \(N_2\) are population densities. Lasing occurs when \(g_0 L > \alpha L + \ln(1/R_1 R_2)/2\), with cavity length \(L\), distributed loss \(\alpha\), and mirror reflectivities \(R_1\), \(R_2\).

    32.2.2 Beam Divergence and Spot Size¶

    A diffraction-limited Gaussian beam has a half-angle divergence:

    \[\theta_{\text{diff}} = \frac{\lambda}{\pi w_0}\tag{32.3}\]

    where \(\lambda\) is the wavelength and \(w_0\) is the beam waist radius. For a hard aperture of diameter \(D\), the far-field diffraction-limited full-angle divergence is approximated by the Rayleigh criterion:

    \[\theta_{1/2} = 2.44\frac{\lambda}{D}\tag{32.4}\]

    The 1/\(e^2\)-radius spot at range \(R\) for a perfect beam is:

    \[r_{\text{spot}}(R) = \theta_{\text{diff}} \cdot R = \frac{\lambda R}{\pi w_0}\tag{32.5}\]

    In practice, real beams are not diffraction-limited due to aberrations in the gain medium, optical elements, and the atmosphere. The beam quality factor \(M^2\) quantifies the departure:

    \[\theta_{\text{real}} = M^2 \cdot \theta_{\text{diff}}\tag{32.6}\]

    A perfect Gaussian beam has \(M^2 = 1.0\). High-quality fibre lasers achieve \(M^2 = 1.05\)–\(1.2\); slab amplifiers reach \(M^2 = 1.2\)–\(2.0\). Beam quality directly degrades irradiance on target, since irradiance scales as \(1/(M^2)^2\).

    32.2.3 Irradiance on Target¶

    The peak irradiance delivered to a target at range \(R\) by a laser of output power \(P\), accounting for beam quality \(M^2\), atmospheric transmission \(\eta_{\text{atm}}\), and optics transmission \(\eta_{\text{opt}}\), is:

    \[I(R) = \frac{P \cdot \eta_{\text{atm}} \cdot \eta_{\text{opt}}}{\pi \left(\frac{M^2 \lambda R}{\pi w_0}\right)^2}\tag{32.7}\]

    Substituting \(\theta = M^2 \lambda / (\pi w_0)\):

    \[I(R) = \frac{P \cdot \eta_{\text{atm}} \cdot \eta_{\text{opt}}}{\pi (\theta R)^2}\tag{32.8}\]

    For a 100 kW system with \(\theta = 2\;\mu\text{rad}\), \(\eta_{\text{atm}} = 0.8\), \(\eta_{\text{opt}} = 0.9\), at \(R = 5\,\text{km}\):

    \[I = \frac{10^5 \times 0.8 \times 0.9}{\pi \times (2 \times 10^{-6} \times 5000)^2} = \frac{72000}{\pi \times 10^{-4}} \approx 2.3\;\text{MW/m}^2 = 230\;\text{W/cm}^2\tag{32.9}\]

    32.2.4 Atmospheric Propagation¶

    The atmosphere attenuates and distorts a laser beam through four principal mechanisms:

    Molecular Absorption¶

    Molecular absorption occurs when photons are absorbed by rotational-vibrational transitions of atmospheric gases. The primary absorbers are H\(_2\)O, CO\(_2\), and O\(_2\). The Beer–Lambert law gives the one-way atmospheric transmittance over path length \(R\):

    \[T_{\text{atm}}(R) = \exp\!\left(-\int_0^R \alpha_{\text{abs}}(s)\,ds\right) \approx \exp(-\alpha_{\text{abs}} \cdot R)\tag{32.10}\]

    where \(\alpha_{\text{abs}}\) is the wavelength-dependent absorption coefficient (m\(^{-1}\)). At 1070 nm (Yb fibre), \(\alpha_{\text{abs}} \approx 3 \times 10^{-5}\;\text{m}^{-1}\) in standard atmosphere, giving \(T_{\text{atm}} \approx 0.86\) at 5 km. Atmospheric windows with low absorption include \(1.0\)–\(1.1\;\mu\text{m}\), \(1.5\)–\(1.8\;\mu\text{m}\), \(3\)–\(5\;\mu\text{m}\), and \(8\)–\(12\;\mu\text{m}\).

    Mie and Rayleigh Scattering¶

    Rayleigh scattering by molecules has a scattering coefficient \(\beta_R \propto \lambda^{-4}\), strongly penalising shorter wavelengths. Mie scattering by aerosols (fog, dust, haze) is approximately wavelength-independent in the \(1\;\mu\text{m}\) range. Total scattering extinction:

    \[\alpha_{\text{scat}} = \beta_R + \beta_M\tag{32.11}\]

    In clear atmosphere, aerosol extinction at 1 \(\mu\)m is approximately \(\alpha_{\text{scat}} \approx 10^{-4}\;\text{m}^{-1}\); in moderate haze it rises to \(5 \times 10^{-4}\;\text{m}^{-1}\), corresponding to a meteorological visibility of \(\sim\)8 km.

    Thermal Blooming¶

    Thermal blooming occurs when the laser beam heats the air along its path, creating a temperature gradient and thus a refractive index gradient \(\Delta n \propto -\Delta T\). This acts as a negative (diverging) lens that spreads the beam, reducing peak irradiance. The dimensionless thermal distortion parameter \(N_D\) characterises the severity:

    \[N_D = \frac{-2\alpha_{\text{abs}} P \frac{dn}{dT}}{\rho C_p V_{\perp} w_0 n_0}\tag{32.12}\]

    where \(\rho\) is air density, \(C_p\) specific heat, \(V_\perp\) is the transverse wind speed, and \(dn/dT \approx -9.2 \times 10^{-7}\;\text{K}^{-1}\) for air at standard conditions. Blooming becomes significant when \(|N_D| > 1\). Wind \(V_\perp > 5\;\text{m/s}\) advects heated air out of the beam path, substantially reducing blooming — this is known as the slewing or wind clearing benefit.

    Atmospheric Turbulence and the Fried Parameter¶

    Turbulence creates random refractive index fluctuations that scatter, tilt, and aberrate the beam. The Fried coherence length \(r_0\) describes the spatial scale over which the wavefront remains coherent:

    \[r_0 = 0.185\left(\frac{\lambda^2}{C_n^2 R}\right)^{3/5}\tag{32.13}\]

    where \(C_n^2\) is the refractive index structure constant (\(10^{-17}\)–\(10^{-13}\;\text{m}^{-2/3}\) depending on turbulence strength). For \(\lambda = 1070\;\text{nm}\), \(C_n^2 = 10^{-15}\;\text{m}^{-2/3}\), \(R = 5\;\text{km}\):

    \[r_0 = 0.185\left(\frac{(1.07 \times 10^{-6})^2}{10^{-15} \times 5000}\right)^{3/5} \approx 0.185 \times (2.29 \times 10^{-4})^{0.6} \approx 5.2\;\text{cm}\tag{32.14}\]

    When the aperture \(D > r_0\), turbulence-induced aberrations dominate the far-field spot size.

    32.2.5 Adaptive Optics¶

    Adaptive optics (AO) systems correct wavefront aberrations in real time to recover near-diffraction-limited performance. The three core subsystems are:

    1. Wavefront sensor (WFS): A Hartmann-Shack sensor divides the aperture into an array of microlenses, each focusing light onto a detector; local spot displacements measure the wavefront gradient \(\nabla W(x,y)\).

    2. Wavefront reconstructor: Least-squares inversion converts gradient measurements to phase map; typical update rate 1–10 kHz.

    3. Deformable mirror (DM): Piezoelectric or MEMS actuators deform a reflective surface to apply a conjugate phase correction \(\phi_{\text{DM}} = -W(x,y)\); typical actuator counts 100–5000.

    Correction residual variance \(\sigma_{\text{AO}}^2\) in radians squared due to fitting error with \(N_{\text{act}}\) actuators across aperture \(D\):

    \[\sigma_{\text{fit}}^2 = a_f \left(\frac{D}{N_{\text{act}}^{1/2} \cdot r_0}\right)^{5/3}\tag{32.15}\]

    where \(a_f \approx 0.28\) for a square actuator grid. Strehl ratio after correction approximates:

    \[S \approx \exp(-\sigma_{\text{total}}^2)\tag{32.16}\]

    A Strehl ratio of \(S = 0.8\) corresponds to \(\sigma_{\text{total}} \approx 0.47\;\text{rad}\), typically achievable with a well-corrected AO system under moderate turbulence.

    32.2.6 Target Interaction and Kill Mechanisms¶

    The irradiance \(I\) at the target surface drives thermal heating. Surface temperature rise at the laser spot:

    \[T_s(t) = \frac{2(1-\rho_r) I \sqrt{\alpha_{\text{th}} t}}{\sqrt{\pi} k_{\text{th}}}\tag{32.17}\]

    where \(\rho_r\) is the surface reflectivity, \(\alpha_{\text{th}} = k_{\text{th}}/(\rho_m c_m)\) is thermal diffusivity, and \(k_{\text{th}}\) is thermal conductivity. Kill occurs when \(T_s\) exceeds either the melting temperature, the ignition temperature of a propellant, or the structural failure temperature of a composite. The kill fluence \(F_{\text{kill}}\) (J/cm\(^2\)) is the energy density required to achieve thermal kill:

    \[F_{\text{kill}} = \frac{E_{\text{dep}}}{A_{\text{spot}}} = I \cdot t_{\text{dwell}}\tag{32.18}\]

    Typical \(F_{\text{kill}}\) values:

    • Aluminium alloy structural panel (3 mm): \(\sim\)2–5 kJ/cm\(^2\)

    • Steel skin: \(\sim\)8–15 kJ/cm\(^2\)

    • Carbon fibre composite (drone body): \(\sim\)0.5–2 kJ/cm\(^2\)

    • Propellant ignition (rocket motor case): \(\sim\)0.1–0.5 kJ/cm\(^2\)

    • Optical seeker glass: \(\sim\)0.05–0.2 kJ/cm\(^2\)

    32.2.7 Dwell Time Calculation¶

    Required dwell time on target for thermal kill:

    \[t_{\text{dwell}} = \frac{F_{\text{kill}} \cdot \pi r_{\text{spot}}^2(R)}{P \cdot \eta_{\text{total}}}\tag{32.19}\]

    Substituting from Equation (32.8):

    \[t_{\text{dwell}} = \frac{F_{\text{kill}}}{I(R)}\tag{32.20}\]

    For a 100 kW laser at \(R = 3\;\text{km}\), \(\eta_{\text{total}} = 0.7\), \(\theta = 2\;\mu\text{rad}\): \(I = 2.5\;\text{kW/cm}^2\); for CFRP drone with \(F_{\text{kill}} = 1\;\text{kJ/cm}^2\): \(t_{\text{dwell}} = 0.4\;\text{s}\). This is tractable for a tracking system.

    32.2.8 Laser Types for HEL Applications¶

    Solid-State Lasers¶

    Nd:YAG (1064 nm): Well-established; wall-plug efficiency \(\eta_{\text{wall}} \approx 10\)–\(15\%\); flash-lamp or diode-pumped; pulsed or CW; excellent for low-power applications.

    Yb:YAG / Yb-fibre (1030–1080 nm): Currently dominant HEL technology. Diode-pumped; \(\eta_{\text{wall}} \approx 30\)–\(45\%\); inherently excellent beam quality in single-mode fibre; power scaled by coherent beam combination (CBC) or spectral beam combination (SBC) of many fibres.

    Coherent Beam Combination: Multiple fibre amplifiers, each seeded from a master oscillator, are phase-locked using an adaptive phase controller. Output beams are combined at a beam splitter or diffractive element. For \(N\) fibres each of power \(P_f\) with phase error \(\sigma_\phi\):

    \[P_{\text{combined}} = N^2 P_f \exp(-\sigma_\phi^2)\tag{32.21}\]

    This quadratic scaling (for near-zero phase error) is the great advantage of CBC. Current demonstrations have combined up to 61 fibres with \(>\)90% combining efficiency.

    Chemical Lasers¶

    COIL (Chemical Oxygen–Iodine Laser, 1315 nm): First megawatt-class HEL; used in the Airborne Laser (ABL) programme. Energy stored chemically; can achieve very high power without a large electrical plant. Primary disadvantage: corrosive chemicals (Cl\(_2\), H\(_2\)O\(_2\)), large logistics burden, effluent management.

    HF/DF Laser (2700/3800 nm): Very high power density; operates in mid-IR where absorption is high; used in MIRACL (Mid-Infrared Advanced Chemical Laser), once the most powerful US laser at 2.2 MW; logistically demanding; no longer in active development.

    Free-Electron Laser (FEL)¶

    The FEL passes a relativistic electron beam through a periodic magnetic field (undulator) to produce tunable coherent radiation. Wavelength is controllable by adjusting electron energy:

    \[\lambda_{\text{FEL}} = \frac{\lambda_u}{2\gamma^2}\left(1 + \frac{K^2}{2}\right)\tag{32.22}\]

    where \(\lambda_u\) is the undulator period, \(\gamma = E/(m_e c^2)\) is the Lorentz factor, and \(K = eB_0\lambda_u/(2\pi m_e c)\) is the undulator parameter. FELs are large, expensive, and not yet practical for tactical systems, but offer continuous wavelength tunability — the ability to select the atmospheric window with lowest attenuation on the fly.

    32.3 Operational Laser Weapon Systems¶

    32.3.1 USS Ponce AN/SEQ-3 LAWS¶

    The Laser Weapon System (LAWS) aboard USS Ponce was the first US Navy laser weapon deployed operationally in 2014. The solid-state, 30 kW system demonstrated the ability to engage small boats, UAVs, and mortar rounds in the Persian Gulf region. It used a commercial off-the-shelf (COTS) beam director and was integrated with the ship’s combat management system. Engagement of a small UAV at ranges up to 1 km was demonstrated, with burn-through times of 1–3 s.

    32.3.2 HELIOS — High Energy Laser with Integrated Optical-dazzler and Surveillance¶

    HELIOS, developed by Lockheed Martin, represents the first production HEL system for the US Navy’s Arleigh Burke–class destroyers (DDG-51 Flight III). Key specifications:

    • Laser power: 60 kW (upgradeable to 150 kW)

    • Wavelength: 1070 nm (Yb fibre)

    • Mission: C-UAS, C-USV (counter unmanned surface vessel), ISR (surveillance/dazzle mode)

    • Beam director: 0.3 m aperture; inertially stabilised gimbal

    • IOC: DDG-72 USS Dewey, 2022

    • Integration: Ship’s Aegis combat system; direct cueing from SPY-1D(V) radar

    32.3.3 HEL TVD — High Energy Laser Tactical Vehicle Demonstrator¶

    Mounted on a Joint Light Tactical Vehicle (JLTV), the HEL TVD programme (50 kW, Raytheon/Kord Technologies) targets short-range air defence: UAVs, RPGs, and RAM (Rockets, Artillery, Mortars). The system demonstrated engagement of a 60 mm mortar round in 2023 trials, representing the hardest C-RAM intercept class for HEL due to the small RCS and short exposure time.

    32.3.4 Iron Beam (Israel)¶

    Iron Beam, developed by Rafael and Elbit Systems, reached operational status in 2025 as the first HEL C-RAM system deployed in combat conditions. At 100 kW, it can engage rockets at 7–10 km range and drones at 4–7 km. During the 2023 Gaza conflict, Iron Dome intercepted  \(90\%\) of incoming rockets; Iron Beam is designed to handle the lower-cost saturation attacks that challenge kinetic interceptors. Cost per engagement is approximately $5 (electricity), versus $50,000–$100,000 per Iron Dome Tamir interceptor.

    32.3.5 DragonFire (United Kingdom)¶

    DragonFire is a consortium programme involving Leonardo UK, MBDA, and QinetiQ, funded by the UK MOD. After successful trials at BUTEC (British Underwater Test and Evaluation Centre) range in 2023, the 50 kW system demonstrated engagement of aerial targets at ranges up to 3 km. The programme targets ship and ground vehicle integration, with service entry planned for 2027–2030.

    32.4 High Power Microwave Weapons¶

    32.4.1 HPM Principles¶

    High Power Microwave weapons deliver nanosecond-to-microsecond pulses of radiofrequency energy at peak powers of 10 MW–10 GW into a target’s electronic subsystems. Damage is through:

    • Front-door coupling: Energy enters via the target’s own receive antenna; amplified by antenna gain; can destroy receiver front-ends with relatively modest field strengths.

    • Back-door coupling: Energy couples through structural apertures — seams, cable penetrations, vents — and propagates as a conducted or radiated disturbance onto circuit boards.

    The susceptibility threshold for semiconductor junctions is expressed in terms of electric field \(E_{\text{thresh}}\) or power density \(S_{\text{thresh}}\):

    \[P_{\text{damage}} = \frac{E_{\text{thresh}}^2}{Z_0} \cdot A_{\text{eff}}\tag{32.23}\]

    where \(Z_0 = 377\;\Omega\) is the impedance of free space and \(A_{\text{eff}}\) is the effective receiving aperture of the coupling path.

    32.4.2 HPM Sources¶

    Magnetron¶

    The magnetron is a crossed-field device (perpendicular electric and magnetic fields) that produces high-power microwave pulses. Widely used in radar and industrial applications; wall-plug efficiency 50–70%; pulsed peak power 1 kW–5 MW; frequency 1–18 GHz. Magnetrons are coherent only within a single pulse — they lack phase memory pulse-to-pulse.

    Klystron¶

    The klystron is a linear-beam amplifier driven by an RF input signal; it produces very high-power, coherent output at fixed frequencies. Used in large radar transmitters and particle accelerators; peak power up to 100 MW; efficiency 40–60%.

    Vircator (Virtual Cathode Oscillator)¶

    The vircator is a compact, single-pulse HPM source requiring no external magnetic field. A high-current electron beam is injected into a resonant cavity; when the space-charge limit is exceeded, a virtual cathode forms and oscillates, radiating at a frequency:

    \[f_{\text{vircator}} \approx \frac{\omega_p}{2\pi} = \frac{1}{2\pi}\sqrt{\frac{n_e e^2}{\epsilon_0 m_e}}\tag{32.24}\]

    where \(n_e\) is electron density, \(e\) electron charge, and \(m_e\) electron mass. Peak powers of 1–10 GW are achievable in a package of \(<\)50 kg, making the vircator attractive for missile-deliverable HPM payloads. Efficiency is typically 1–5%.

    32.4.3 HPM Systems and Programs¶

    CHAMP (Counter-electronics High-powered Microwave Advanced Missile Project)¶

    CHAMP, developed by Boeing for AFRL, is a cruise-missile-based HPM weapon. The weapon carries a repetitively-pulsed HPM source powered by a turbine generator from the JP-10 fuel supply. In a 2012 demonstration at the Utah Test and Training Range, CHAMP made ten passes over seven targets, successfully neutralising the electronics at each. The weapon then self-destructed. CHAMP demonstrated:

    • Multiple target engagement in a single sortie

    • Non-kinetic defeat of hardened facilities

    • Standoff engagement at cruise missile range (500–1000 km)

    • No collateral structural damage

    Active Denial System (ADS)¶

    The ADS operates at 95 GHz, where the millimetre-wave radiation is absorbed in the outermost 0.4 mm of human skin (the epidermis), producing an intense heating sensation without penetrating to underlying tissue. Parameters:

    • Frequency: 95 GHz (3.16 mm wavelength)

    • Power: 100 kW (system); 10–50 kW at aperture

    • Beam divergence: \(\sim\)0.5° (Gaussian)

    • Effective range: 500–1000 m

    • Skin penetration: \(\delta = \sqrt{2/(\omega\mu\sigma)} \approx 0.4\;\text{mm}\) at 95 GHz

    • Pain onset: \(T_{\text{skin}} > 44^{\circ}\text{C}\) after \(\sim\)3 s exposure

    The ADS is classified as a non-lethal weapon for crowd control. It was deployed to Afghanistan in 2010 but not operationally used; the perception of the weapon as a pain ray made it politically sensitive.

    32.4.4 THOR — Tactical High-power Operational Responder¶

    THOR, developed by AFRL, is a containerised ground-based HPM system designed specifically for counter-UAS applications. A single HPM pulse is sufficient to cause electronic upset or burnout in a small drone’s flight controller. THOR can engage multiple drones simultaneously within its beam footprint — a key advantage over single-track HEL systems. THOR underwent desert environment testing in 2020 and was loaned to the US Air Force for base perimeter defence evaluation.

    32.4.5 Coupling Analysis¶

    For a target at range \(R\) in the main beam of an HPM antenna with gain \(G_t\):

    \[S_{\text{target}} = \frac{P_t G_t}{4\pi R^2}\tag{32.25}\]

    Power coupled into a target aperture of effective area \(A_{\text{coup}}\):

    \[P_{\text{coup}} = S_{\text{target}} \cdot A_{\text{coup}} = \frac{P_t G_t A_{\text{coup}}}{4\pi R^2}\tag{32.26}\]

    For \(P_t = 1\;\text{GW}\), \(G_t = 30\;\text{dB} = 1000\), \(R = 1\;\text{km}\), \(A_{\text{coup}} = 0.01\;\text{m}^2\):

    \[P_{\text{coup}} = \frac{10^9 \times 10^3 \times 0.01}{4\pi \times 10^6} = 796\;\text{W}\tag{32.27}\]

    This is far above the semiconductor burnout threshold of milliwatts to watts, indicating that HPM readily defeats unshielded electronics at tactical ranges.

    32.5 Particle Beam Weapons¶

    32.5.1 Principles¶

    Particle beam weapons accelerate charged particles — electrons, protons, or heavier ions — through a particle accelerator to relativistic velocities. At the target, the beam deposits energy through:

    • Coulomb interactions: elastic and inelastic scattering; produces ionisation, excitation, and radiation damage in semiconductors and biological tissue;

    • Nuclear interactions: for protons and ions, nuclear spallation at high enough energies can disrupt nuclear material;

    • Bremsstrahlung radiation: electrons produce X-ray secondary radiation in dense targets.

    The range of a proton in air (the Bragg curve) at kinetic energy \(E_k\) (MeV) follows approximately:

    \[R_{\text{proton}} \approx 0.0022 \cdot E_k^{1.77}\;\text{m (in air at STP)}\tag{32.28}\]

    A 100 MeV proton has a range of \(\sim\)8 m in air — far too short for weaponisable beam weapons in atmosphere.

    32.5.2 Neutral Particle Beam (NPB)¶

    In space, where there is no atmosphere, a charged particle beam can propagate freely, though space-charge repulsion eventually spreads the beam. The NPB concept involves accelerating ions to high energy, then stripping an electron to produce a neutral atom beam that is unaffected by magnetic fields but propagates in a straight line. The SDI (Strategic Defense Initiative) programme in the 1980s developed NPB concepts for boost-phase intercept of ICBMs in mid-course. The Alpha Project demonstrated a 1 MeV hydrogen neutral beam. No operational space-based NPB system has been deployed.

    32.5.3 Current Status¶

    Particle beam weapons remain at TRL 2–4. The fundamental challenges are:

    1. Accelerator mass: linear accelerators producing 100 MeV beams weigh tens of tonnes;

    2. Atmospheric propagation: utterly impractical below 80 km altitude;

    3. Power: continuous high-energy beams require hundreds of megawatts;

    4. Beam pointing: relativistic beam deflection requires large superconducting magnets.

    32.6 Engagement Geometry Analysis¶

    32.6.1 Slant Range for Ship-Based Engagement¶

    For a ship-based HEL system at height \(H\) above the waterline engaging a target at elevation angle \(\varepsilon\):

    \[R_{\text{slant}} = \frac{H}{\sin\varepsilon}\tag{32.29}\]

    For a sea-skimming missile at 5 m altitude, hull height 15 m, and minimum detection range 10 km: \(\varepsilon = \arcsin(15/10{,}000) \approx 0.086°\) — very shallow, requiring a highly stabilised low-elevation beam director.

    32.6.2 Effective Spot Size with Jitter¶

    Beam wander due to platform vibration and atmospheric tip-tilt introduces an additional pointing error \(\sigma_j\) (rad). The effective irradiance-equivalent spot radius is:

    \[r_{\text{eff}} = \sqrt{r_{\text{diff}}^2 + (\sigma_j R)^2}\tag{32.30}\]

    where \(r_{\text{diff}} = M^2 \lambda R / (\pi w_0)\) is the diffraction-limited radius. Jitter reduces peak irradiance:

    \[I_{\text{jitter}} = I_{\text{ideal}} \cdot \exp\!\left(-\frac{2\sigma_j^2 R^2}{r_{\text{diff}}^2}\right) \quad \text{(for } \sigma_j R \ll r_{\text{diff}}\text{)}\tag{32.31}\]

    For \(\sigma_j = 1\;\mu\text{rad}\) (achievable with inertially stabilised beam director), \(R = 5\;\text{km}\), \(r_{\text{diff}} = 10\;\text{cm}\): \(\sigma_j R = 5\;\text{mm}\), which is small compared to \(r_{\text{diff}}\), so jitter is manageable. At longer ranges or with higher jitter (ship in sea-state 4), \(\sigma_j\) may rise to 5–10 \(\mu\)rad, significantly degrading irradiance.

    32.6.3 Tracking Slew Rate Requirement¶

    The fire-control system must track the target throughout the dwell time. The required angular slew rate:

    \[\dot{\theta}_{\text{track}} = \frac{V_T}{R}\tag{32.32}\]

    For a drone at \(V_T = 50\;\text{m/s}\), \(R = 2\;\text{km}\): \(\dot{\theta} = 25\;\text{mrad/s}\), easily achieved by modern gimbals (\(>\)3 rad/s). For a fast jet at \(V_T = 300\;\text{m/s}\), \(R = 1\;\text{km}\): \(\dot{\theta} = 300\;\text{mrad/s} = 17°/\text{s}\), requiring a fast-slewing gimbal and high control bandwidth.

    32.6.4 Power Scaling Laws¶

    From Equation (32.8), irradiance at range \(R\) scales as:

    \[I \propto \frac{P}{R^2}\tag{32.33}\]

    Halving the dwell time requires doubling \(I\), which requires doubling \(P\) (at fixed range). Halving the range at constant power quadruples \(I\), quartering the dwell time. These scaling laws drive the operational tactic of closing range before engaging with HEL.

    32.7 Power Systems for Directed Energy Weapons¶

    32.7.1 Wall-Plug Efficiency and Waste Heat¶

    For a laser of wall-plug efficiency \(\eta_{\text{wall}}\), the thermal waste heat per unit of laser output power:

    \[P_{\text{waste}} = P_{\text{laser}}\left(\frac{1}{\eta_{\text{wall}}} - 1\right)\tag{32.34}\]

    For Yb fibre at \(\eta_{\text{wall}} = 0.40\) and \(P_{\text{laser}} = 100\;\text{kW}\):

    \[P_{\text{waste}} = 10^5 \times (2.5 - 1) = 150\;\text{kW}\tag{32.35}\]

    This 150 kW of waste heat must be managed by a thermal management system (TMS). At sea, seawater provides a convenient heat sink. For ground vehicles, the TMS is a major mass and volume driver.

    32.7.2 Pulsed Power Systems¶

    HPM weapons require very high instantaneous power (gigawatts) for very short durations (nanoseconds–microseconds). This is achieved through pulsed power systems:

    • Capacitor bank: stores energy \(U = \frac{1}{2}CV^2\); typical parameters: \(C = 10\;\text{mF}\), \(V = 100\;\text{kV}\), \(U = 50\;\text{kJ}\); switched by spark gaps or solid-state switches;

    • Marx generator: charges \(N\) capacitors in parallel, discharges in series; output voltage \(NV_0\); achieves \(>\)1 MV at \(>\)100 kJ in compact geometry;

    • Flux compression generator (FCG): single-use explosive-driven device; compresses magnetic flux to produce \(>\)100 MJ pulse; used in EMP bombs.

    Pulse compression and forming: a pulse-forming network (PFN) shapes the discharge into a rectangular pulse of impedance-matched duration. For a transmission-line PFN of impedance \(Z_0\), pulse duration \(\tau = 2l/v_p\), where \(l\) is the electrical length and \(v_p\) is the propagation speed.

    32.7.3 Continuous Power — Ship and Vehicle¶

    For shipboard HEL, the vessel’s gas turbine or combined diesel-and-gas (CODAG) plant typically produces 30–100 MW. A 100 kW laser drawing 250 kW at the wall adds \(<\)1% to total ship load — entirely manageable. For ground vehicles, a JLTV-class platform produces \(\sim\)40 kW; a 50 kW HEL requires a dedicated generator trailer or next-generation vehicle with enhanced power generation. The US Army’s XM1299 Extended-Range Cannon Artillery (ERCA) programme considered a dedicated prime-mover for HEL power.

    32.8 Counter-DEW Measures¶

    32.8.1 Reflective Coatings¶

    High-reflectivity coatings reduce the fraction of incident laser energy absorbed. Gold (\(\rho_r \approx 0.98\) at 1  extmum) and silver (\(\rho_r \approx 0.96\) at 1  extmum) are effective. However, at a sustained irradiance of 100 W/cm\(^2\), even 2% absorption equates to 2 W/cm\(^2\) deposited — sufficient for thermal damage over time. Furthermore, ablation of the coating itself eventually exposes the substrate.

    32.8.2 Spinning and Tumbling¶

    Inducing spin or tumble in the target distributes the laser energy around the circumference rather than concentrating it at one spot. For a cylinder of radius \(r\) and surface thermal conductivity \(k\), the reduction in peak temperature rise is approximately:

    \[\frac{\Delta T_{\text{spin}}}{\Delta T_{\text{static}}} \approx \sqrt{\frac{\alpha_{\text{th}}}{r^2 \omega_{\text{spin}}}}\tag{32.36}\]

    At high spin rate the target effectively sees a time-averaged irradiance, reducing the peak temperature rise. This is particularly relevant for spin-stabilised rockets.

    32.8.3 Obscurants and Smoke¶

    Aerosol clouds of graphite, aluminium flakes, or metallic foil chaff scatter and absorb laser energy. Attenuation in a dense smoke cloud can exceed 10 dB (90% energy loss) per 100 m. The extinction coefficient of a cloud of spherical particles of radius \(a\), number density \(n\), and extinction efficiency \(Q_{\text{ext}}\) is:

    \[\mu_{\text{ext}} = n \cdot Q_{\text{ext}} \cdot \pi a^2\tag{32.37}\]

    Modern obscurant grenades (e.g., L8 on UK Challenger 2 MBT) deploy within 0.5 s and create a cloud effective against 1–12 \(\mu\)m wavelengths. However, obscurants also blind the target’s own sensors.

    32.8.4 Speed as a Counter-DEW Measure¶

    For a fast-moving target at velocity \(V_T\) at range \(R\), the beam slew rate required is \(\dot\theta = V_T/R\). At extreme approach velocities, dwell time is limited by the finite length of the engagement window. For a Mach 3 (1020 m/s) target crossing the laser’s lethal range from \(R_{\text{outer}} = 5\;\text{km}\) to \(R_{\text{inner}} = 1\;\text{km}\):

    \[t_{\text{window}} = \frac{R_{\text{outer}} - R_{\text{inner}}}{V_T} = \frac{4000}{1020} \approx 3.9\;\text{s}\tag{32.38}\]

    If the target requires 5 s of continuous dwell at the beam irradiance achievable at those ranges, the laser cannot complete the engagement — speed is an effective countermeasure.

    32.8.5 Ablative Shielding¶

    Ablative materials absorb laser energy through pyrolysis (chemical decomposition) rather than conduction. The ablation rate limits temperature rise:

    \[\dot{m}_{\text{abl}} = \frac{I \cdot A_{\text{spot}}}{L_{\text{abl}}}\tag{32.39}\]

    where \(L_{\text{abl}}\) (J/kg) is the ablation enthalpy (latent heat plus decomposition energy). Carbon–phenolic ablators have \(L_{\text{abl}} \approx 10^7\;\text{J/kg}\), providing effective protection at moderate fluences, at the cost of added mass.

    32.9 Future Directions in Directed Energy Weapons¶

    32.9.1 Multi-Kilowatt to Megawatt Scaling¶

    The path to megawatt-class solid-state HEL involves:

    1. Coherent Beam Combination (CBC): Phase-locking hundreds of fibre amplifiers; each fibre limited to \(\sim\)3–5 kW before nonlinear effects (stimulated Brillouin scattering) degrade beam quality;

    2. Spectral Beam Combination (SBC): Combining beams at different wavelengths with a diffraction grating; no phase-locking required; multi-wavelength output;

    3. Next-generation gain media: Tm-fibre (2 \(\mu\)m), Ho-YAG (2.1 \(\mu\)m) for atmospheric window matching.

    32.9.2 Tactical High Energy Laser (THEL) and IRON BEAM Succession¶

    Post-2025, the Israel MOD plans integration of Iron Beam into a multi-tier air defence architecture alongside Iron Dome, David’s Sling, and Arrow. The key technology push is to extend HEL range to 10–15 km while maintaining kill probability \(>\)95% against salvo attacks of 10+ simultaneous targets.

    32.9.3 DEW for Anti-Satellite (ASAT) Applications¶

    Ground-based DEW has been used for satellite dazzling (temporary blinding of imaging sensors) since the 1970s. Permanent damage to satellite solar panels or sensors requires fluences of \(>\)0.1 kJ/cm\(^2\) through the full atmospheric column (\(\sim\)80 km effective zenith path). Adaptive optics on large-aperture telescopes (1–4 m) can deliver this level under good seeing conditions.

    32.9.4 Integration with AI Fire Control¶

    Modern DEW fire-control systems incorporate AI-based algorithms for:

    • Real-time atmospheric propagation estimation (using guide-star or point-ahead angle data);

    • Predictive aimpoint selection (targeting the most vulnerable sub-system);

    • Multi-target prioritisation in swarm engagements;

    • Engagement authority management (human-machine interface for Rules of Engagement compliance).

    32.10 Summary¶

    Directed energy weapons represent a transformative technology in the aerospace and defence sector. High Energy Laser systems have crossed the threshold from laboratory demonstrations to operational deployment, with HEL weapons now defending naval vessels and fixed sites in multiple countries. High Power Microwave weapons have demonstrated the ability to non-kinetically neutralise electronics over wide areas. Both technologies address a critical cost asymmetry: a $5 laser shot versus a $100,000 missile interceptor fundamentally changes the economics of air defence against mass drone and rocket attacks.

    Critical remaining challenges are: improving atmospheric propagation in adverse weather; reducing SWaP (Size, Weight, and Power) of the power conditioning and thermal management systems; and achieving multi-target, multi-layer DEW architectures that can handle saturation attacks by large drone swarms. The next decade will likely see 300 kW–1 MW HEL systems deployed on major surface combatants, providing a qualitative step change in air defence capability.
