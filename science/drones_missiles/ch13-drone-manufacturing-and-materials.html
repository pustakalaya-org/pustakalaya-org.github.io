    Chapter 13: Drone Manufacturing and Materials¶

    13.1 Introduction to Drone Manufacturing¶

    The manufacture of unmanned aerial vehicles (UAVs) spans an exceptionally wide range of production philosophies, from small-batch artisanal builds in university laboratories to high-rate defence production lines delivering hundreds of airframes per year. Regardless of scale, the engineering discipline of drone manufacturing integrates materials science, structural mechanics, electronics, software, and quality assurance into a coherent production system. This chapter provides a rigorous, end-to-end treatment of that system, covering composite fabrication, precision machining, additive processes, electronics assembly, quality control, and the emerging digital-factory paradigm.

    A fundamental observation is that the choice of manufacturing route directly determines the achievable structural mass fraction, and therefore the payload-to-gross-weight ratio that defines mission utility. For a fixed-wing combat drone with a design gross weight \(W_0 = 500\,\mathrm{kg}\), a structural mass fraction \(f_s = m_s/W_0\) reduction from 0.32 to 0.27 frees 25 kg for additional fuel or payload — a 15% improvement achievable primarily through disciplined composite manufacturing.

    13.2 Product Lifecycle and Programme Management¶

    13.2.1 Phase Gates from Concept to Production¶

    The standard aerospace product lifecycle follows a phase-gate model aligned with the US DoD acquisition framework and the INCOSE systems engineering handbook. Each gate is a formal review at which the programme may proceed, loop back, or terminate.

    Concept Phase encompasses market or mission analysis, operational requirements, and rough order-of-magnitude (ROM) cost estimation. The output is a System Requirements Document (SRD) and a business case with a return-on-investment projection.

    Preliminary Design Review (PDR) validates that the selected design approach meets top-level requirements. Key deliverables include the preliminary 3-D model, initial weight budget, aerodynamic analysis (CFD or handbook methods), structural sizing, and power budget. Risk register is established with probability-impact matrices.

    Critical Design Review (CDR) examines the detail design. All drawings are released to revision A. Finite-element models (FEM) are correlated with preliminary test data. Manufacturing plans and make-vs-buy decisions are finalised.

    Prototype / Engineering Development Model (EDM) phase produces one to three flight articles built to production-intent processes. The EDM is used for flight-test envelope expansion and to validate manufacturing processes.

    Qualification and First Article Inspection (FAI) formally verify that the production design and process yield hardware that meets specifications. AS9102 governs FAI for aerospace structures.

    Low-Rate Initial Production (LRIP) ramps the production system while the qualification programme is completed. Typically 10–20% of the planned full rate.

    Full-Rate Production (FRP) follows a successful production readiness review (PRR). The learning curve drives unit cost down as cumulative output increases.

    13.2.2 Build-to-Print, Build-to-Spec, and COTS Integration¶

    Three procurement and manufacturing paradigms coexist in UAV programmes:

    Build-to-Print (BTP)
    The buyer provides fully dimensioned drawings; the supplier manufactures to those exact specifications. Intellectual property remains with the buyer. Used for structural components where geometry is performance-critical.
    Build-to-Spec (BTS)
    The buyer provides a performance specification; the supplier designs and manufactures to meet it. IP typically resides with the supplier. Common for motors, ESCs, and payload gimbals.
    COTS Integration
    Commercial off-the-shelf components are procured from catalogue and integrated without modification. Lowest NRE cost but supply-chain risk if the product is discontinued.

    13.2.3 Make-vs-Buy Analysis¶

    A rigorous make-vs-buy decision evaluates total cost of ownership (TCO), not merely unit price. The decision framework computes the net present value (NPV) of each option:

    \[\mathrm{NPV}_{\mathrm{make}} = -C_{\mathrm{NRE}} + \sum_{t=1}^{T} \frac{(P_{\mathrm{buy},t} - C_{\mathrm{make},t})\,Q_t}{(1+r)^t}\tag{13.1}\]

    where \(C_{\mathrm{NRE}}\) is the non-recurring engineering investment, \(P_{\mathrm{buy},t}\) is the per-unit buy price in period \(t\), \(C_{\mathrm{make},t}\) is the per-unit make cost, \(Q_t\) is the annual quantity, \(r\) is the discount rate, and \(T\) is the programme horizon in years. If \(\mathrm{NPV}_{\mathrm{make}} > 0\), internal manufacture is preferred on purely economic grounds, subject to strategic factors (IP retention, supply-chain security, export control).

    Key cost drivers for the make option include tooling, floor space, capital equipment depreciation, direct labour, material scrap, and quality overhead. For composites, tooling amortised over the production run is often the dominant NRE term.

    13.2.4 Quality Management: AS9100 and ISO 9001¶

    AS9100 Rev D is the aerospace quality management system (QMS) standard, building upon ISO 9001:2015 with additional requirements for configuration management, first-article inspection, risk management, and key characteristics. Certification requires a third-party audit and is mandatory for suppliers to prime aerospace contractors.

    Fundamental metrics within an AS9100 QMS include:

    • On-time delivery (OTD): target \(\geq 95\%\)

    • Incoming quality rate (IQR): incoming lots accepted / total lots

    • Defects per million opportunities (DPMO): Six Sigma target \(\leq 3.4\)

    • Cost of poor quality (CoPQ): internal failures + external failures + appraisal + prevention costs

    13.3 Composite Manufacturing¶

    Fibre-reinforced polymers (FRPs) are the dominant structural material in modern UAVs, offering specific stiffness and specific strength superior to all structural metals. Carbon-fibre-reinforced polymer (CFRP) achieves specific tensile modulus \(E/\rho \approx 110\,\mathrm{GPa\cdot cm^3/g}\) versus \(26\,\mathrm{GPa\cdot cm^3/g}\) for 7075 aluminium.

    13.3.1 Fibre and Matrix Systems¶

    Reinforcing fibres are characterised by their tensile modulus and strength. Standard-modulus (SM) carbon fibre (e.g. Toray T700) has \(E_f = 230\,\mathrm{GPa}\), \(\sigma_{fu} = 4900\,\mathrm{MPa}\), \(\rho_f = 1.80\,\mathrm{g/cm^3}\). Intermediate-modulus (IM) fibre (T800) achieves \(E_f = 294\,\mathrm{GPa}\). High-modulus (HM) fibre (M55J) reaches \(E_f = 540\,\mathrm{GPa}\) at the cost of reduced failure strain (\(\varepsilon_{fu} \approx 0.8\%\)) and increased brittleness.

    Epoxy matrices (e.g. Hexion EPON 862, Cytec 5320-1, Hexcel 8552) provide glass-transition temperatures \(T_g = 150\)–\(200\,^\circ\mathrm{C}\) in the cured state. Toughened epoxy systems with thermoplastic interleaves (e.g. Hexcel M21) raise mode-I interlaminar fracture toughness from \(G_{Ic} \approx 150\,\mathrm{J/m^2}\) to \(\geq 600\,\mathrm{J/m^2}\).

    The rule-of-mixtures longitudinal modulus of a unidirectional ply is:

    \[E_1 = V_f E_f + (1-V_f) E_m\tag{13.2}\]

    The transverse modulus is estimated by the Halpin-Tsai equation:

    \[E_2 = E_m \frac{1 + \xi \eta V_f}{1 - \eta V_f},\quad \eta = \frac{E_f/E_m - 1}{E_f/E_m + \xi}\tag{13.3}\]

    where \(\xi = 2\) for the transverse direction. Typical aerospace prepreg has fibre volume fraction \(V_f = 0.57\)–\(0.62\).

    13.3.2 Prepreg Layup Process¶

    Prepreg (pre-impregnated fabric or tape) is the highest-quality composite feedstock, with precisely controlled resin content (typically 34–38% by mass) and uniform fibre distribution. The process flow is:

    1. Tool preparation: Clean tool surface to \(Ra < 0.8\,\mu\mathrm{m}\). Apply release agent (Frekote 770-NC or Loctite Frekote 44-NC) in 3–5 coats, buffing each to a dry film. Apply peel ply if a bonding surface is required.

    2. Cutting: Automated ply cutting machine (Gerber, Lectra, Zund) cuts plies to nest patterns with \(<0.5\%\) material waste via optimisation algorithms. Manual cutting with template and rotary cutter is used for prototypes.

    3. Layup: Plies are applied sequentially. Each ply is consolidated by roller or squeegee to remove trapped air, particularly at ply drops and thickness transitions. Layup sequence follows laminate design code (e.g. \([0/\pm45/90]_s\) for quasi-isotropic laminates).

    4. Debulking: Every 4–6 plies, a vacuum of \(\geq 0.85\,\mathrm{bar}\) is drawn for 15–30 minutes to compact the laminate and remove interply voids.

    5. Vacuum bag assembly: Sequence from tool surface outward: release film / peel ply / breather cloth / vacuum bag / sealant tape. Bag leakage rate must be \(\leq 2.5\,\mathrm{mbar/min}\) before autoclave loading.

    6. Cure cycle: The standard two-dwell epoxy cure cycle for Hexcel 8552: ramp at \(1\)–\(3\,^\circ\mathrm{C/min}\) to \(110\,^\circ\mathrm{C}\), hold 60 min; ramp to \(180\,^\circ\mathrm{C}\), hold 120 min; cool at \(\leq 3\,^\circ\mathrm{C/min}\) to \(60\,^\circ\mathrm{C}\) before pressure release. Autoclave pressure: 7 bar.

    7. Demould and trim: Part is extracted after cooling. Trimming to net shape by waterjet, CNC router, or diamond-coated abrasive wheel.

    The degree of cure \(\alpha\) is tracked using an Arrhenius-based kinetic model:

    \[\frac{d\alpha}{dt} = A\,e^{-E_a/(RT)}(1-\alpha)^n\tag{13.4}\]

    where \(A\) is the pre-exponential factor, \(E_a\) the activation energy, \(R = 8.314\,\mathrm{J/(mol\cdot K)}\), and \(n\) the reaction order. Full cure (\(\alpha \geq 0.97\)) is verified by differential scanning calorimetry (DSC).

    13.3.3 Vacuum-Assisted Resin Transfer Moulding (VARTM)¶

    VARTM uses atmospheric pressure (one-sided vacuum) to drive resin through a dry preform. Darcy’s law describes the resin flow front velocity:

    \[v = -\frac{K}{\mu} \frac{\partial P}{\partial x}\tag{13.5}\]

    where \(K\) is the in-plane permeability (\(10^{-11}\)–\(10^{-9}\,\mathrm{m^2}\) for woven CF), \(\mu\) is the resin dynamic viscosity, and \(\partial P/\partial x\) is the pressure gradient. The flow front position for a constant-pressure one-dimensional case:

    \[x_f(t) = \sqrt{\frac{2K \Delta P\,t}{\mu\,\phi}}\tag{13.6}\]

    where \(\phi\) is the preform porosity and \(\Delta P\) is the driving pressure differential (\(\leq 1\,\mathrm{bar}\) for VARTM). Flow simulation tools (PAM-RTM, LIMS) optimise gate locations to prevent dry spots. Resin pot-life must exceed predicted fill time by a factor \(\geq 1.5\).

    13.3.4 Filament Winding¶

    Filament winding deposits continuous fibre tows over a rotating mandrel. The geodesic winding path satisfies Clairaut’s relation:

    \[r\sin\phi = r_0\sin\phi_0 = \text{const}\tag{13.7}\]

    For a cylindrical tube wound at angle \(\pm\phi\), the netting-theory hoop stress capacity is:

    \[\sigma_\theta = \frac{p r}{t} = \frac{n_f \sigma_{fu}}{2}\left(\sin^2\phi + \nu_{12}\cos^2\phi\right)\tag{13.8}\]

    Wet winding impregnates rovings in a resin bath immediately before deposition (\(V_f = 50\)–\(55\%\)); dry winding uses pre-towpreg (\(V_f = 58\)–\(62\%\), higher quality). Fibre tension \(T_f = 5\)–\(25\,\mathrm{N/roving}\) must be maintained \(\pm5\%\) across the winding cycle to avoid fibre waviness.

    13.3.5 Resin Transfer Moulding (RTM)¶

    RTM uses matched tooling with resin injected under pressure. The fill time equation for a rectangular cavity:

    \[t_{\mathrm{fill}} = \frac{\phi L^2 \mu}{2 K P_{\mathrm{inj}}}\tag{13.9}\]

    Injection pressures of 1–10 bar are typical for conventional RTM. High-pressure RTM (HP-RTM, \(>30\,\mathrm{bar}\)) enables cycle times of 3–10 minutes, increasingly applied to UAV production at volumes \(>1000\)/year.

    13.3.6 Automated Fibre Placement (AFP)¶

    AFP machines deposit slit tapes (3.175–12.7 mm wide) over complex double-curved surfaces. Each tow is cut, clamped, and restarted individually, allowing curvilinear “steered” fibre paths. The compaction roller applies \(100\)–\(400\,\mathrm{N}\) normal force.

    Common AFP defect types:

    Gaps
    Spaces between adjacent tows; limit \(<0.8\,\mathrm{mm}\), max 3 gaps/ply.
    Overlaps
    Tow overlap \(>0.3\,\mathrm{mm}\) creates thickness variation.
    Fibre waviness
    Tow steering radius \(<600\,\mathrm{mm}\) risks tow buckling.
    Twisted tows
    Tow twist \(>5^\circ/100\,\mathrm{mm}\) reduces compressive strength.

    13.3.7 Composite Tooling¶

    CTE mismatch between tool and part induces spring-in at cured corners:

    \[\Delta\theta = \theta_0\left[\frac{(\alpha_\perp - \alpha_\parallel) (1+\nu_{12})}{\cos^2\theta_0} \Delta T\right]\tag{13.10}\]

    where \(\theta_0\) is the nominal corner angle, \(\alpha_\parallel\) and \(\alpha_\perp\) are in-plane and through-thickness CTE values.

    13.4 CNC Machining¶

    13.4.1 Material Stock and Selection¶

    Aluminium alloy 7075-T651 is the workhorse machined structural material in UAV production: \(\sigma_{yt} = 503\,\mathrm{MPa}\), \(E = 71.7\,\mathrm{GPa}\), \(\rho = 2.81\,\mathrm{g/cm^3}\), machinability rating 8/10. Titanium Ti-6Al-4V (Grade 5) is used for highly loaded fittings and fastener bosses (\(\sigma_{yt} = 880\,\mathrm{MPa}\), \(\rho = 4.43\,\mathrm{g/cm^3}\)) despite higher cost and lower machinability rating 3/10. Engineering plastics (Delrin/POM, PEEK, UHMWPE) are machined for lightweight brackets, bushings, and fairings where structural loads are modest.

    13.4.2 Multi-Axis Machining Capabilities¶

    3-axis
    Tool moves in \(X\), \(Y\), \(Z\); suitable for prismatic parts with features on one face. Achieves tolerances \(\pm 0.05\,\mathrm{mm}\).
    4-axis
    Adds rotary axis \(A\) (rotation about \(X\)). Enables cylindrical parts.
    5-axis simultaneous
    Continuously varying tool orientation vector. Required for complex aerodynamic surfaces and undercut features. Tolerances \(\pm 0.01\)–\(0.02\,\mathrm{mm}\). Collision avoidance verified by CAM simulation (Mastercam, NX CAM, Hypermill).

    13.4.3 Cutting Parameters and Tool Wear¶

    The Taylor tool-life equation:

    \[V_c\,T_{\mathrm{tool}}^n = C\tag{13.11}\]

    where \(n \approx 0.25\) for uncoated HSS, \(n \approx 0.35\) for coated carbide. Recommended parameters for 7075 aluminium: \(V_c = 250\)–\(500\,\mathrm{m/min}\), feed per tooth \(f_z = 0.05\)–\(0.15\,\mathrm{mm}\), axial depth \(a_p = 0.5\)–\(5\,\mathrm{mm}\). For Ti-6Al-4V: \(V_c = 40\)–\(80\,\mathrm{m/min}\), \(f_z = 0.03\)–\(0.08\,\mathrm{mm}\), flood coolant mandatory, TiAlN-coated carbide.

    The material removal rate is:

    \[\dot{V} = a_p \cdot a_e \cdot f_z \cdot z \cdot n_{\mathrm{spindle}}\tag{13.12}\]

    where \(z\) is the number of cutter flutes and \(n_{\mathrm{spindle}}\) is spindle speed (rpm).

    13.4.4 Surface Finish and Post-Processing¶

    Aerodynamic external surfaces require \(Ra \leq 1.6\,\mu\mathrm{m}\); bearing surfaces \(Ra \leq 0.4\,\mu\mathrm{m}\). Hard anodising (MIL-A-8625 Type III) deposits an Al\(_2\)O\(_3\) layer 25–75 \(\mu\)m thick, hardness 400–600 HV. Type II anodising (5–25 \(\mu\)m) provides corrosion protection only. Alodine (MIL-C-5541 chromate conversion coating) is applied in thin film (\(<2\,\mu\mathrm{m}\)) for electrical conductivity continuity at bonding interfaces.

    13.4.5 Electrical Discharge Machining (EDM)¶

    Wire EDM uses a continuously moving brass wire (0.10–0.33 mm diameter) to erode material via spark discharge in deionised water. Material removal rate:

    \[\dot{m}_{\mathrm{EDM}} = \frac{E_d \cdot f_p}{H_w}\tag{13.13}\]

    where \(E_d\) is energy per discharge pulse, \(f_p\) is pulse frequency, and \(H_w\) is the specific erosion enthalpy. Achieves tolerances \(\pm 0.003\,\mathrm{mm}\); ideal for hard-metal spars, Ti landing-gear brackets, and airfoil templates.

    13.4.6 Waterjet Cutting¶

    Abrasive waterjet (AWJ) cutting uses a water-garnet slurry at 350–690 MPa through a 0.25–1.0 mm orifice. No heat-affected zone: critical for CFRP where heat causes delamination. Kerf width \(0.8\)–\(1.2\,\mathrm{mm}\); burr-free on metals. CFRP panel (3 mm thick) cutting speed: \(1500\)–\(3000\,\mathrm{mm/min}\) at 420 MPa.

    13.5.1 Technology Overview¶

    Additive manufacturing (AM) enables rapid iteration, complex geometry without tooling, and on-demand production of replacement parts in deployed environments.

    13.5.2 Fused Deposition Modelling (FDM/FFF)¶

    Interlayer bond strength is governed by the degree of polymer inter-diffusion, modelled by the reptation theory:

    \[\frac{\sigma_{\mathrm{weld}}}{\sigma_\infty} \propto \left(\frac{t_c}{\tau_r}\right)^{1/4}\tag{13.14}\]

    where \(t_c\) is the contact time above \(T_g\) and \(\tau_r\) is the reptation time. Faster printing (shorter \(t_c\)) yields weaker layer adhesion, explaining the empirical 60–80% bulk strength reported for FDM parts. Carbon-fibre-filled PETG (15–20% short fibre by mass) increases tensile modulus from 3.5 GPa to 8–12 GPa. Nylon PA12 and PA6 offer better ductility and environmental resistance than PLA for operational hardware.

    Warping of large FDM parts is controlled by a heated build platform (\(T_{\mathrm{bed}} = 60\)–\(110\,^\circ\mathrm{C}\)), a heated enclosure (\(T \approx 45\,^\circ\mathrm{C}\)), and brim/raft adhesion layers.

    13.5.3 Selective Laser Sintering (SLS) and Multi-Jet Fusion (MJF)¶

    SLS fuses nylon powder with a CO\(_2\) laser. PA12 properties: \(\sigma_{ut} = 48\,\mathrm{MPa}\), \(E = 1.7\,\mathrm{GPa}\), elongation 20%, \(\rho = 0.95\,\mathrm{g/cm^3}\). Parts are isotropic (\(\pm 5\%\) in XYZ) unlike FDM. MJF (HP) achieves surface finish \(Ra \approx 8\,\mu\mathrm{m}\) vs \(12\,\mu\mathrm{m}\) for SLS and faster throughput. Applications: flight control mounts, access panels, sensor housings, duct sections.

    13.5.4 Metal Selective Laser Melting (SLM/DMLS)¶

    Powder is fully melted by a focused laser (\(P = 200\)–\(1000\,\mathrm{W}\), \(d_{\mathrm{beam}} = 70\)–\(200\,\mu\mathrm{m}\), layer thickness 30–60 \(\mu\)m). The volumetric energy density governs densification:

    \[E_v = \frac{P}{v_s \cdot h_s \cdot t_l}\tag{13.15}\]

    where \(v_s\) is scan speed, \(h_s\) is hatch spacing, and \(t_l\) is layer thickness. Optimal \(E_v\) for AlSi10Mg: \(40\)–\(80\,\mathrm{J/mm^3}\). Mechanical properties of SLM AlSi10Mg (as-built): \(\sigma_{ut} = 400\)–\(430\,\mathrm{MPa}\), \(\sigma_{y0.2} = 230\)–\(270\,\mathrm{MPa}\), \(E = 70\,\mathrm{GPa}\), elongation 4–8%.

    Post-processing:

    • Stress relief anneal: \(300\,^\circ\mathrm{C}\)/2 h (AlSi10Mg); \(800\,^\circ\mathrm{C}\)/2 h (Ti-6Al-4V).

    • HIP (hot isostatic pressing): 150 MPa argon at \(920\,^\circ\mathrm{C}\) for Ti-6Al-4V; reduces residual porosity from \(<0.5\%\) to \(<0.1\%\); fatigue life improvement 20–50%.

    • Machining of critical interfaces to tolerance.

    • Surface finishing: bead blast \(Ra 4\)–\(6\,\mu\)m; electropolish to \(Ra 0.5\,\mu\)m.

    13.5.5 Continuous Fibre Reinforced AM (Markforged Process)¶

    The Markforged process co-extrudes a continuous fibre tow (carbon fibre, Kevlar, HSHT fibreglass) within a nylon matrix. Fibre volume fraction \(V_f \approx 35\)–\(40\%\). Predicted longitudinal modulus by ROM (Eq. (13.2)): \(E_1 \approx 0.38 \times 70 + 0.62 \times 2.5 \approx 28\,\mathrm{GPa}\). Measured \(E_1 = 54\,\mathrm{GPa}\) for CF-reinforced nylon, \(\sigma_{ut,1} = 800\,\mathrm{MPa}\), approaching aluminium in specific strength. Applications: structural ribs, wing spar inserts, and load-bearing brackets.

    13.5.6 Design for Additive Manufacture (DfAM)¶

    Key DfAM rules for UAV structural parts:

    • Self-supporting overhangs: angle from horizontal \(\geq 45^\circ\) for FDM/SLS; \(\geq 35^\circ\) for SLM.

    • Minimum wall thickness: 0.8 mm (FDM), 0.5 mm (SLS), 0.3 mm (SLM).

    • Lattice structures: gyroid, BCC, FCC unit cells reduce mass 30–60% at equal stiffness vs solid.

    • Topology optimisation (SIMP method):

      \[\min_\rho\; \mathbf{f}^T\mathbf{u}\quad \text{s.t.}\; V(\rho)/V_0 \leq f,\; 0 < \rho_{\min} \leq \rho_i \leq 1\tag{13.16}\]

    • Trapped powder: provide \(\geq 2\) mm drain holes in SLM internal cavities.

    Quality assurance for AM parts uses X-ray CT to map internal porosity; minimum detectable void \(\approx 100\,\mu\mathrm{m}\) at standard scan resolutions. Porosity limit for structural applications: \(<0.5\%\) by volume.

    13.6 Electronics Manufacturing¶

    13.6.1 PCB Design for UAV Applications¶

    Flight controller, motor controller, and communications PCBs must survive vibration, thermal cycling, and humidity of UAV operation.

    • Layer count: 4-layer minimum for digital logic; 6–16 layer for high-speed interfaces (PCIe, USB 3.0, MIPI CSI-2).

    • Controlled impedance: single-ended \(50\,\Omega\); differential pairs \(100\,\Omega\) (LVDS, USB 3.0) or \(90\,\Omega\) (USB 2.0); tolerance \(\pm 10\%\).

    • Dielectric: FR4 (\(\varepsilon_r = 4.2\)–\(4.8\)) for general digital; Rogers RO4003C (\(\varepsilon_r = 3.55 \pm 0.05\), \(\tan\delta = 0.0021\)) for RF traces at 2.4 GHz and above.

    • Thermal management: 2 oz copper planes; thermal vias (via-in-pad for QFN/BGA); aluminium-base PCBs for high-power motor controllers.

    • Trace current capacity per IPC-2152:

      \[I = k_1 (\Delta T)^{k_2} A^{k_3}\tag{13.17}\]

      where \(A\) is trace cross-sectional area, \(\Delta T\) is allowable temperature rise, \(k_1\), \(k_2\), \(k_3\) are empirical constants from IPC-2152 Table 6-1.

    IPC-6012 classification: Class 2 for general electronics; Class 3 (minimum annular ring 0.075 mm) mandatory for flight-critical avionics. Surface finishes: ENIG for BGA and fine-pitch pads; HASL for general through-hole; OSP for low-cost production.

    13.6.2 SMT Assembly¶

    The SMT assembly process:

    1. Solder paste print: Stainless steel stencil (100–150 \(\mu\)m thick); squeegee at \(60^\circ\), speed \(25\)–\(75\,\mathrm{mm/s}\). Solder paste SAC305 (96.5Sn/3.0Ag/0.5Cu), Type 4 powder (20–38 \(\mu\)m). Aperture area ratio \(> 0.66\) for clean paste release.

    2. Pick and place: Multi-head gantry machine (Yamaha, Fuji NXT, Juki); placement accuracy \(\pm 0.025\)–\(0.05\,\mathrm{mm}\); throughput 10,000–80,000 cph.

    3. Reflow: SAC305 profile: preheat ramp \(1.0\)–\(2.0\,^\circ\mathrm{C/s}\) to \(150\,^\circ\mathrm{C}\), soak 60–90 s, ramp to peak \(245\)–\(250\,^\circ\mathrm{C}\), time above liquidus (\(217\,^\circ\mathrm{C}\)) 30–60 s, cool-down \(<4\,^\circ\mathrm{C/s}\).

    4. AOI: Automated optical inspection checks solder bridges, missing components, and misalignment.

    5. X-ray: BGA and QFN solder joints; void content \(<25\%\) by area.

    13.6.3 Conformal Coating¶

    Conformal coating protects PCBs from humidity, salt fog, and contamination per MIL-I-46058C:

    AR (acrylic)
    Easy rework; \(-65\) to \(+130\,^\circ\)C; good humidity resistance.
    SR (silicone)
    \(-65\) to \(+200\,^\circ\)C; preferred for motor controllers.
    UR (polyurethane)
    Very good chemical resistance; harder than acrylic.
    XY (parylene)
    Vacuum-deposited, 5–30 \(\mu\)m, pinhole-free; best moisture barrier; requires specialised stripping for rework.

    Coating thickness: 25–130 \(\mu\)m single coat. IPC-A-610 Class 3: 100% coverage verified by UV fluorescence inspection.

    13.6.4 ESD Control¶

    ESD can destroy sensitive devices at voltages as low as 100 V (HBM). ANSI/ESD S20.20 ESD protection programme:

    • Wrist straps: resistance \(1\,\mathrm{M}\Omega \pm 10\%\) to ground; tested before each use.

    • Conductive flooring: \(10^6\)–\(10^9\,\Omega\) surface resistance.

    • ESD packaging: static-shielding bags (\(<10\,\mathrm{nJ}\) energy on contact).

    • Ionisers to neutralise insulator surfaces.

    13.7 Assembly Processes¶

    13.7.1 Assembly Sequence Planning¶

    The master assembly sequence (MAS) is developed using CAPP tools linked to the digital CAD model under a model-based definition (MBD) philosophy. A typical small-UAV assembly sequence:

    1. Structural sub-assemblies: fuselage shells bonded; cured under pressure fixture.

    2. Main spar installed; shear pins inserted and secured.

    3. Wing skins bonded to rib/spar skeleton; vacuum-bagged overnight.

    4. Landing gear and motor mounts installed; torqued per fastener schedule.

    5. Wire harness routed and secured; connector backshells torqued.

    6. Avionics bay populated: FC, GPS, IMU, radio, power distribution board.

    7. Propulsion: motor mounted, ESC installed, propeller balanced and attached.

    8. Final weight measurement and functional test.

    13.7.2 Fastener Installation¶

    Torque specifications use the nut-factor (K-factor) method:

    \[T = K \cdot F_i \cdot d\tag{13.18}\]

    where \(K = 0.15\)–\(0.25\) depending on thread finish and lubrication. Thread-locking compound (Loctite 243, medium strength) is applied to vibration-exposed fasteners. Safety wiring used on flight-critical fasteners per MS33540.

    13.7.3 Structural Bonding¶

    The peel stress at the adhesive bondline edge must satisfy:

    \[\sigma_{\mathrm{peel}} = \frac{M_e}{b\,t_a} \leq \frac{\sigma_{a,\mathrm{allow}}}{\mathrm{SF}}\tag{13.19}\]

    Surface preparation for aluminium: chromic acid anodise (CAA) or phosphoric acid anodise (PAA) + BR127 primer; bond strength \(>30\,\mathrm{MPa}\) lap shear. For CFRP: peel-ply removal immediately before bonding; light grit-blast (120 grit); wipe with acetone. Bondline thickness controlled by glass beads or shims (0.1–0.3 mm).

    13.7.4 Wire Harness Fabrication¶

    • Wire specification: MIL-DTL-22759 (PTFE-insulated, rated \(-65\) to \(+200\,^\circ\mathrm{C}\)); 30–40% lighter than PVC equivalent.

    • Connectors: MIL-C-26482 Series 1 (bayonet); MIL-DTL-38999 (environmental seal).

    • Shielded twisted pair for analogue signals; shield grounded at one end.

    • Minimum bend radius \(10\times\) conductor diameter; tie wraps at max 50 mm intervals.

    Voltage drop along a power run:

    \[\Delta V = I \cdot R = I \cdot \frac{\rho_e\,L}{A_w}\tag{13.20}\]

    where \(\rho_e = 1.72\times10^{-8}\,\Omega\cdot\mathrm{m}\) for copper, \(L\) is wire length, \(A_w\) is cross-sectional area. Maximum allowable \(\Delta V \leq 2\%\) of supply voltage at full load.

    13.7.5 Potting and Environmental Sealing¶

    Electronics are potted with two-part polyurethane (Dow Sylgard 170) or rigid epoxy (Emerson & Cuming Stycast 2651). Potting process:

    1. Pre-bake PCB at \(80\,^\circ\mathrm{C}\) for 2 h to remove absorbed moisture.

    2. Mix resin/hardener; vacuum degas (\(<1\,\mathrm{mbar}\), 5 min).

    3. Pour at \(5\)–\(10\,\mathrm{mm/min}\) to allow air escape.

    4. Cure at RT 24 h; post-cure \(80\,^\circ\mathrm{C}\)/2 h.

    Maximum operating temperature range: \(-55\) to \(+125\,^\circ\mathrm{C}\).

    13.7.6 Weight Budgeting and Mass Reduction¶

    The structural mass fraction:

    \[f_s = \frac{m_{\mathrm{structure}}}{m_{\mathrm{MTOM}}}\tag{13.21}\]

    Typical values: small multi-rotor \(f_s = 0.15\)–\(0.25\); medium fixed-wing \(f_s = 0.25\)–\(0.35\). Mass reduction techniques: replace aluminium brackets with CFRP (density 2.8 \(\to\) 1.6 g/cm\(^3\)), hollow titanium fasteners (HiLok), mouse-holing of non-load-path regions, and honeycomb sandwich panels in place of solid composite shear webs.

    13.8 Quality Control and Inspection¶

    13.8.1 Receiving Inspection and Traceability¶

    All incoming structural materials are accompanied by a certificate of conformance (CoC) and a certificate of test (CoT) traceable to a test batch. Prepreg is stored at \(-18\,^\circ\mathrm{C}\); cumulative out-time at room temperature tracked against a 30-day limit. Incoming lot sample plan: 10% per MIL-STD-1916.

    13.8.2 First Article Inspection (FAI) per AS9102¶

    AS9102 Rev B governs FAI for aerospace parts. Required documentation:

    1. Design documentation review (drawings, specs, deviations).

    2. Material and process certification review.

    3. 100% dimensional inspection of all drawing callouts.

    4. Functional test results.

    5. As-built weight.

    6. FAI report signed by QA and customer.

    Partial FAI (PFAI) required after any engineering change affecting form, fit, or function, and after a production break of more than 24 months.

    13.8.3 Non-Destructive Testing Methods¶

    UT C-scan
    Focused transducer scans the part in water immersion or squirter coupling. Maps disbonds, delaminations, and porosity. Lateral resolution \(1\)–\(3\,\mathrm{mm}\); sensitivity \(<0.5\%\) void volume.
    X-radiography
    Detects density variations (porosity, inclusions, solder voids). Real-time radioscopy for production screening.
    Flash thermography
    IR camera records thermal response after flash excitation. Disbonds (air cavities) appear as warm regions. Coverage rate \(0.5\,\mathrm{m^2/min}\).
    Dye penetrant (DP)
    Detects surface-breaking cracks in metal parts. Process: clean \(\to\) penetrant (15 min dwell) \(\to\) remove \(\to\) developer \(\to\) UV inspection.
    Eddy current (ET)

    Detects subsurface cracks and corrosion. Probe frequency \(f\) determines skin depth:

    \[\delta = \sqrt{\frac{1}{\pi f \mu \sigma}}\tag{13.22}\]

    Higher frequency = shallower penetration, higher surface resolution.

    13.8.4 Coordinate Measuring Machine (CMM)¶

    CMM probing with ruby-tipped probe (\(\varnothing\,1\)–\(5\,\mathrm{mm}\)) measures 3-D coordinates to \(\pm 1\)–\(3\,\mu\mathrm{m}\) (bridge CMM). GD&T callouts evaluated: flatness, circularity, true position, angularity, perpendicularity. Calibration traceable to national standards per ISO 14253-1.

    13.8.5 Functional Testing Protocol¶

    Prior to delivery each UAV undergoes:

    1. Motor run-in: 10%, 50%, 100% throttle for 2 min each; vibration and current monitored.

    2. ESC calibration: throttle range programmed; current limiting verified.

    3. IMU calibration: accelerometer, gyroscope, magnetometer.

    4. GPS lock verification: \(\geq 6\) satellites, PDOP \(< 3.0\).

    5. Control surface travel check: actuation angles and direction.

    6. Datalink check: uplink/downlink verified at maximum design range.

    13.8.6 Environmental Qualification (HALT and HASS)¶

    HALT (Highly Accelerated Life Test) applies stepped stress beyond specification limits: cold step stress \(-40\,^\circ\mathrm{C}\) to \(-80\,^\circ\mathrm{C}\) in \(10\,^\circ\mathrm{C}\) steps; hot step stress \(+60\,^\circ\mathrm{C}\) to \(+100\,^\circ\mathrm{C}\) in \(5\,^\circ\mathrm{C}\) steps; vibration step stress 5 Grms to 40 Grms in 5 Grms steps; combined temperature and vibration stress. HALT identifies design margins; HASS (Highly Accelerated Stress Screening) at reduced levels is used as a production screen.

    13.9 Production Scaling and Lean Manufacturing¶

    13.9.1 Lean Manufacturing Principles¶

    5S
    Sort, Set in order, Shine, Standardise, Sustain: organises the workspace to eliminate waste and reduce errors.
    Value Stream Mapping (VSM)
    Identifies all value-adding and non-value-adding steps. Target: reduce non-value-added time from 80–90% (batch) to \(<50\%\) (lean).
    Takt time

    The pace of customer demand:

    \[T_{\mathrm{takt}} = \frac{T_{\mathrm{available}}}{Q_{\mathrm{demand}}}\tag{13.23}\]

    Production cycle time at each station must \(\leq T_{\mathrm{takt}}\).

    Kanban
    Pull-based inventory control; eliminates excess WIP.
    Poka-yoke
    Error-proofing via asymmetric connectors, colour-coded wire insulation.

    13.9.2 Jigs and Fixtures¶

    Datum scheme per ASME Y14.5: primary (3 points), secondary (2 points), tertiary (1 point) datums uniquely locate the part. Jig accuracy \(\leq 1/3\) of the part tolerance: for \(\pm 0.5\,\mathrm{mm}\) wing attachment tolerance, jig accuracy \(\leq \pm 0.17\,\mathrm{mm}\). Modular fixturing systems (Bluco, Siegmund) allow rapid reconfiguration between variants.

    13.9.3 Supply Chain Management¶

    UAV supply chains: Tier 1 (system integrators, major sub-systems), Tier 2 (component suppliers: motor winders, PCB fabricators, composite houses), Tier 3 (raw material suppliers). ITAR/EAR compliance is mandatory; State Department licences required for ITAR-controlled items. Sole-source risk mitigated by qualifying \(\geq 2\) sources for each critical component.

    13.9.4 Learning Curve and Cost Estimation¶

    The Wright learning curve:

    \[C_N = C_1 N^{\log_2(p)}\tag{13.24}\]

    where \(C_1\) is first-unit cost, \(N\) is cumulative unit number, and \(p\) is the learning curve percentage (80% curve = 20% cost reduction per doubling of quantity). For UAV airframe assembly, \(p = 0.80\)–\(0.85\) is typical.

    The DAPCA IV manufacturing labour equation:

    \[H_{\mathrm{mfg}} = F_{\mathrm{diff}} \cdot 7.37 \cdot W_e^{0.82} \cdot V_{\max}^{0.484} \cdot Q^{0.641}\tag{13.25}\]

    where \(H_{\mathrm{mfg}}\) is total manufacturing hours, \(W_e\) is empty weight (lb), \(V_{\max}\) is maximum speed (knots), \(Q\) is production quantity, and \(F_{\mathrm{diff}}\) is a difficulty factor (\(1.0\) for aluminium, \(1.5\)–\(2.0\) for composite-primary structures).

    13.11 Emerging Manufacturing Technologies¶

    13.11.1 Generative Design and Topology Optimisation¶

    Generative design tools (Autodesk Fusion 360 Generative, nTopology, Altair Inspire) combine structural FEA with topology optimisation to produce geometries that cannot be conceived by manual design. Integration with metal SLM enables manufacture of these organic forms. A wing attachment bracket optimised by SIMP (Eq. (13.16)) achieved 38% mass reduction at identical stiffness compared to the machined aluminium baseline.

    The SIMP penalisation drives intermediate density elements to either 0 or 1:

    \[E(\rho_i) = \rho_i^p E_0, \quad p = 3\text{ (typical)}\tag{13.26}\]

    13.11.2 Model-Based Definition and Digital Manufacturing¶

    The MBD paradigm stores all product and manufacturing information in the 3-D CAD model:

    • PMI (Product and Manufacturing Information): GD&T, surface finish, material specifications embedded in geometry.

    • MBOM (Manufacturing Bill of Materials) linked to CAD; changes propagate automatically.

    • Digital work instructions: AR headsets display assembly steps projected onto the physical airframe (PTC Vuforia, Scope AR).

    • Paperless factory: zero paper drawings; all data accessed via tablets and AR.

    13.11.3 Robotic Assembly¶

    Collaborative robots (cobots: Universal Robots UR10e, KUKA LBR iiwa) equipped with force-torque sensors (\(<0.1\,\mathrm{N\cdot m}\) resolution) handle delicate composite panels. Applications: automated fastener installation (torque accuracy \(\pm 2\%\)), sealing bead application at constant pressure, and sub-assembly transfer via AMRs (autonomous mobile robots). Cobot payload capacity: 5–25 kg; reach: 850–1850 mm.

    13.11.4 Smart Factory and Industry 4.0¶

    IoT-enabled manufacturing equipment feeds a manufacturing execution system (MES):

    • CNC machine spindle load, tool wear, and part program identity logged per serial number.

    • Autoclave cure data linked to batch traveller; real-time cure kinetics model flags anomalies.

    • Overall Equipment Effectiveness (OEE):

      \[\mathrm{OEE} = \mathrm{Availability} \times \mathrm{Performance} \times \mathrm{Quality}\tag{13.27}\]

      Target: \(\geq 70\%\); world-class: \(\geq 85\%\).

    • Predictive maintenance: vibration and temperature sensors on spindles feed ML algorithms to predict remaining useful life.

    • Digital twin: virtual replica of the production line updated in near-real-time for scheduling, bottleneck analysis, and what-if studies.

    13.12 Environmental and Regulatory Considerations in Manufacturing¶

    Composite manufacturing generates hazardous waste (resin, solvents, hexavalent chromium from chromate conversion coatings) regulated under RCRA and REACH. Substitution of Cr\(^{6+}\) processes with trivalent chromium or sol-gel alternatives is an active area; MIL-DTL-81706B now lists trivalent Cr as an acceptable alternative under Type I treatment.

    PFAS (per- and polyfluoroalkyl substances) used in some release agents are under regulatory scrutiny (PFOA, PFOS phase-outs). Silicone-based release agents (Frekote series) are the current preferred alternative.

    End-of-life composite waste is difficult to recycle. Pyrolysis recovers carbon fibre at 50–70% of virgin fibre mechanical properties. Solvolysis in supercritical water or alcohol recovers higher-quality fibre but remains pre-commercial.

    13.13 Chapter Summary¶

    Drone manufacturing integrates a broad portfolio of processes spanning composite fabrication, precision machining, additive manufacture, electronics assembly, and systems integration, each governed by its own standards, process parameters, and quality criteria. The route from raw materials to a flight-qualified airframe demands rigorous process control at every stage, underpinned by a capable QMS and traceable NDT. As production volumes increase, lean manufacturing, automation, and digital-factory technologies will continue to reduce unit cost while improving consistency.

    Key performance benchmarks achieved by current best-practice drone manufacturing: structural mass fraction \(f_s = 0.18\)–\(0.28\) for CFRP airframes; void content \(<0.5\%\) in autoclave composites; PCB assembly DPMO \(<500\) with conformal coating coverage \(>99\%\); learning-curve exponents of 0.80–0.85 yielding 30–40% unit-cost reductions over the first 100 production units. Future trajectories point toward fully automated composite layup, in-process cure monitoring, and AI-driven quality control systems capable of detecting defects in real time and adjusting process parameters autonomously.
