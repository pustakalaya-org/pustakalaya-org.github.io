    Chapter 26: Missile Electronics and Power¶

    26.1 Electronics Overview and Avionics Bay Architecture¶

    Modern guided missiles are, in essence, precision electromechanical systems whose lethality is dominated by the sophistication of their electronics. From the moment the fire-control system closes the launch authority to terminal impact, a dense chain of sensing, computing, and actuation electronics must function flawlessly across temperature extremes of \(-55\,^\circ\text{C}\) to \(+125\,^\circ\text{C}\), under acoustic loads exceeding 165 dB SPL, and through shock inputs of 100–1500 \(g\). This chapter surveys the major electronic subsystems, their physical architecture, power conditioning requirements, computational platforms, data buses, thermal management, and electromagnetic compatibility (EMC) provisions.

    26.1.1 Avionics Bay Layout¶

    The avionics bay — also called the guidance section or electronics bay — is typically located immediately forward of the warhead section and immediately aft of the seeker dome. Packaging density is extreme: a 150 m m-diameter tactical missile may contain all guidance, navigation, control, and power electronics within a cylindrical volume of roughly \(150\,\text{mm} \times 400\,\text{mm}\).

    Principal layout philosophies include:

    Modular chassis with card-edge connectors. Each functional circuit (navigation processor, power converter, motor drive, FPGA DSP) resides on a standardised card that slides into an aluminium chassis rail. This mirrors aerospace practice derived from VMEbus or CompactPCI, modified for environmental ruggedness. Card pitch is typically 10 m m–15 m m.

    Stacked PCB assembly. For very small missiles (e.g., MANPADS, miniature precision munitions), individual PCBs are stacked along the missile axis, interconnected by flexible circuits or rigid-flex boards, and potted in syntactic foam for vibration isolation.

    Cold-rail chassis. An aluminium or beryllium–copper structural rail serves simultaneously as a structural member and a primary thermal conduction path, drawing heat from component hot-spots to an external heat sink or phase-change material absorber. This is the dominant architecture for AMRAAM-class missiles.

    The avionics chassis must withstand:

    • Axial acceleration at launch: \(20\)–\(60\,g\) (gun-launched rounds up to \(15{,}000\,g\))

    • Sustained boost acceleration: up to \(60\,g\) for \(2\)–\(4\,\text{s}\)

    • Vibroacoustic environment: \(20\)–\(2000\,\text{Hz}\), \(0.04\,g^2/\text{Hz}\) power spectral density

    • Thermal cycling: \(-55\,^\circ\text{C}\) to \(+85\,^\circ\text{C}\) storage; \(-40\,^\circ\text{C}\) to \(+70\,^\circ\text{C}\) operational

    26.1.2 Electromagnetic Interference Shielding¶

    Missile electronics occupy a uniquely hostile electromagnetic environment. They must receive coherent RF signals in the microvolt range while in close proximity to a high-power radar or datalink transmitter and a motor driver producing fast \(dV/dt\) edges. The governing standard is MIL-STD-461G, Requirements for the Control of Electromagnetic Interference Characteristics of Subsystems and Equipment.

    Key shielding provisions:

    • The outer skin acts as the primary Faraday cage; skin-to-bulkhead bonding resistance \(< 2.5\,\text{m}\Omega\).

    • Individual sub-enclosures (seeker electronics, power converters) are secondary shields, achieving \(\geq 60\,\text{dB}\) attenuation from 10 k H z to 10 G H z.

    • Cable penetrations pass through EMI filters (feed-through capacitors, ferrite chokes) mounted at the bulkhead.

    • Fibre-optic links replace copper for critical data paths traversing high-gradient EM fields.

    MIL-STD-704F specifies aircraft electrical power quality: frequency \(400\,\text{Hz}\pm5\,\text{Hz}\), voltage \(115/200\,\text{V}\) AC, \(28\,\text{V}\) DC; these specifications are the origin for the power conditioning requirements applied internally to the missile after it is powered from the platform umbilical prior to launch.

    MIL-STD-1553B is the primary data highway, operating at 1 Mbps on a dual-redundant twisted-shielded pair; it is detailed in Section 26.6.

    26.2 Power Systems¶

    A missile power system must deliver stable, conditioned electrical energy from the moment of launch through terminal guidance — a mission time spanning from \(<2\,\text{s}\) (short-range AAM) to several minutes (cruise missile). Three primary source technologies are employed: thermal batteries, turbogenerators, and silver-zinc batteries, often in combination.

    26.2.1 Thermal Batteries¶

    Thermal batteries are the universal choice for short-duration, high-power missile applications. They are inert at ambient temperature and are activated by a pyrotechnic heat pellet, reaching full operating voltage in fewer than 50 ms. The electrochemistry is typically lithium–aluminium anode / iron disulphide (\(\text{FeS}_2\)) cathode (Li-Al/FeS\(_2\)), with a molten salt (\(\text{LiCl}\)–\(\text{KCl}\) eutectic, melting point \(352\,^\circ\text{C}\)) electrolyte.

    Key performance parameters:

    • Specific energy: \(350\)–\(450\,\text{Wh/kg}\) (system level, \(\approx 400\,\text{Wh/kg}\) typical)

    • Specific power: up to \(1\,\text{kW/kg}\) for short-duration cells

    • Activation time: \(< 50\,\text{ms}\) (specification per NAVSWC TR 89-333)

    • Operating temperature: \(400\,^\circ\text{C}\)–\(550\,^\circ\text{C}\) internally (the missile electronics see regulated DC output, not this temperature)

    • Storage life: \(\geq 20\,\text{years}\) (inert, no self-discharge)

    • Mission duration: from \(2\,\text{s}\) to \(\approx 180\,\text{s}\) depending on cell stack size

    The available energy from a thermal battery stack of \(n\) cells, each of open-circuit voltage \(V_0\) and internal resistance \(r\), delivering current \(I\) is:

    \[E_{\text{avail}} = n\,\int_0^{t_f} \left(V_0 - I\,r(T,t)\right)\,I\,\mathrm{d}t\tag{26.1}\]

    where \(r(T,t)\) is the time- and temperature-varying internal resistance, decreasing rapidly after activation as electrolyte reaches full molten state.

    The terminal voltage regulation across the mission is:

    \[V_{\text{term}}(t) = V_0(t) - I(t)\,r(T,t)\tag{26.2}\]

    Battery designers specify a minimum terminal voltage \(V_{\min}\) at end-of-life (typically \(80\%\) of nominal) and a maximum terminal voltage \(V_{\max}\) at start of mission, both of which must be accommodated by the downstream power conditioning circuitry.

    26.2.2 Turbogenerators¶

    Longer-range missiles — cruise missiles, anti-ship missiles, long-range AAMs — require sustained electrical power for durations of \(60\,\text{s}\) to \(30\,\text{min}\) that is impractical from thermal batteries. Two turbogenerator architectures are used:

    Ram Air Turbine (RAT). A small turbine is deployed into the airstream, extracting kinetic energy from the dynamic pressure. Output scales with:

    \[P_{\text{RAT}} = \eta_{\text{RAT}}\,\frac{1}{2}\,\rho\,V_\infty^3\,A_{\text{disc}}\tag{26.3}\]

    where \(\eta_{\text{RAT}} \approx 0.25\)–\(0.35\) (Betz limit tempered by practical losses), \(\rho\) is air density, \(V_\infty\) is free-stream velocity, and \(A_{\text{disc}}\) is turbine disc area. A missile flying at Ma 0.8 at sea level achieves \(q \approx 38\,\text{kPa}\); a RAT of \(A_{\text{disc}} = 0.008\,\text{m}^2\) produces approximately \(1.5\,\text{kW}\).

    Exhaust-driven turbogenerator. In turbojet or turbofan-powered cruise missiles, a small auxiliary turbine taps bleed air from the engine compressor or the hot exhaust. Output range: \(200\,\text{W}\)–\(2{,}000\,\text{W}\) depending on engine size. This architecture is used in Tomahawk (Williams F107 engine, \(\approx 400\,\text{W}\) generation).

    The turbine power specific to a bleed-air generator is:

    \[P_{\text{bleed}} = \dot{m}_{\text{bleed}}\,c_p\,T_{t2}\left[1 - \left(\frac{p_3}{p_{t2}}\right)^{(\gamma-1)/\gamma}\right]\,\eta_{\text{mech}}\,\eta_{\text{gen}}\tag{26.4}\]

    where \(\dot{m}_{\text{bleed}}\) is bleed mass flow rate, \(c_p\) the specific heat at constant pressure, \(T_{t2}\) total temperature at extraction point, \(p_3/p_{t2}\) pressure ratio, and \(\eta_{\text{mech}}\), \(\eta_{\text{gen}}\) are mechanical and generator efficiencies respectively.

    26.2.3 Silver-Zinc Batteries¶

    Silver-zinc (Ag-Zn) batteries occupy a middle ground: higher specific energy than thermal batteries at system level for durations \(>120\,\text{s}\), pre-activated (electrolyte filled immediately before use or shipped wet-charged), and capable of high pulse currents. Specific energy: \(130\)–\(200\,\text{Wh/kg}\) (system level). They are used in early-generation AAMs (AIM-7 Sparrow) and some torpedo systems.

    26.2.4 Power Conditioning¶

    Raw output from any of the above sources is noisy, variable, and must be conditioned before distribution to sensitive electronics. The power conditioning unit (PCU) performs:

    • Step-down DC-DC conversion: input \(18\)–\(36\,\text{V}\) (thermal battery range), output \(+5\,\text{V}\), \(+3.3\,\text{V}\), \(+1.8\,\text{V}\) (digital logic), \(\pm 15\,\text{V}\) (analogue), \(\pm 28\,\text{V}\) (actuation bus)

    • PWM switching frequency: \(200\,\text{kHz}\)–\(2\,\text{MHz}\) (synchronised across channels to avoid intermodulation with IF frequencies in seeker)

    • Line regulation: \(\pm 1\%\) from no-load to full load

    • Transient response: \(< 5\,\text{ms}\) recovery to \(\pm 2\%\) for a \(50\%\) step load change

    • EMI filtering: LC low-pass filter at each converter output, \(C_{\text{bulk}}\) at input; ferrite beads at cable exit points

    The switching converter efficiency is characterised by:

    \[\eta_{\text{converter}} = \frac{P_{\text{out}}}{P_{\text{in}}} = \frac{V_{\text{out}}\,I_{\text{out}}}{V_{\text{in}}\,I_{\text{in}}} = 1 - \frac{P_{\text{sw}} + P_{\text{cond}} + P_{\text{core}}}{P_{\text{in}}}\tag{26.5}\]

    where \(P_{\text{sw}}\) are switching losses (proportional to \(f_{\text{sw}}\)), \(P_{\text{cond}}\) are conduction losses (proportional to \(I_{\text{out}}^2\)), and \(P_{\text{core}}\) are magnetic core losses.

    26.3 Guidance Computer¶

    26.3.1 Processing Architecture¶

    The guidance computer (GC) executes the navigation filter, guidance law, autopilot, seeker tracking loop, built-in test (BIT), and telemetry formatting — typically at a deterministic control loop rate of \(100\,\text{Hz}\)–\(1\,\text{kHz}\).

    Three processor families dominate military missile programmes:

    PowerPC (e.g., Freescale MPC8640D). RISC architecture, radiation-tolerant commercial variants available, used in AMRAAM, JASSM. Operates at \(1\,\text{GHz}\), dual-core, with integrated DDR2 controller. Radiation-hardened variants (e.g., BAE Systems RAD750) are derived from PowerPC 750 and provide \(<100\,\text{krad}\) TID tolerance.

    SPARC (e.g., Aeroflex UT699). SPARC V8 ISA, designed explicitly for space/missile radiation environments. Clock: \(66\,\text{MHz}\). Total ionising dose (TID): \(300\,\text{krad}\). Latch-up immune. Used in strategic long-range missiles.

    ARM Cortex-R (real-time) and Cortex-A (applications). The Cortex-R4F is used in tactical munitions for deterministic real-time control. The Cortex-A9 and A53 appear in programmable munitions with software-defined guidance. Radiation-hardened Cortex-R5 is available (STMicroelectronics STHV3200).

    FPGA co-processor. Virtually all modern missile guidance computers pair a central processor with one or more FPGAs for:

    • Seeker signal processing (pulse Doppler processing, angle tracking)

    • AESA radar waveform generation and beamforming

    • Navigation filter hardware acceleration (matrix multiply)

    • Interface logic for MIL-STD-1553, RS-422, SpaceWire

    The Xilinx (AMD) Kintex-7 and Kintex UltraScale+ are representative high-end FPGAs. For rad-hard applications, Microsemi/Actel ProASIC3 and RTAX families are used.

    26.3.2 Memory Architecture¶

    • EEPROM (mission data): stores flight plan waypoints, target coordinates, frequency plan, authentication keys. Write endurance: \(\geq 10^5\) cycles. Retention: 10 years. Size: \(256\,\text{kB}\)–\(4\,\text{MB}\). Example: Microchip AT28C256.

    • Flash (program store): stores the guidance software image, typically \(8\)–\(64\,\text{MB}\). Rad-hard NOR flash: Cypress GL512S18 series. Executed in-place (XIP) or loaded to SDRAM at boot.

    • SDRAM (computation): DDR2/DDR3 SDRAM at \(256\,\text{MB}\)–\(1\,\text{GB}\) for navigation filter workspace, radar data cube processing. Access latency: \(<10\,\text{ns}\).

    • Scratchpad SRAM: fast zero-latency workspace for inner control loops (\(64\,\text{kB}\)–\(512\,\text{kB}\)), radiation-hardened SRAM (e.g., Cypress CY7C1470) with single-event upset (SEU) scrubbing.

    26.3.3 Real-Time Operating System¶

    The RTOS provides deterministic scheduling, interrupt management, and inter-task communication. Common choices:

    • VxWorks (Wind River): preemptive priority scheduling, \(<1\,\mu\text{s}\) interrupt latency, certified to DO-178C DAL A, used in AMRAAM, Patriot PAC-3.

    • LynxOS-178: POSIX-compliant, ARINC 653 partitioning, used in avionics applications.

    • RTEMS (open-source, DARPA-funded): used in missile and spacecraft programmes. Supports PowerPC, SPARC, ARM targets.

    • Bare-metal with AUTOSAR-style scheduling: simple missiles use a cyclic executive with no OS overhead.

    The worst-case execution time (WCET) of the guidance loop must satisfy:

    \[T_{\text{WCET}} \leq T_{\text{loop}} - T_{\text{margin}}\tag{26.6}\]

    where \(T_{\text{loop}} = 1/f_{\text{control}}\) and \(T_{\text{margin}} \geq 20\%\) of \(T_{\text{loop}}\).

    26.4 Inertial Measurement Unit¶

    The IMU provides the six degree-of-freedom state (three-axis specific force, three-axis angular rate) that feeds the navigation filter. Requirements differ sharply by missile class.

    26.4.1 Fibre-Optic Gyroscope (FOG)¶

    The FOG exploits the Sagnac effect: counter-propagating light beams in a fibre coil of length \(L\) and radius \(R\) acquire a phase difference:

    \[\Delta\phi = \frac{8\pi\,N\,A\,\Omega}{c\,\lambda}\tag{26.7}\]

    where \(N\) is the number of turns, \(A\) is the coil area (\(A = \pi R^2\)), \(\Omega\) is the input rotation rate, \(c\) is the speed of light, and \(\lambda\) is the source wavelength.

    The Northrop Grumman LN-200 FOG IMU is the canonical tactical missile IMU: gyro bias stability \(< 1\,\text{deg/h}\), random walk \(0.05\,\text{deg}/\sqrt{\text{h}}\), accelerometer bias \(<0.3\,\text{mg}\). The LN-200 occupies a volume of \(\approx 737\,\text{cm}^3\) and weighs \(\approx 0.75\,\text{kg}\).

    26.4.2 Ring Laser Gyroscope (RLG)¶

    The RLG uses two laser beams circulating in opposite directions in a closed optical path; rotation causes a frequency difference (beat frequency) \(\Delta f\):

    \[\Delta f = \frac{4A}{\lambda\,P}\,\Omega\tag{26.8}\]

    where \(P\) is the optical path length. The Honeywell GG1342 RLG is representative of the class: scale factor stability \(<5\,\text{ppm}\), bias \(<0.003\,\text{deg/h}\), used in Patriot PAC-2, Standard Missile-3.

    RLGs have a lock-in problem at very low rotation rates, mitigated by mechanical dithering. FOGs do not have this limitation, making them preferable for newer designs.

    26.4.3 MEMS Gyroscopes and Accelerometers¶

    MEMS inertial sensors occupy \(<1\,\text{cm}^3\) and consume \(<50\,\text{mW}\), making them attractive for small munitions and roll-stabilised dispenser sub-munitions. Typical MEMS gyro performance: bias \(5\)–\(100\,\text{deg/h}\), ARW \(0.1\)–\(1\,\text{deg}/\sqrt{\text{h}}\). They are adequate for GPS-aided navigation or short-range (\(<20\,\text{km}\)) ballistic trajectories where navigation accuracy requirements are relaxed.

    MEMS accelerometers for short-range tactical missiles: bias \(1\)–\(10\,\text{mg}\). For strategic missiles, the Pendulous Integrating Gyro Accelerometer (PIGA) provides bias \(<10\,\mu g\) and is the sensor of choice in strategic ballistic missiles (Minuteman III, Trident D5).

    26.4.4 Thermal Stabilisation¶

    IMU bias is a strong function of temperature. The gyro bias thermal coefficient is typically:

    \[B(T) = B_0 + k_T\,(T - T_{\text{ref}})\tag{26.9}\]

    where \(k_T \approx 0.01\)–\(0.1\,\text{deg/h/}^\circ\text{C}\) for FOG and RLG sensors. IMUs are thermally stabilised using resistive heaters and proportional-integral (PI) temperature controllers to maintain the sensor at a constant \(T_{\text{ref}}\), typically \(+50\,^\circ\text{C}\). Warm-up time: \(2\)–\(15\,\text{min}\) for strategic-grade IMUs (unacceptable for quick-reaction aircraft-launched missiles, which rely on pre-warmed canister storage). Tactical missile IMUs accept the thermal uncertainty and calibrate in-flight.

    Navigation accuracy from a pure inertial system degrades with time according to the Schuler period and random walk; position error for a FOG IMU:

    \[\sigma_{\text{pos}}(t) \approx \frac{1}{2}\,B_{\text{acc}}\,t^2 + \frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}\,N_g\,g\,t^{3/2}\tag{26.10}\]

    where \(B_{\text{acc}}\) is accelerometer bias and \(N_g\) is gyro angle random walk coefficient.

    26.5 Digital Signal Processing¶

    26.5.1 Seeker DSP¶

    The seeker DSP performs matched filtering, Doppler processing, angle estimation, and target tracking. For a pulsed Doppler radar seeker:

    Range processing: the received pulse after dechirp (stretch processing for LFM waveform):

    \[s_{\text{IF}}(t) = A\,\exp\!\left[j2\pi\left(f_{\text{IF}}\,t + \frac{B}{T}\,\tau\,t\right)\right]\tag{26.11}\]

    where \(f_{\text{IF}}\) is intermediate frequency, \(B\) is chirp bandwidth, \(T\) pulse duration, \(\tau = 2R/c\) two-way time delay. An FFT of \(s_{\text{IF}}(t)\) yields a range spectrum where target bin \(k = \lfloor B\tau/\Delta f \rfloor\).

    Doppler processing: a coherent burst of \(N\) pulses at PRF \(f_r\) is Doppler-processed by a second FFT across slow time:

    \[v_r = \frac{f_D\,\lambda}{2}\tag{26.12}\]

    where \(f_D\) is the Doppler frequency bin and \(\lambda\) is wavelength.

    The Texas Instruments TMS320C66x (C66x) multi-core DSP is optimised for this processing: 8 cores, each delivering 32 GFLOPS SP, for a total of 256 GFLOPS at 1.25 GHz. The Xilinx Zynq UltraScale+ SoC (ARM Cortex-A53 + FPGA fabric) is increasingly used as a single-chip solution that unifies the GC processing and seeker DSP.

    26.5.2 Digital Beamforming for AESA Seekers¶

    Active electronically scanned array (AESA) seekers digitise received signals at each element (or subarray) and form beams in software. For an \(N\)-element linear array with element spacing \(d\), the array steering vector for angle \(\theta\) is:

    \[\mathbf{a}(\theta) = \left[1,\, e^{j\psi},\, e^{j2\psi},\, …,\, e^{j(N-1)\psi}\right]^T,\quad \psi = \frac{2\pi\,d\,\sin\theta}{\lambda}\tag{26.13}\]

    The beamformed output for steering angle \(\theta_0\) is \(y(t) = \mathbf{w}^H\,\mathbf{x}(t)\) where \(\mathbf{w} = \mathbf{a}(\theta_0)/N\). Adaptive nulling (MVDR/Capon beamformer) suppresses jamming: \(\mathbf{w}_{\text{MVDR}} = \mathbf{R}_{xx}^{-1}\,\mathbf{a}(\theta_0) / \left(\mathbf{a}^H\,\mathbf{R}_{xx}^{-1}\,\mathbf{a}\right)\) where \(\mathbf{R}_{xx}\) is the received signal covariance matrix.

    FPGA implementation processes real-time covariance matrix estimation and inversion for arrays up to 64 elements at PRFs of \(50\)–\(200\,\text{kHz}\).

    26.6 Data Buses and Communication Interfaces¶

    26.6.1 MIL-STD-1553B¶

    MIL-STD-1553B defines a command/response, time-division multiplexed, half-duplex serial communication bus operating at \(1\,\text{Mbps}\) on a shielded twisted pair (impedance \(70\)–\(85\,\Omega\)). The bus topology is dual-redundant (Bus A and Bus B); both carry identical traffic and the Bus Controller (BC) switches to Bus B on detected errors.

    Architecture roles:

    • Bus Controller (BC): one per bus; initiates all transfers; issues RT-to-RT, BC-to-RT (transmit), and RT-to-BC (receive) command words.

    • Remote Terminal (RT): up to 31 RTs per bus; each has a 5-bit address (0–30; address 31 reserved for broadcast); responds to BC commands.

    • Bus Monitor (MT): passive listener for test/telemetry; does not transmit.

    Word format: 20-bit Manchester II biphase encoded word = 3 sync bits + 16 data bits + 1 odd parity bit. End-to-end message latency: \(\leq 32\,\mu\text{s}\) for a 32-word message. Bit error rate: \(<10^{-7}\) (with hardware error detection).

    The 1553 message cycle rate in a typical missile application: guidance loop data (IMU, seeker angle) at 100 Hz; actuation commands at 200 Hz; telemetry at 50 Hz.

    26.6.2 Other Interfaces¶

    • ARINC 429: unidirectional, simplex, 12.5 or 100 kbps; label-based, 32-bit words; used for navigation sensor outputs in larger missiles with avionic heritage.

    • RS-422: balanced differential serial, up to 10 Mbps, point-to-point; used for seeker digital interface, GPS receiver, altimeter.

    • CAN bus (ISO 11898): up to 1 Mbps; used for non-flight-critical subsystems such as thermal battery monitor, fin actuator diagnostics; lower complexity than 1553.

    • SpaceWire (ECSS-E-ST-50-12C): up to 400 Mbps, synchronous serial, point-to-point or network; used for high-bandwidth seeker data transfer in imaging IR seeker interfaces.

    • Ethernet (10/100 GbE): emerging in ground test and some developmental flight hardware; not yet standard for in-flight missile buses due to non-determinism concerns.

    26.7 Thermal Management of Electronics¶

    Electronics in a sealed missile bay generate heat that cannot escape by natural convection (no airflow, sealed structure). The dominant heat transfer mechanisms are:

    1. Conduction through the PCB, mounting screws, and chassis rail to the missile skin.

    2. Radiation (minor contribution at the temperature deltas involved).

    3. Phase-change material (PCM) absorption — thermal energy is stored in the latent heat of fusion of a PCM (e.g., n-eicosane, melting point \(36.7\,^\circ\text{C}\), \(L_f = 247\,\text{kJ/kg}\)).

    The fundamental thermal resistance network from junction to ambient is:

    \[T_j = T_{\text{skin}} + P_d\,\left(\theta_{jc} + \theta_{cs} + \theta_{sa}\right)\tag{26.14}\]

    where \(\theta_{jc}\) is junction-to-case resistance, \(\theta_{cs}\) is case-to-chassis, \(\theta_{sa}\) is chassis-to-ambient (skin), and \(P_d\) is component power dissipation. The design limit is \(T_j \leq 125\,^\circ\text{C}\) for COTS/JAN Class B parts, \(\leq 150\,^\circ\text{C}\) for Class S (space grade).

    Heat pipe thermal management is increasingly applied in larger missiles: the heat pipe transports heat from the hot spot (FPGA, DSP) to a condenser plate at the missile outer wall with an effective thermal conductivity \(10\)–\(100\times\) that of copper, with no moving parts. Operating temperature range: \(-50\,^\circ\text{C}\) to \(+200\,^\circ\text{C}\) (ammonia working fluid for low-temperature ranges; water for ambient range).

    The heat pipe effective conductivity:

    \[Q_{\text{hp}} = \frac{k_{\text{eff}}\,A_{\text{cs}}\,\Delta T}{L}\tag{26.15}\]

    where \(k_{\text{eff}}\) can exceed \(10{,}000\,\text{W/(m\cdot K)}\) for ammonia heat pipes compared to \(400\,\text{W/(m\cdot K)}\) for copper.

    26.8 EMI Hardening and Radiation Hardness¶

    26.8.1 MIL-STD-461 Compliance¶

    MIL-STD-461G defines conducted and radiated emission/susceptibility requirements for equipment on military platforms. For missile electronics, the critical tests are:

    • CE102: conducted emissions on power leads, \(10\,\text{kHz}\)–\(10\,\text{MHz}\).

    • RE102: radiated emissions electric field, \(10\,\text{kHz}\)–\(18\,\text{GHz}\).

    • CS101: conducted susceptibility, power leads, \(30\,\text{Hz}\)–\(150\,\text{kHz}\) (for aircraft-mounted systems).

    • RS103: radiated susceptibility, \(10\,\text{kHz}\)–\(40\,\text{GHz}\); worst case: high-power radar on ship deck, field strength up to \(200\,\text{V/m}\).

    26.8.2 EMP Hardening¶

    Nuclear electromagnetic pulse (HEMP) produces an extremely fast field: rise time \(<2.5\,\text{ns}\), peak field \(50\,\text{kV/m}\) at line-of-sight from a high-altitude burst. Protection measures:

    • Shielded enclosures achieving \(\geq 100\,\text{dB}\) shielding effectiveness (all seams, penetrations continuously welded or gasketted with beryllium-copper EMI gaskets).

    • Metal oxide varistors (MOVs) and transient voltage suppression (TVS) diodes on all external cable entry points; response time \(<1\,\text{ns}\).

    • Fibre optic data links for signals between sections, eliminating antenna coupling on conductive runs.

    • Dielectric windows rather than metallic conduit for antenna feeds, with ferrite-loaded absorbers lining waveguide transitions.

    26.8.3 Nuclear Radiation Hardness¶

    Strategic missiles must function through the nuclear-radiation environment of a nuclear airburst. Critical effects:

    • Total Ionising Dose (TID): accumulated dose degrades gate oxide threshold voltage; radiation-hardened parts (RHBD — Radiation Hardened By Design) tolerate \(300\,\text{krad}\)–\(1\,\text{Mrad}\) (Si).

    • Single-Event Effects (SEE): heavy ions or protons strike a logic node causing single-event upset (SEU — bit flip), single-event latch-up (SEL — destructive), or single-event burnout (SEB). SEU mitigation: triple modular redundancy (TMR), EDAC (Error Detection and Correction) memory scrubbing at \(< 10\,\text{ms}\) intervals.

    • Dose-Rate Effects: prompt radiation from a nearby burst causes transient photocurrents in all junctions; current-limiting resistors and bulk CMOS logic designs (not bipolar) are preferred.

    Radiation hardness requirements for a strategic ICBM guidance electronics package are typically:

    • TID: \(\geq 1\,\text{Mrad(Si)}\)

    • SEU cross-section: \(< 10^{-10}\,\text{cm}^2/\text{bit}\) at LET \(80\,\text{MeV\,cm}^2/\text{mg}\)

    • Dose rate: \(10^{10}\,\text{rad(Si)/s}\) survival

    Conformal coating (acrylic, silicone, polyurethane to IPC-CC-830) protects PCBs from condensation, fungal growth, and partial radiation shielding.

    26.9 Datalink Integration¶

    Many modern missiles are equipped with two-way datalinks enabling mid-course target update, man-in-the-loop control, and post-launch retargeting. The datalink architecture varies by missile class.

    26.9.1 Two-Way Datalink¶

    Missiles such as AMRAAM (AIM-120D) and Brimstone carry a two-way datalink:

    • Uplink (platform-to-missile): target position update (inertial coordinates), mid-course guidance corrections, terminal phase enable, waypoint updates. Bit rate: \(1\)–\(100\,\text{kbps}\).

    • Downlink (missile-to-platform): seeker status, current position/velocity, BIT health, handover request. Bit rate: \(1\)–\(100\,\text{kbps}\).

    The link budget for the uplink:

    \[\text{SNR}_{\text{uplink}} = \frac{P_t\,G_t\,G_r\,\lambda^2}{(4\pi R)^2\,k_B\,T_s\,B_n\,L_{\text{sys}}}\tag{26.16}\]

    where \(P_t\) is transmit power, \(G_t\)/\(G_r\) are transmit/receive antenna gains, \(R\) is range, \(k_B\) is Boltzmann’s constant, \(T_s\) system noise temperature, \(B_n\) noise bandwidth, and \(L_{\text{sys}}\) system losses.

    26.9.2 LOAL and Lock-On Before Launch (LOBL)¶

    Lock-On Before Launch (LOBL): seeker acquires and tracks target before launch; pilot must see the target. Classic fire-and-forget for short-range AAMs (AIM-9L/M).

    Lock-On After Launch (LOAL): missile is launched without seeker lock; mid-course inertial/datalink guidance positions the missile for seeker acquisition in the terminal phase. LOAL-Direct (LOAL-D) and LOAL-Indirect (LOAL-I) describe whether the missile has a direct or indirect line of sight on launch. LOAL is essential for beyond-visual-range (BVR) shots and is standard on AMRAAM, Meteor, Aster.

    26.9.3 GPS Integration¶

    GPS provides one-way position updates for cruise missiles and GPS/INS hybrid guidance. The GPS receiver outputs position and velocity at 1–10 Hz; these are fed into the navigation Kalman filter to reset accumulated IMU drift. Anti-jam provisions:

    • Controlled reception pattern antenna (CRPA): null-steering antenna array rejects jammers with \(25\)–\(40\,\text{dB}\) rejection.

    • Selective Availability Anti-Spoofing Module (SAASM): military P(Y) and M-code reception.

    • Inertial coasting: missile continues on pure INS if GPS is jammed; accuracy degrades at the IMU drift rate.

    The GPS/INS coupled position estimation:

    \[\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{k|k} = \hat{\mathbf{x}}_{k|k-1} + \mathbf{K}_k\,\left(\mathbf{z}_k - \mathbf{H}\,\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{k|k-1}\right)\tag{26.17}\]

    where \(\mathbf{K}_k = \mathbf{P}_{k|k-1}\,\mathbf{H}^T\,\left(\mathbf{H}\,\mathbf{P}_{k|k-1}\,\mathbf{H}^T + \mathbf{R}\right)^{-1}\) is the Kalman gain.

    26.10 Reliability and Testing¶

    26.10.1 MTBF Prediction¶

    Reliability prediction follows MIL-HDBK-217F (Notice 2), Reliability Prediction of Electronic Equipment. The parts count method gives a system failure rate:

    \[\lambda_{\text{sys}} = \sum_{i=1}^{N} N_i\,\lambda_{b,i}\,\pi_{Q,i}\,\pi_{E,i}\tag{26.18}\]

    where \(\lambda_{b,i}\) is the base failure rate for component \(i\), \(\pi_{Q,i}\) is the quality factor (Class S parts: \(\pi_Q = 0.25\); COTS: \(\pi_Q = 10\)), and \(\pi_{E,i}\) is the environment factor (\(\pi_E = 4\) for airborne, inhabited; \(\pi_E = 8\) for airborne, uninhabited). \(\text{MTBF} = 1/\lambda_{\text{sys}}\).

    26.10.2 Component Derating¶

    Components are derated to reduce operating stress below rated values, following MIL-STD-975 and NASA EEE-INST-002:

    • Resistors: operate at \(\leq 50\%\) rated power.

    • Capacitors: operate at \(\leq 50\%\) rated voltage (electrolytic: \(\leq 60\%\)).

    • Semiconductors: junction temperature \(\leq 110\,^\circ\text{C}\) (Class S parts rated to \(125\,^\circ\text{C}\)).

    • Switching transistors: \(V_{DS}\) or \(V_{CE}\) at \(\leq 70\%\) rated value.

    26.10.3 Burn-In and Screening¶

    Individual ICs are burned in at \(125\,^\circ\text{C}\) for \(168\,\text{h}\) to remove infant mortality failures (early life of the bathtub reliability curve). Assembled PCBs undergo environmental stress screening (ESS):

    • Thermal cycling: \(-55\,^\circ\text{C}\) to \(+85\,^\circ\text{C}\), 10 cycles, rate \(\geq 5\,^\circ\text{C}/\text{min}\).

    • Random vibration: \(0.04\,g^2/\text{Hz}\), \(20\)–\(2000\,\text{Hz}\), \(\geq 5\,\text{min}\) per axis.

    • Functional test at temperature extremes.

    Highly Accelerated Life Testing (HALT) pushes beyond design margins (temperature: \(-100\,^\circ\text{C}\) to \(+200\,^\circ\text{C}\); vibration: \(>50\,g_{\text{rms}}\)) to precipitate design weaknesses rapidly. HASS (Highly Accelerated Stress Screening) applies a less aggressive HALT-like screen in production.

    26.10.4 Storage Life¶

    Arrhenius model for chemical/thermal degradation of non-metallic materials (conformal coat, potting, O-rings):

    \[t_{\text{life}}(T_{\text{storage}}) = A\,\exp\!\left(\frac{E_a}{k_B\,T_{\text{storage}}}\right)\tag{26.19}\]

    where \(E_a\) is the activation energy (\(\approx 0.7\)–\(1.1\,\text{eV}\) for typical elastomers), \(k_B = 8.617\times10^{-5}\,\text{eV/K}\), and \(T_{\text{storage}}\) is absolute storage temperature. Accelerated aging at \(+70\,^\circ\text{C}\) for 1000 h is used to verify 20-year storage life at \(+20\,^\circ\text{C}\). Electronic components with solid-state construction have inherently longer storage life; electrolytic capacitors and batteries are the dominant life-limiters.

    26.10.5 Summary¶

    Missile electronics engineering is a discipline defined by extremes: extreme packaging density, extreme environmental conditions, extreme reliability demands for a one-shot system, and extreme performance requirements from guidance computers that must execute advanced tracking algorithms in microseconds. The convergence of COTS-heritage FPGA/SoC technology with radiation-hardened, mil-qualified packaging is the dominant trend, enabling unprecedented guidance precision while containing the non-recurring engineering cost. The data bus ecosystem centred on MIL-STD-1553B has proven remarkably durable and will coexist with SpaceWire and fibre Ethernet as bandwidth demands escalate with AESA seekers and synthetic aperture modes. Power system design remains dominated by the thermal battery for tactical systems, with turbogenerators reserving their role for sustained-cruise applications. Reliability assurance through HALT, HASS, and Arrhenius-validated storage life remains the cornerstone of programmatic risk reduction for missile electronics programmes.
