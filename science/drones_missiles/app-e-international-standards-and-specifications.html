    Appendix E: International Standards and Specifications¶

    E.1 International Standards and Specifications¶

    This appendix consolidates the principal international standards, military specifications, regulatory frameworks, and treaty obligations that govern the design, manufacturing, testing, export, operation, and certification of unmanned aerial systems (UAS), guided missiles, and associated subsystems. Engineers working in this domain must navigate a complex, multi-layered regulatory environment spanning national defence ministries, civil aviation authorities, international bodies, and treaty verification regimes. The material is organised into nine thematic sections followed by a master index of all referenced documents.

    Conventions used in this appendix: Columns labelled Applicability use the shorthand: UAV = unmanned aerial vehicle / UAS; MSL = guided missile / munition; AVN = manned aviation / avionics; SYS = system-level / cross-domain; MFG = manufacturing / quality; ECO = export / treaty.

    E.2 US Military Standards (MIL-STDs and MIL-HDBKs)¶

    Military standards promulgated by the US Department of Defense (DoD) and its predecessor agencies constitute the foundational technical baseline for virtually all Western defence programmes. They are maintained and distributed through the Defense Standardization Program Office (DSPO) and are publicly available via the DLA (Defense Logistics Agency) Assist QuickSearch database. Compliance with applicable MIL-STDs is typically invoked through the Statement of Work (SOW) and Contract Data Requirements List (CDRL) of DoD acquisition contracts, and increasingly adopted voluntarily by allied nations and commercial aerospace primes.

    E.3 NATO Standards (STANAGs)¶

    NATO Standardisation Agreements (STANAGs) are agreed upon by member nations through the NATO Standardisation Office (NSO) and the relevant NATO bodies (NAAG, NAEW, NAFAG, NNAG). STANAGs are ratified at the national level; the implementation obligation depends on the ratification status of each member nation. Allied Engineering Publications (AEPs), Allied Ordnance Publications (AOPs), and Allied Technical Publications (ATPs) provide detailed technical guidance subordinate to STANAGs.

    Table E.4. Key NATO STANAGs for UAS and Missile Systems
    STANAG Title Ed. Scope Appl.
    4586 UAS Control System (UCS) Architecture 4 Defines standard UCS architecture enabling interoperability between NATO UAS and ground control stations; five levels of interoperability (LOI 1–5); data link interfaces; C2 messages (STANAG 4586 DLI); handover protocols. UAV
    4671 Unmanned Aerial Vehicles Airworthiness Requirements (USAR) 1 Military airworthiness requirements for fixed-wing UAS with MTOM \(>\)150 kg; structural, propulsion, flight performance, FCS, EMC, reliability; structured parallel to CS-23. UAV

    4439 Policy for Introduction and Assessment of Insensitive Munitions 3 IM policy framework: slow cook-off, fast cook-off, bullet impact, fragment impact, shaped charge jet tests (STANAG 4496); threat risk analysis; IM statement required in all new munitions programmes. MSL

    E.4 Aviation Regulatory Standards (FAA, EASA, ICAO)¶

    Civil aviation authorities regulate unmanned systems both in dedicated UAS regulations and through adaptation of existing manned aviation frameworks. The three-tier structure (ICAO standards and recommended practices \(\to\) regional regulations \(\to\) national rules) governs how drone operators obtain approval to fly and how manufacturers certify their products.

    E.5.4 Data Bus Standards¶

    ARINC 429.

    ARINC 429 (Mark 33 DITS) is the dominant avionics data bus for civil aircraft and commercial UAS avionics. It is a unidirectional serial bus: one transmitter to up to 20 receivers on a twisted-shielded pair. Two speeds: low speed (12.5 kbps) and high speed (100 kbps). Words are 32 bits wide: 8-bit label (octal, identifies parameter type), 2-bit source-destination identifier (SDI), 19-bit data (BNR or BCD format), 2-bit sign/status matrix (SSM), and 1-bit odd parity. Each label word is transmitted continuously on a loop or as required. Typical avionics using ARINC 429: AHRS, ADC, FMS, DME/VOR, ILS, TCAS, GPWS.

    ARINC 664 (AFDX — Avionics Full-Duplex Switched Ethernet).

    Defined in ARINC Specification 664 Part 7, AFDX is based on IEEE 802.3 Ethernet but adds determinism through Virtual Links (VLs). Each VL has a guaranteed bandwidth allocation (BAG — Bandwidth Allocation Gap: 1–128 ms) and a maximum frame size (1518 bytes). The network uses Commercial Off-the-Shelf (COTS) 100BASE-TX switches with traffic shaping at the End Systems. Latency is bounded and deterministic, unlike standard Ethernet. Used on Airbus A380/A350, Boeing 787, Eurofighter Typhoon upgrades. Bandwidth: 100 Mbps full-duplex per link; 1 Gbps variants emerging.

    MIL-STD-1553B.

    See Section E.2. The 1553B bus is also specified under AS15531 (SAE equivalent) and DEF STAN 00-18 (UK). A bus segment may have up to 31 Remote Terminals (RT addresses 0–30; address 31 is broadcast-only). Message types: BC-to-RT (command data); RT-to-BC (status + data); RT-to-RT; mode codes (synchronise, transmitter shutdown, initiate self-test, etc.). Typical transfer latency: \(<1\) ms per transaction; maximum bus loading \(\sim\)75% of 1 Mbit/s. Dual-bus redundancy ensures \(P_\mathrm{loss} < 10^{-9}\)/hr for interconnect.

    E.7 Testing Standards¶

    E.7.1 DEF STAN 00-970: UK Military Airworthiness¶

    British Defence Standard 00-970, Design and Airworthiness Requirements for Service Aircraft, is the UK Military Aviation Authority (MAA) technical standard for military aircraft including unmanned systems. It is structured as a series of Leaflets grouped into Sections:

    • Section 1: Definitions and general requirements (Leaflets 1/1–1/5).

    • Section 2: Structural requirements (Leaflets 2/1–2/15); design loads, fatigue, damage tolerance; similar to JSSG-2006.

    • Section 3: Propulsion (Leaflets 3/1–3/9); engine installation, intake, exhaust, fire protection.

    • Section 4: Systems and equipment (Leaflets 4/1–4/18); hydraulics, electrical, fuel, FCS, avionics.

    • Section 5: Performance and handling qualities (Leaflets 5/1–5/7).

    • Section 6: Operations (Leaflets 6/1–6/4); extreme cold/hot weather.

    • Section 7: Flight testing (Leaflets 7/1–7/5).

    • Section 8: Rotary-wing-specific (Leaflets 8/1–8/12).

    • Section 9: Unmanned aircraft (Leaflets 9/1–9/7); command link, emergency recovery, autonomous operations.

    The MAA’s Regulatory Article (RA) 5841 maps DEF STAN 00-970 to unmanned aircraft categories (Category A \(>\)20 kg; Category B 4–20 kg; Category C \(<\)4 kg) with proportionate requirements.

    E.7.2 NATO AEP-7: Aerial Target Systems¶

    NATO Allied Engineering Publication AEP-7 provides standardisation guidance for aerial target drones used in weapon systems evaluation and crew training. It defines target drone performance envelopes (speed, altitude, manoeuvre), radar cross-section (RCS) augmentation devices, infrared signature enhancement, miss-distance indication (MDI) systems, telemetry, and safety systems (range safety commands, flight termination). Member nations use AEP-7 to ensure that missiles fired at targets from different NATO nations produce comparable evaluation data.

    E.7.3 STANAG 4187: Fuze Safety and Arming Testing¶

    STANAG 4187 (Edition 2) specifies the environmental and functional test sequence for fuze safe-and-arm (S&A) devices before and after environmental conditioning. The test sequence is:

    1. Visual inspection and dimensional check.

    2. Drop test: 1.2 m onto concrete in six orientations.

    3. Vibration per STANAG 4370 (equivalent to MIL-STD-810): 5–500 Hz; 0.1g²/Hz.

    4. Temperature cycling: \(-54^{\circ}\)C to \(+71^{\circ}\)C; 5 cycles.

    5. Humidity: 95% RH; \(38^{\circ}\)C; 24 h.

    6. Arming function test: verify arming delay \(\geq\)0.3 s post-launch.

    7. Safe separation test: verify fuze safe at launch velocity + 10%.

    8. Statistical acceptance: 100 samples; \(P_a \geq 0.99\) at 90% confidence.

    E.8.2 Export Administration Regulations (EAR) and ECCN Codes¶

    The EAR, administered by the Bureau of Industry and Security (BIS), controls dual-use goods on the Commerce Control List (CCL). Each item receives an Export Control Classification Number (ECCN) structured as: Category (0–9) + Product Group (A–E) + Reason for Control. Key drone-related ECCNs include:

    • 7A003: Military aircraft navigation equipment (gyros, INS).

    • 7A004: Sun sensors; star trackers; attitude determination equipment.

    • 7D003: Software specially designed for inertial navigation systems.

    • 7E101: Technology for development or production of 7A003 items.

    • 9A012: Remotely piloted vehicles with \(>10\ \mathrm{kN}\) payload capacity, range \(>300\ \mathrm{km}\), or specific guidance accuracy — AT, NS, MT controls.

    • 9A120: Unmanned aerial vehicles capable of delivering CBRN weapons.

    • 9E101: Technology for development of items in 9A012.

    E.8.3 Missile Technology Control Regime (MTCR)¶

    The MTCR (established 1987; 35 member states as of 2024) restricts the export of missiles and related technology capable of delivering weapons of mass destruction. Items are categorised:

    Category I (strong presumption of denial): Missiles or other unmanned delivery systems capable of delivering a payload of \(\geq 500\ \mathrm{kg}\) to a range of \(\geq 300\ \mathrm{km}\); complete rocket systems; production facilities.

    Category II (case-by-case review): Components and sub-systems usable in Category I systems but with non-WMD uses: propellants; propulsion components; flight instruments; guidance components; structural materials; launchers; re-entry vehicles; computers and electronics; test equipment.

    Member states include: Australia, Austria, Belgium, Canada, Czech Republic, Denmark, Finland, France, Germany, Greece, Hungary, Iceland, Ireland, Italy, Japan, Luxembourg, Netherlands, New Zealand, Norway, Poland, Portugal, Republic of Korea, South Africa, Spain, Sweden, Switzerland, Turkey, Ukraine, United Kingdom, and United States.

    E.8.4 Wassenaar Arrangement¶

    The Wassenaar Arrangement (1996; 42 participating states) controls exports of conventional arms and dual-use goods and technologies. Two control lists: Munitions List (ML): military items (ML10 for military aircraft including UAS); Dual-Use List: nine categories of sensitive technologies. Relevant items for UAS and missiles: ML4 (bombs, missiles, torpedoes), ML10 (aircraft and UAS), ML11 (electronic equipment for military use), Category 7 (navigation and avionics), Category 9 (aerospace and propulsion).

    E.8.5 New START Treaty¶

    The New Strategic Arms Reduction Treaty (signed 2010; extended 2021; currently suspended by Russia 2023) limits US and Russian strategic nuclear forces. Verification provisions have technical implications for missile design documentation:

    • Deployed warhead limit: 1,550 per side.

    • Deployed delivery vehicle limit: 700 ICBMs, SLBMs, and heavy bombers.

    • Total launcher limit: 800 (deployed + non-deployed).

    • Telemetry exchanges: each side broadcasts telemetry on 5 ICBM/SLBM tests per year; data exchanged annually.

    • On-site inspections: 18 per year (Type 1: deployed systems sites; Type 2: non-deployed systems sites).

    • Unique identifiers (UIDs): each ICBM, SLBM, and heavy bomber assigned a unique number for tracking.

    E.8.6 Convention on Certain Conventional Weapons (CCW)¶

    The CCW (1980, Geneva) contains protocols relevant to missile and munition design:

    • Protocol II (Amended 1996): Restrictions on use of mines, booby traps, and other devices; technical requirements for anti-personnel mines (detectability; self-neutralisation after 120 days; self-destruction after 30 days).

    • Protocol V (2003) — Explosive Remnants of War: post-conflict responsibilities to clear UXO; record-keeping on cluster munition delivery; risk education; victim assistance. Directly affects submunition design (self-destruct mechanisms required; dud rate \(<1\%\)).

    • Group of Governmental Experts on LAWS: ongoing discussions (2014–present) on Lethal Autonomous Weapons Systems; meaningful human control; accountability gap; preambular language under consideration for new protocol.

    E.10.2 JARUS SORA v2.0 Methodology¶

    The Specific Operations Risk Assessment (SORA), developed by JARUS (Joint Authorities for Rulemaking on Unmanned Systems) and adopted by EASA for the Specific category, is a structured ten-step risk assessment process:

    1. ConOps definition: Precise description of the operation (airspace, UAS type, population density, environmental conditions).

    2. Intrinsic Ground Risk Class (GRC): Derived from UAS characteristic dimension and population density of overflown area (GRC 1–10).

    3. Ground Risk Mitigations: M1 (operational restrictions), M2 (UAS technical and operational requirements), M3 (emergency response); each reduces GRC by 0, 1, or 2 levels; Final GRC (FGRC) = GRC \(-\) mitigations.

    4. Initial Air Risk Class (ARC): Derived from airspace volume and density of manned traffic (ARC-a to ARC-d).

    5. Strategic Mitigations: Airspace restrictions reduce ARC by 1 or 2 levels; Final ARC.

    6. Tactical Mitigations: DAA system performance; TMPR (Tactical Mitigation Performance Requirement) categories: Low, Medium, High.

    7. SAIL Determination: Specific Assurance and Integrity Level I–VI from matrix of FGRC and Final ARC.

    8. OSO Assessment: 24 Operational Safety Objectives evaluated against SAIL; each rated Low/Medium/High; gaps identified.

    9. Adjacent Airspace: Evaluation of third-party airspace impact.

    10. Comprehensive Safety Portfolio: Final documentation package for competent authority submission.

    E.10.3 ASTM International UAS Standards¶

    ASTM Committee F38 on Unmanned Aircraft Systems has published over 30 standards. Selected key standards:

    • F3196-18: Standard Practice for Seeking Approval for Beyond Visual Line of Sight (BVLOS) Small Unmanned Aircraft System (sUAS) Operations — documents the application process, safety case structure, and operational risk assessment for FAA BVLOS waivers.

    • F3269-17: Standard Practice for Methods to Safely Bound Flight Behavior of Unmanned Aircraft Systems Containing Complex Functions — defines techniques (barriers, monitors, assertions) to bound the behaviour of AI/ML-based flight functions.

    • F3178-16: Standard Practice for Operational Risk Management of Small Unmanned Aircraft Systems (sUAS) — risk matrix methodology for operational approval.

    • F3322-18: Standard Specification for Small Unmanned Aircraft System (sUAS) Parachutes — parachute descent rate, deployment reliability, altitude constraints.

    • F3413-19: Standard Guide for Training for Remote Pilot in Command of Unmanned Aircraft Systems — competency framework; training syllabus; ground school and practical test standards.

    • F3364-20: Standard Practice for Detect and Avoid (DAA) in Unmanned Aircraft Systems — DAA system performance requirements; alert levels; self-separation and collision avoidance manoeuvres.

    E.11 Standard Interfaces and Protocols¶

    E.11.1 MAVLink v2¶

    MAVLink (Micro Air Vehicle Link), originally developed at ETH Zurich in 2009 and now maintained by Dronecode / ArduPilot, is the de facto lightweight messaging protocol for UAS communication between autopilots and ground control stations. Version 2 (2017) extends the original with 24-bit message IDs, message signing, and payload truncation.

    Packet structure (MAVLink v2):

    • Byte 0: Start-of-frame (0xFD).

    • Byte 1: Payload length (0–255 bytes).

    • Byte 2: Incompatibility flags (0x01 = signed).

    • Byte 3: Compatibility flags.

    • Byte 4: Packet sequence number (0–255; wraps).

    • Byte 5: System ID (1–255; sender system).

    • Byte 6: Component ID (1–255; sender component).

    • Bytes 7–9: Message ID (24-bit, little-endian).

    • Bytes 10–(10+n\(-\)1): Payload (n bytes, message-specific).

    • Bytes (10+n) to (11+n): CRC-16/MCRF4XX checksum + message-specific seed.

    • (If signed) Bytes (12+n) to (18+n): 13-byte signature (link ID, timestamp, HMAC-SHA256 truncated).

    Key message types: HEARTBEAT (ID 0): system type, autopilot type, base mode, custom mode, system status; transmitted at 1 Hz. GLOBAL_POSITION_INT (ID 33): latitude, longitude, altitude, relative altitude, velocities. SET_POSITION_TARGET_GLOBAL_INT (ID 86): offboard position setpoints. COMMAND_LONG (ID 76): MAV_CMD enumeration (e.g., TAKEOFF, LAND, RTL, DO_SET_MODE). MISSION_ITEM_INT (ID 73): individual waypoint; frame, command, coordinates, autocontinue. PARAM_SET / PARAM_VALUE (IDs 23/22): parameter upload/readback for autopilot configuration.

    E.11.2 DroneCAN (UAVCAN v1)¶

    DroneCAN (formerly UAVCAN, now rebranded to distinguish from the v1 specification) is an open lightweight protocol for intra-vehicle communication on CAN bus, designed for UAS avionics integration of ESCs, GPS, IMU, battery monitors, and actuators. Key characteristics:

    • Physical layer: ISO 11898-1 CAN 2.0B; 1 Mbps nominal; CAN FD optional (up to 8 Mbps data phase).

    • Node IDs: 1–127; dynamic node-ID allocation via automatic plug-and-play (PnP) protocol or static assignment.

    • Data types: Defined in DSDL (Data Structure Definition Language); compiled to C/C++ serialisers; standard types: sensor_msgs.IMU, equipment.esc.Status, equipment.gnss.Fix2, equipment.power.BatteryInfo.

    • Transfer types: Message broadcast (anonymous or identified), service request/response (inter-node RPC).

    • Health monitoring: Each node publishes uavcan.node.Heartbeat at 1 Hz with health (OK/WARNING/ERROR/CRITICAL), mode (OPERATIONAL/MAINTENANCE/SOFTWARE_UPDATE), uptime, and vendor-specific status code.

    • Firmware update: uavcan.file.BeginFirmwareUpdate service enables over-the-bus firmware updates with integrity verification.

    E.11.3 NATO STANAG 4607: GMTI Data Format¶

    STANAG 4607 (Edition 3) defines the Ground Moving Target Indicator (GMTI) message format for dissemination of moving target data from airborne MTI radars (including SAR-MTI and STAP radar modes) to ground exploitation stations. Structure:

    • Segment types: Mission Segment (platform, sensor, classification); Dwell Segment (sensor position, time, target reports); HRR (High-Range Resolution) Segment; Range-Doppler Segment; Job Definition Segment; Free Text Segment.

    • Target report fields: Measurement index; target latitude/longitude (1\(\mu\)rad resolution); geodetic height; target range; range rate (velocity); radar cross section; SNR; target classification.

    • Coordinate systems: WGS-84 geodetic; time tag to UTC (1 extmus resolution).

    • Transport: UDP/IP multicast; Link 16 Message J6.0; VMF (Variable Message Format) encapsulation.

    E.11.4 NATO STANAG 4609: Motion Imagery¶

    STANAG 4609 (Edition 4) defines the NATO Digital Motion Imagery Standard for airborne and ground ISR systems. Based on SMPTE ST 336M (KLV metadata encoding) and MISB (Motion Imagery Standards Board) local sets:

    • Container format: MPEG-2 Transport Stream (MPEG-2 TS); H.264/AVC or H.265/HEVC video codec; AAC audio.

    • KLV metadata: Key-Length-Value triplets embedded as MISB ST 0601 (UAS datalink local set): platform heading, pitch, roll; sensor lat/lon/alt; slant range; sensor horizontal/vertical FOV; frame centre lat/lon; target width; target location lat/lon; ground speed; wind speed/direction.

    • Security: MISB ST 1402 (motion imagery metadata for security classification); STANAG 4609 security labels.

    • Frame rates: 30 fps (NTSC); 25 fps (PAL); up to 60 fps for WAMI (Wide Area Motion Imagery).

    • Dissemination: NATO C2 system interfaces; Distributed Common Ground System (DCGS); NSA-encrypted data links.

    E.11.5 Other Interface and Geospatial Standards¶

    • OpenDRIVE (ASAM odr): XML-based road network description for autonomous ground vehicle navigation; road geometry (arc, spiral, polynomial); lane topology; traffic signs; junction logic. Used in UAS simulation environments for urban operations modelling.

    • OGC CDB (Common DataBase): Open Geospatial Consortium standard for synthetic environment databases; hierarchical LOD tile structure; vectors, rasters, and 3D models; used in high-fidelity UAS and missile flight simulation.

    • RTCA DO-377A: Minimum Operational Performance Standards for C2 Link Systems Supporting Operations of UAS in US Airspace — availability \(\geq 99.9\%\); data integrity (BER \(< 10^{-6}\)); latency \(< 200\) ms for safety-critical commands; radio frequency protection for 5030–5091 MHz band.

    • IEEE 802.11p (WAVE): Wireless Access in Vehicular Environments; used for UAS swarm communication in ground-proximity operations; 5.9 GHz; 10 MHz channels; OFDM; DSRC.

    • SRP (Spatial Reference Protocol): GeoServer/WFS-based interface for UAS traffic management UTM platforms (NASA UTM, EASA U-Space); delivers real-time airspace status, NOTAMs, and UAS position tracks.

    E.13 Interoperability and Compliance Considerations¶

    Engineers and programme managers must carefully map applicable standards at the outset of any UAS or missile development programme. The following principles guide standards selection and compliance planning:

    Hierarchical applicability.

    Standards exist in a hierarchy: international treaty obligations and ICAO SARPs (Standards and Recommended Practices) take precedence; then regional regulations (EASA, CAAC); then national authority rules (FAA, CAA); then military authority requirements (DoD, MAA); then industry consensus standards (RTCA, SAE, ASTM) used as means of compliance.

    Tailoring.

    Military programme offices routinely tailor MIL-STDs to programme-specific requirements using the tailoring process defined in MIL-STD-961E (Defence and Programme-Unique Specifications Format and Content). Tailoring must be documented in a Tailoring Rationale and approved by the contracting officer’s technical representative (COTR).

    Means of compliance.

    Civil certification authorities accept alternative means of compliance (AMOC/AltMoC) when an applicant demonstrates equivalent safety. For novel technologies such as AI-based flight control or battery-electric propulsion, dedicated Special Conditions (EASA SC-VTOL; FAA SC for eVTOL) supplement or replace existing certification specifications.

    Configuration management of standards.

    Standards are revised frequently; programme management must track revision status. MIL-HDBK-516C was last revised 2014; DO-178C effective 2012; DO-160G Edition G effective 2010. The applicable revision is that in force at contract award unless the SOW specifies otherwise. Contractors must maintain a Standards Library with current subscriptions to DLA Assist, RTCA, SAE, ASTM, and EASA databases.

    Export control early engagement.

    Export control classification must be performed at programme inception. Technical data generated during development of ITAR-controlled systems is itself controlled. Foreign nationals working on such programmes require ITAR exemption documentation (e.g., DoS 22 CFR §125.4 bona fide employee exemption) or a DSP-5 export licence. Violations are not cured retroactively; voluntary disclosure to DDTC mitigates but does not eliminate penalties.

    Interoperability architecture.

    NATO STANAG 4586 Level of Interoperability (LOI) framework provides a structured approach to multi-national UAS interoperability: LOI 1 — indirect receipt of ISR data; LOI 2 — direct receipt of ISR data; LOI 3 — UAS status monitoring; LOI 4 — control of payload; LOI 5 — full control of UAS including launch and recovery. Achieving LOI 4 or 5 interoperability requires compliance with both the STANAG 4586 Data Link Interface (DLI) and the applicable communication link standard (STANAG 7085 or Link 16 STANAG 5516).

    Note: Standards are subject to periodic revision. Readers should verify currency with the issuing body before application to a specific programme. The information in this appendix reflects the state of international standardisation as of early 2026.
