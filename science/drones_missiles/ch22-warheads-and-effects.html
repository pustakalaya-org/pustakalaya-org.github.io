    Chapter 22: Warheads and Effects¶

    22.1 Warheads and Effects¶

    The warhead is the lethal element of a guided weapon—the subsystem that converts stored chemical (or nuclear) energy into a physical mechanism that damages or destroys the target. Warhead engineering is a discipline that spans detonation physics, terminal ballistics, material science, structural mechanics, and probabilistic lethality analysis. Unlike propulsion, which drives the weapon toward the target, the warhead determines whether the kill actually occurs; a perfectly guided missile that delivers an ineffective warhead achieves nothing.

    This chapter treats all major warhead types in systematic engineering depth: high-explosive (HE) blast and fragmentation; shaped charge (HEAT); explosively formed penetrators (EFP); thermobaric and fuel-air explosives; kinetic energy penetrators; EMP warheads; submunitions; and a public-domain overview of nuclear warheads. Lethality assessment methodology completes the chapter.

    22.2 Warhead Design Fundamentals¶

    22.2.1 Function and Energy Conversion¶

    A warhead performs energy conversion in three stages:

    1. Initiation: A fuze (Chapter 23) provides the initiation stimulus (electrical pulse, shock) at the correct moment and geometry.

    2. Detonation: Explosive decomposes exothermally in microseconds, producing a detonation wave at velocity \(D\) (velocity of detonation, VOD) and Chapman–Jouguet pressure \(P_{CJ}\):

      \[P_{CJ} = \frac{\rho_0\,D^2}{\gamma_{CJ}+1}\tag{22.1}\]

      where \(\rho_0\) is the unreacted explosive density and \(\gamma_{CJ} \approx 3\) for most high explosives. For TNT (\(\rho_0 = 1.64\) g/cm\(^3\), \(D = 6950\) m/s): \(P_{CJ} \approx 19\) GPa.

    3. Terminal effect: Blast wave propagation, fragment projection, jet formation, or fireball, depending on warhead type.

    22.2.2 Lethality Probability¶

    The probability of kill \(P_k\) against a target depends on the lethal area of the warhead \(A_{lethal}\) and the target vulnerable area \(A_{vuln}\):

    \[P_k = 1 - \exp\!\left(-\frac{A_{lethal}}{A_{target}}\right)\tag{22.2}\]

    For a weapon with circular lethal radius \(R_L\) and a target of effective diameter \(d_t\), assuming a bivariate normal CEP distribution with CEP \(= \sigma\sqrt{2\ln 2}\):

    \[P_k = 1 - \exp\!\left(-\frac{(R_L + d_t/2)^2}{2\sigma^2}\right)\tag{22.3}\]

    This shows the trade between warhead lethal radius and delivery accuracy. For a given \(P_k\) requirement, a 2\(\times\) improvement in accuracy (\(\sigma/2\)) allows a \(4\times\) reduction in lethal area (smaller warhead), or achieves higher \(P_k\) with the same warhead.

    22.2.3 Warhead Mass Fraction¶

    The warhead mass \(M_w\) as a fraction of the all-up missile mass \(M_{total}\) typically falls in the range:

    \[f_w = \frac{M_w}{M_{total}} = 0.20\text{--}0.35\tag{22.4}\]

    Higher mass fractions (heavy warheads) reduce the propellant available and shorten range; lower fractions reduce lethality. The trade is resolved by the intended target set: anti-personnel missiles carry small (2–5 kg) warheads (\(f_w < 0.10\)); heavy anti-ship missiles may carry warheads of 150–500 kg (\(f_w \approx 0.35\)).

    22.3 High Explosive Blast and Fragmentation Warheads¶

    22.3.1 Explosive Fills¶

    The performance of a blast/fragmentation warhead depends critically on the explosive fill. Key parameters are the velocity of detonation (VOD), the detonation pressure \(P_{CJ}\), and the relative effectiveness (RE factor) referenced to TNT.

    Table 22.2. Properties of common military explosive fills
    Explosive \(\rho_0\) (g/cm\(^3\)) VOD (m/s) \(P_{CJ}\) (GPa) RE Factor Notes
    TNT 1.64 6950 19.0 1.00 Reference explosive
    RDX (Cyclonite) 1.82 8750 33.8 1.60 Insensitive at density
    HMX (Octogen) 1.91 9110 39.3 1.70 Highest perf. CHNO
    PETN 1.77 8400 31.0 1.27 Detonator trains
    Comp B (60%RDX/40%TNT) 1.72 7980 29.5 1.35 Common fill
    Comp C-4 1.60 8050 26.3 1.37 Plastic explosive
    PBXN-109 1.68 7920 27.0 1.22 Insensitive munition
    TATB 1.90 7760 29.0 1.00 Extremely insensitive
    PBXN-110 1.75 7900 28.0 1.15 Very insensitive; W80

    The TNT equivalence for blast purposes:

    \[W_{TNT} = W_{HE}\cdot RE_{blast}\tag{22.5}\]

    Note that blast RE factors differ from fragmentation or brisance RE factors; a single RE number is a simplification.

    22.3.2 Blast Wave Physics: Friedlander Waveform¶

    A detonation in free air generates a spherical blast wave. At range \(R\), the overpressure follows the Friedlander profile:

    \[P(t) = P_s\left(1 - \frac{t - t_a}{t^+}\right) \exp\!\left(-b\,\frac{t-t_a}{t^+}\right), \quad t_a \leq t \leq t_a + t^+\tag{22.6}\]

    where \(P_s\) is the peak overpressure, \(t_a\) is the arrival time, \(t^+\) is the positive phase duration, and \(b\) is the waveform parameter. The positive impulse per unit area:

    \[i^+ = \int_{t_a}^{t_a+t^+} P(t)\,dt = \frac{P_s\,t^+}{b^2}\left[b - 1 + e^{-b}\right]\tag{22.7}\]

    22.3.3 Hopkinson–Cranz Scaling¶

    All blast parameters scale with the scaled distance:

    \[Z = \frac{R}{W^{1/3}}\tag{22.8}\]

    where \(R\) is the range in metres and \(W\) is the TNT equivalent charge mass in kilograms. The peak overpressure (Kingery-Bulmash):

    \[P_s(Z) = \frac{0.84}{Z} + \frac{2.7}{Z^2} + \frac{0.43}{Z^3} \quad [\text{MPa, for }Z > 0.5\;\text{m/kg}^{1/3}]\tag{22.9}\]

    This empirical expression is widely used in preliminary design. Key damage thresholds versus peak overpressure are given in Table 22.3.

    Table 22.3. Blast damage thresholds for key targets
    Effect \(P_s\) (kPa) Notes
    Eardrum rupture (50% probability) 35 Personnel casualty
    Lung haemorrhage threshold 70 Below this: survivable
    Lung rupture (50% lethality) 100 LD\(_{50}\) for blast lung
    Window glass failure 3–7 Building damage indicator

    22.3.4 Natural Fragmentation¶

    When a cylindrical HE-filled warhead detonates, the case is accelerated outward and fractures into fragments. The fragment mass distribution follows the Mott distribution:

    \[\frac{dN}{dm} = \frac{N_0}{2\mu}\,\exp\!\left(-\sqrt{\frac{m}{\mu}}\right)\tag{22.10}\]

    equivalently, the number of fragments with mass \(>m\) is:

    \[N(>m) = N_0\,\exp\!\left(-\sqrt{\frac{m}{\mu}}\right)\tag{22.11}\]

    where \(N_0\) is the total number of fragments and \(\mu\) is a characteristic fragment mass:

    \[\mu = B\,t^{5/6}\,d^{1/3}\left(1 + t/d\right)\tag{22.12}\]

    where \(t\) is the case wall thickness, \(d\) is the warhead diameter, and \(B\) is the Mott constant (depends on case material; for mild steel, \(B \approx 0.1\) g\(^{2/3}\)cm\(^{-5/2}\)).

    22.3.5 Gurney Fragment Velocity¶

    The initial fragment velocity is predicted by the Gurney equation. For a cylindrical warhead (charge mass \(C\), case mass \(M\)):

    \[V_f = \sqrt{2\,E_G}\,\sqrt{\frac{C/M}{1 + C/(2M)}}\tag{22.13}\]

    where \(\sqrt{2\,E_G}\) is the Gurney velocity (specific to the explosive):

    Table 22.4. Gurney velocities for common explosive fills
    Explosive \(\sqrt{2E_G}\) (m/s) Notes
    TNT 2440 Reference
    Comp B 2700 Standard fill
    RDX 2930 Higher performance
    HMX 2970 Highest CHNO
    C-4 2840 Plastic
    PBXN-109 2590 IM formulation

    For \(C/M = 1\) and Comp B: \(V_f = 2700\sqrt{1/1.5} = 2205\) m/s.

    22.3.6 Fragment Pattern and Spray Angle¶

    In flight, fragments are projected in a static pattern (determined by warhead geometry) but the dynamic spray angle is Doppler-shifted by the missile velocity \(V_m\):

    \[\sin\theta_{dyn} = \frac{V_f\,\sin\theta_{stat}} {\sqrt{V_f^2 + V_m^2 + 2\,V_f\,V_m\cos\theta_{stat}}}\tag{22.14}\]

    For high-speed missiles (\(V_m > 500\) m/s) the forward fragment pattern is strongly compressed toward the nose. Warhead design adjusts charge shape (conical forward face, hemispherical aft) to compensate and maintain the desired spray angle at the target.

    22.3.7 Continuous Rod Warhead¶

    Used against air targets, the continuous rod warhead contains a bundle of rods (typically 12 mm \(\times\) 100 mm, steel, welded alternately at ends) wrapped around the HE charge. On detonation the rods form an expanding hoop:

    \[R_{rod}(t) = R_0 + V_{rod}\,t\tag{22.15}\]

    where \(V_{rod} \approx 600\)–900 m/s. The continuous hoop remains coherent for 15–25 ms (expanding to 10–20 m diameter) and cuts through the structure of an aircraft target. The lethal condition is that the rod hoop intersects the target; lethal radius:

    \[R_L = \frac{n_{rods}\,l_{rod}\,w_{rod}}{2\pi\,R_{hoop}}\tag{22.16}\]

    22.4 Shaped Charge (HEAT) Warheads¶

    22.4.1 Monroe/Munroe Effect and Jet Formation¶

    The shaped charge exploits the Munroe effect: a conical metal liner (typically copper) backed by HE detonates from the apex rearward. The detonation wave collapses the liner progressively from tip to base. Each element of the liner is accelerated to velocity \(v\) by the detonation pressure and directed along the liner axis. The Birkhoff (PER) theory for jet tip velocity:

    \[V_{tip} = V_{det}\,\frac{1 + \cos\beta}{2}\cdot\frac{1}{\sin(\beta/2)}\tag{22.17}\]

    where \(\beta\) is the cone half-angle and \(V_{det}\) is the detonation front velocity in the liner. Practical tip velocities are 7000–10000 m/s. The jet tail velocity is 1000–2000 m/s; this velocity gradient stretches the jet over time, increasing its length.

    22.4.2 Penetration Mechanics¶

    Shaped charge jet penetration depth \(P\) in the hydrodynamic regime (tip velocity \(\gg\) plastic wave speed in target):

    \[P = L_{jet}\,\sqrt{\frac{\rho_{jet}}{\rho_{target}}}\tag{22.18}\]

    where \(L_{jet}\) is the effective jet length and \(\rho_{jet}\), \(\rho_{target}\) are jet and target material densities. For a copper jet (\(\rho_j = 8.9\) g/cm\(^3\)) into rolled homogeneous armour (RHA, \(\rho_t = 7.85\) g/cm\(^3\)):

    \[\frac{P}{L_{jet}} = \sqrt{\frac{8.9}{7.85}} = 1.065\tag{22.19}\]

    A 200 mm effective jet length penetrates approximately 213 mm RHA before the hydrodynamic limit applies. With jet break-up and particulation, effective penetration is 50–80% of the theoretical limit: \(P \approx 5\)–7 calibres for a standard design, \(P \approx 8\)–10 calibres for optimised design.

    22.4.3 Stand-off Distance¶

    The jet stretches and develops maximum coherent length at an optimal stand-off distance \(S_{opt}\):

    \[S_{opt} = (4\text{--}8)\,D_{calibre}\tag{22.20}\]

    where \(D_{calibre}\) is the warhead diameter. At shorter stand-off the jet has not fully developed; at longer stand-off, particulation and lateral dispersion reduce penetration. For the RPG-7 PG-7VM warhead (\(D = 70\) mm): \(S_{opt} = 280\)–560 mm. The piezoelectric fuze nose probe is sized to maintain this stand-off.

    22.4.4 Liner Materials¶

    • OFHC copper (70:30 Cu:Zn brass): Standard; grain size \(<\)50 \(\mu\)m; Vickers hardness 60–80 HV; excellent ductility sustains long coherent jet.

    • Tantalum: Density 16.6 g/cm\(^3\) (highest of practical liner materials); superior penetration over copper for the same \(L_{jet}/D\) ratio; cost-prohibitive for most applications.

    • Molybdenum: Intermediate density 10.2 g/cm\(^3\); harder; used in EFP liners.

    • Mild steel: Lower performance; used for low-cost applications (RPG warheads).

    22.4.5 Explosive Reactive Armour (ERA) Defeat and Tandem Charges¶

    ERA (e.g., Kontakt-5, Relikt) consists of explosive-filled tiles on the tank hull. When the shaped charge jet strikes the tile, the explosive detonates and drives flyer plates into the jet path, disrupting jet coherence and reducing penetration by 50–80%.

    Tandem warhead design defeats ERA with two sequential charges:

    1. Precursor (forward) charge: Small shaped charge (50–70 mm diameter) mounted ahead of the main warhead. Detonates on contact with the ERA tile, consuming the ERA before the main charge arrives.

    2. Main charge: Standard or EFP warhead, following 1–5 ms behind; the ERA tile is now expended and cannot protect the base armour.

    The precursor jet must arrive at the ERA with sufficient velocity (\(>\)3000 m/s) to initiate the tile explosive, but the precursor must not destroy the main warhead’s path of approach. Stand-off between precursor and main charge \(\Delta S \approx 200\)–400 mm.

    \[P_{tandem} \approx P_{main} - \Delta P_{ERA} + P_{precursor_{ERA}} \approx P_{main}\tag{22.21}\]

    22.5 Explosively Formed Penetrators (EFP)¶

    22.5.1 Principle and Formation¶

    The EFP warhead uses a shallow dish-shaped (concave) metal liner (typically molybdenum or copper) backed by the explosive charge. On detonation, the liner inverts and accelerates without forming a fine jet—instead it forms a coherent, dense, bullet-like projectile:

    • Projectile diameter: 50–85% of liner diameter

    • Projectile length-to-diameter: 1.5–5 (compact penetrator)

    • Projectile velocity: 1500–3000 m/s

    • Formation time: 50–100 \(\mu\)s

    The EFP formation is governed by liner curvature \(R_c\), liner thickness \(t_L\), and explosive type. The axial velocity of the EFP:

    \[V_{EFP} = \frac{2\,P_{CJ}}{\rho_L}\,\frac{t_L}{R_c}\,\eta_{EFP}\tag{22.22}\]

    where \(\eta_{EFP} \approx 0.6\)–0.8 is an efficiency factor.

    22.5.2 Stand-off and Penetration¶

    Unlike the HEAT jet, the EFP does not particulate; it remains coherent from 20 to 50 calibres stand-off (versus 4–8 calibres for HEAT):

    \[\text{EFP effective stand-off range: } S_{EFP} = (20\text{--}50)\,D\tag{22.23}\]

    Penetration depth of an EFP in RHA using the modified Tate–Alekseevskii model:

    \[P_{EFP} = L_0\,\sqrt{\frac{\rho_p}{\rho_t}} \left(1 - \frac{Y_t}{k\,\rho_t\,V_{EFP}^2}\right)\tag{22.24}\]

    where \(L_0\) is initial projectile length, \(Y_t\) is target yield strength, and \(k \approx 1.5\). For molybdenum EFP at 2000 m/s into RHA: \(P_{EFP} \approx 0.8\)–\(1.5\,D_{liner}\). This is lower absolute penetration than a HEAT jet, but the large coherent projectile diameter creates a much larger behind-armour damage volume.

    22.5.3 Selectable Warhead and IED Application¶

    Selectable warheads switch between HEAT and EFP mode via a fuze logic signal:

    • Soft target detected \(\rightarrow\) EFP mode (long stand-off, large fragmentation effect)

    • Hard armour target detected \(\rightarrow\) HEAT mode (maximum penetration)

    EFP technology was notoriously exploited in IED (improvised explosive device) construction by Mahdi Army forces in Iraq (2004–2011). Copper-dish EFPs buried in road surfaces were command-detonated to attack vehicle undersides. These side-attack EFPs from Claymore-type mines or top-attack systems (e.g., Intelligent Munitions System) demonstrate the dual-use nature of EFP physics.

    22.6 Thermobaric Warheads¶

    22.6.1 Fuel-Air Explosive (FAE) Mechanism¶

    The fuel-air explosive (FAE) warhead creates a dispersed fuel cloud that is subsequently ignited, producing a near-field detonation or strong deflagration. The two-stage process:

    1. Dispersal: A small bursting charge (central dissemination charge) ruptures the fuel container and atomises the fuel into a 5–30 \(\mu\)m aerosol cloud. Cloud radius:

      \[R_{cloud} = C_d\,\left(\frac{M_{fuel}}{\rho_{air}}\right)^{1/3}\tag{22.25}\]

      where \(C_d \approx 4\)–6 is an empirical coefficient.

    2. Ignition: After a timed delay (50–200 ms for cloud development), one or more igniter charges trigger combustion throughout the cloud volume. If stoichiometric mixing is achieved, a near-detonation (deflagration-to-detonation transition, DDT) occurs with VOD approaching 1500–2500 m/s.

    22.6.2 Blast Characteristics¶

    FAE produces a fundamentally different pressure-time profile than HE:

    • Extended positive phase: Duration 3–5\(\times\) longer than equivalent HE blast; impulse is very high.

    • Negative phase: Strong sub-ambient negative phase (partial vacuum) follows; particularly damaging to lungs and hollow organs.

    • Oxygen depletion: Consumption of atmospheric oxygen in confined spaces creates asphyxiation hazard.

    The positive impulse of an FAE compared to TNT at the same mass:

    \[\frac{i^+_{FAE}}{i^+_{TNT}} \approx 2\text{--}3 \quad \text{(at ranges } Z > 3 \text{ m/kg}^{1/3}\text{)}\tag{22.26}\]

    The overpressure advantage is less at close range (FAE peak \(P_s\) is lower than HE for equivalent mass); FAE superiority lies in its impulse at medium range.

    22.6.3 Thermobaric Explosives¶

    Pure thermobaric warheads (as distinct from FAE) incorporate solid metal fuels—typically aluminium powder (1–20 \(\mu\)m)—into an explosive matrix. The aluminium reacts with detonation products (H\(_2\)O, CO\(_2\)) in an exothermic after-burn:

    \[2\,\text{Al} + \text{H}_2\text{O} \rightarrow \text{Al}_2\text{O}_3 + 3\,\text{H}_2 \quad \Delta H = -837\;\text{kJ/mol}\tag{22.27}\]
    \[4\,\text{Al} + 3\,\text{O}_2 \rightarrow 2\,\text{Al}_2\text{O}_3 \quad \Delta H = -1676\;\text{kJ/mol}\tag{22.28}\]

    The total heat release per kilogram of thermobaric explosive is 5000–9000 kJ/kg, compared to \(\sim\)4600 kJ/kg for TNT. The sustained high pressure in enclosed spaces (bunkers, tunnels, building interiors) is devastating; walls prevent pressure relief and extend the positive phase further.

    22.6.4 Operational Systems¶

    • BLU-73/B FAE: Ethylene oxide fuel; 45 kg; dispersed by M130 dispenser; 15 m lethal radius in open; BLU-72 uses propylene oxide.

    • TOS-1A Buratino (Russia): 220 mm thermobaric rocket; Al-powder thermobaric fill; 24-round launcher; maximum range 6 km; area denial in urban warfare.

    • RPG-7TBG-7 round: Single-shot shoulder-fired thermobaric; effective against bunkers, light armour; lethal radius 10 m in open, 40 m in enclosed space.

    • BLU-118/B: 2000-lb penetrating thermobaric bomb; used against Tora Bora cave complexes (2001); penetrates earth before detonating thermobaric fill.

    22.7 Kinetic Energy Penetrators¶

    22.7.1 Long-Rod Penetrator Theory¶

    Kinetic energy (KE) penetrators attack armour by overwhelming its strength with the kinetic energy of a dense, fast rod. Above the ballistic limit, penetration occurs in the hydrodynamic regime where both rod and target behave as fluids. The basic hydrodynamic penetration depth (Tate-Alekseevskii):

    \[P = L\,\sqrt{\frac{\rho_p}{\rho_t}}\tag{22.29}\]

    where \(L\) is the rod length and \(\rho_p\), \(\rho_t\) are penetrator and target densities. For a tungsten rod (\(\rho_p = 17.6\) g/cm\(^3\)) into RHA (\(\rho_t = 7.85\) g/cm\(^3\)): \(P = L \times 1.497\). A 600 mm rod penetrates \(\sim\)900 mm RHA in pure hydrodynamic theory; accounting for strength effects the modified Tate model gives:

    \[P = L\,\sqrt{\frac{\rho_p}{\rho_t}}\,\left(1 - \frac{R_t - Y_p} {\frac{1}{2}\rho_p V^2}\right)\tag{22.30}\]

    where \(R_t\) is the dynamic target resistance (\(\sim\)5\(Y_t\)) and \(Y_p\) is the penetrator yield strength. At \(V = 1700\) m/s and \(Y_p = 1600\) MPa for tungsten alloy, the strength correction is approximately \(15\text{--}20\%\).

    22.7.2 Materials: Tungsten vs DU¶

    Tungsten Heavy Alloy (93W, WHA):
    Composition 93% W, 4.9% Ni, 2.1% Fe; density 17.6 g/cm\(^3\); \(Y_p \approx 1500\) MPa; elongation 8–12%. Adiabatic shear bands form at impact, but the rod tends to mushroom rather than self-sharpen.
    Depleted Uranium (DU, U-238):
    Density 18.5 g/cm\(^3\); \(Y_p \approx 1200\) MPa; key advantage is adiabatic shear plugging (self-sharpening): the nose continuously fractures away, maintaining a sharp tip and increasing effective penetration by 10–15% over WHA at the same geometry. Also pyrophoric: fragments ignite inside the tank on penetration (incendiary effect). Disadvantages: mild radioactivity (\(\alpha\)-emitter), environmental contamination, logistic and political restrictions.

    22.7.3 Tank APFSDS Round¶

    Armour-Piercing, Fin-Stabilised, Discarding Sabot (APFSDS):

    • Calibre: 120 mm gun (L55 or M256 smoothbore)

    • Muzzle velocity: 1680–1760 m/s

    • Penetrator L/D: 25–30

    • Penetrator dimensions: 22 mm \(\times\) 700 mm (typical)

    • Penetration at 2 km (0\(^\circ\) obliquity): 600–800 mm RHA

    \[P_{APFSDS} \approx 0.6\,L\,\sqrt{\frac{\rho_p}{\rho_t}}\cdot\eta_{obliquity}(\theta)\tag{22.31}\]

    where \(\eta_{obliquity}\) decreases with impact obliquity angle \(\theta\) (60\(^\circ\) obliquity reduces penetration by \(\sim\)40%).

    22.7.4 Kinetic Energy Missile (KEM)¶

    A missile-delivered KE weapon uses the closing velocity of the missile to impart kinetic energy. No warhead explosive is needed; the missile body itself is the penetrator. The impact KE:

    \[E_k = \frac{1}{2}\,M_{missile}\,V_c^2\tag{22.32}\]

    At \(V_c = 1500\) m/s and \(M_{missile} = 15\) kg: \(E_k = 16.9\) MJ \(\approx\) 4 kg TNT equivalent. However, the penetrator L/D is typically much lower than a tank APFSDS, so penetration per unit cross-section is less efficient. Used in anti-satellite (ASAT) and ballistic missile defence (hit-to-kill) applications where the closing velocity is 5–10 km/s and no explosive is needed.

    22.8 EMP Warheads¶

    22.8.1 Nuclear EMP (NEMP)¶

    A nuclear burst generates an electromagnetic pulse through the Compton electron mechanism:

    1. Prompt gamma rays (0–100 ns) ionise air molecules, ejecting Compton electrons.

    2. The asymmetric earth/air boundary creates a net downward current surge.

    3. The rapidly varying current generates a broad-spectrum electromagnetic field pulse.

    The three components of NEMP have distinct characteristics:

    \(E_1\) (fast):
    Rise time \(<\)5 ns; peak 10–50 kV/m at ground level for a high-altitude (HEMP) burst. Destroys semiconductor junctions by dielectric breakdown.
    \(E_2\) (intermediate):
    1 \(\mu\)s to 1 s; similar to natural lightning; most existing lightning protection handles this component.
    \(E_3\) (slow):
    1–1000 s; geomagnetic storm from the magnetohydrodynamic (MHD) interaction of the fireball; induces quasi-DC currents in long conductors (power lines, pipelines); transformer core saturation and burnout.

    The peak \(E_1\) electric field for a HEMP detonation at altitude \(H\):

    \[E_{1,max} = E_0\,\exp\!\left(-\frac{R_{slant} - H}{H_{scale}}\right)\tag{22.33}\]

    where \(E_0 \approx 50\) kV/m is the peak field (from Compton electron theory) and \(H_{scale} \approx 7.5\) km is the atmospheric scale height. The \(E_1\) pulse can illuminate a continent-wide area from a single 300 km altitude burst.

    22.8.2 Non-Nuclear EMP (NNEMP)¶

    Flux Compression Generator (FCG).

    The FCG converts explosive energy to electrical energy with very high efficiency (\(\eta \approx 30\text{--}50\%\)):

    1. A solenoid carrying initial current \(I_0\) (from a seed capacitor bank) surrounds an explosive-filled tube.

    2. Detonation compresses the magnetic flux within the tube, driving current to peak levels:

      \[I_{peak} = I_0\,\sqrt{\frac{L_0}{L_{min}}}\tag{22.34}\]

      where \(L_0\) is the initial inductance and \(L_{min}\) is the minimum inductance at full compression. Compression ratios \(L_0/L_{min} = 100\)–1000; \(I_{peak}\) reaches 10–100 MA in tens of microseconds.

    3. The output drives an antenna or HPM source.

    High Power Microwave (HPM).

    HPM warheads generate focused microwave energy to defeat electronics at range without direct contact:

    • Frequency: 1–35 GHz (typically 9–10 GHz for penetration through apertures); 95 GHz for Active Denial System

    • Peak power: 10 MW–1 GW; pulse width 10 ns–1 \(\mu\)s

    • Power density at target: \(S = P_{rad}\,G/(4\pi R^2)\); must exceed HPM threshold \(S_{HPM}\) to cause effect

    • Effect mechanism: couple energy through cable apertures, antenna ports, or seams; induce \(>\)1 V at sensitive nodes

    • Boeing CHAMP (Counter-electronics High Power Microwave Advanced Missile Project): AGM-86 airframe; 10 GHz HPM; demonstrated 7 target passes in single flight (2012)

    \[S_{target} = \frac{P_{HPM}\,G_{ant}}{4\pi R^2}\,\tau_{atm}\tag{22.35}\]

    22.8.3 EMP Hardening¶

    Electronics protection against EMP:

    • Faraday cage: Continuous metallic enclosure; attenuation \(A = 20\log_{10}(1/|S_{21}|)~\text{dB}\); sealed seams required.

    • Transient voltage suppressors (TVS): Bi-directional Zener/avalanche diodes at all interface cable entries; clamp voltage \(<\)5 V; response time \(<\)1 ps.

    • Surge arrestors and gas tubes: First-stage protection for power lines; 10 kA discharge capability.

    • Fibre optic interconnects: Replace copper signal cables; immune to common-mode EMP coupling.

    • MIL-STD-461 / MIL-STD-464: Hardening requirements; \(E_1\) threat environment 50 kV/m; test to RS105 (radiated susceptibility, transient).

    22.9 Submunitions and Cluster Munitions¶

    22.9.1 Dispenser Systems¶

    Cluster munitions dispense multiple submunitions over a large area from a single delivery vehicle. The CBU-87 Combined Effects Munition (CEM):

    • Dispenser: SUU-65/B; 370 BLU-97/B submunitions

    • Area coverage: 100 m \(\times\) 400 m pattern

    • BLU-97/B: HEAT (12 mm penetration); fragmentation; incendiary zirconium disc; total mass 1.5 kg

    • Ribbon parachute on each submunition slows descent and orients HEAT cone downward

    The fragment density at range \(r\) from the dispenser centreline:

    \[\phi(r) = \frac{N_{sub}\,N_{frag}}{\pi\,r_{lethal}^2}\tag{22.36}\]

    where \(N_{sub}\) is the number of submunitions and \(N_{frag}\) is the number of lethal fragments per submunition.

    22.9.2 Sensor-Fuzed Weapon (SFW)¶

    The Textron BLU-108 SFW (used in CBU-97 and CBU-105 WCMD) is the most sophisticated conventional submunition. Operation:

    1. Dispenser opens at \(\sim\)500 m altitude; 10 BLU-108 SFW sensors descend on parachutes.

    2. Each BLU-108 spins up (spin-arm) using aerodynamic vanes.

    3. At 30 m altitude: rocket motor fires; 4 Skeet EFP submunitions are ejected tangentially.

    4. Each Skeet contains an IR sensor (scanning arc below).

    5. IR sensor detects vehicle thermal signature; EFP fires downward into top armour (50 mm penetration).

    6. If no target detected after full rotation: self-destruct at ground impact (99% reliability to MIL-DTL-23659 IM standard).

    \[P_{kill,SFW} = 1 - \prod_{k=1}^{4}(1 - P_{k,Skeet})\tag{22.37}\]

    For \(P_{k,Skeet} = 0.35\) per Skeet: \(P_{kill,SFW} = 1 - 0.65^4 = 0.821\).

    22.9.3 M85 DPICM and Insensitive Munitions¶

    The M85 DPICM (Dual Purpose Improved Conventional Munition) combines shaped charge anti-armour and fragmentation anti-personnel effects. The IM (insensitive munitions) requirement (MIL-DTL-23659) mandates:

    • Bullet impact: 12.7 mm AP at 850 m/s; no detonation

    • Fragment impact: No detonation

    • Shaped charge attack: No detonation or sympathetic detonation

    • Fast cook-off: Fuel fire; controlled burn, no detonation

    • Slow cook-off: Slow heating; deflagration permitted; no detonation

    IM explosive formulations (PBXN-109, PBXN-110, PAX-29) substitute less sensitive binders (HTPB, BDNPA-F) and use less brisant primary explosives. The trade-off is typically 5–15% reduction in \(P_{CJ}\) and Gurney velocity compared to standard fills.

    22.9.4 Convention on Cluster Munitions (CCM)¶

    The 2008 Convention on Cluster Munitions (Oslo Treaty) prohibits the use, production, stockpiling, and transfer of cluster munitions. As of 2025:

    • Signatories: \(>\)110 states parties

    • Non-signatories include: USA, Russia, China, Israel, India, Pakistan (the primary users and producers)

    • Key exemption criteria (Art. 2): Self-destructing and self-deactivating submunitions with dud rate \(<\)1% are exempted from the CCM definition in some interpretations

    22.10 Nuclear Warheads: Public Domain Overview¶

    The following section addresses nuclear warhead physics at the level of publicly available, declassified information. No classified design details are presented.

    22.10.1 Fission Devices¶

    Nuclear weapons derive their energy from nuclear fission of heavy nuclides (U-235 or Pu-239). A critical mass of fissile material is assembled rapidly to initiate and sustain a supercritical chain reaction.

    Gun-Type Assembly.

    Two sub-critical pieces of U-235 are brought together by a gun mechanism. The Little Boy bomb (Hiroshima, 1945): 64 kg U-235; gun barrel; total mass 4400 kg; yield \(\sim\)15 kt. The gun type is inefficient and cannot use Pu-239 (due to Pu-240 contamination causing pre-detonation).

    Implosion Assembly.

    A sub-critical sphere of Pu-239 (or U-235) is surrounded by shaped explosive lenses that produce a precisely simultaneous spherical detonation wave, compressing the core to \(2.5\text{--}3\times\) normal density, ensuring supercriticality. The Fat Man bomb (Nagasaki, 1945): 6.2 kg Pu-239; 32-point implosion lens design; total mass 4500 kg; yield \(\sim\)21 kt.

    The critical mass for an uncompressed Pu-239 sphere:

    \[M_c \approx \frac{4\pi}{3}\,\rho_{Pu}\,r_c^3;\quad r_c = \sqrt{\frac{3}{4\pi}\,\frac{M_c}{\rho_{Pu}}} \approx 6.2\;\text{cm (bare sphere)}\tag{22.38}\]

    Reflectors (natural U, Be) reduce \(M_c\) to 5.6 kg; compression to 3\(\rho_0\) reduces effective \(M_c\) by \(9\times\).

    22.10.2 Thermonuclear Devices¶

    Two-stage thermonuclear (Teller–Ulam) devices use a fission primary to implode and ignite a fusion secondary (lithium deuteride, \(^6\)LiD):

    1. Primary fission weapon detonates; X-rays and soft radiation compress and heat the secondary.

    2. Li-6 + neutron \(\rightarrow\) T + He-4; T + D \(\rightarrow\) He-4 + n + 17.6 MeV (fusion).

    3. Fusion neutrons fission a U-238 tamper (sparkplug), adding yield (fission-fusion-fission).

    Fusion reaction energy release:

    \[\text{D} + \text{T} \rightarrow {}^4\text{He} + n + 17.6\;\text{MeV}\tag{22.39}\]

    Yields of 100 kt to 50 Mt are achievable. Modern strategic warheads (W88: 475 kt, W87: 300 kt) are compact and hardened for re-entry vehicle delivery.

    22.10.3 Re-entry Vehicle and Fuzing¶

    Strategic nuclear warheads are delivered in hardened re-entry vehicles (RVs) that must survive:

    • Re-entry heating: 8–12 km/s; peak stagnation temperature \(>\)6000 K; ablative heat shield (carbon phenolic).

    • Blast: hardened RV survives 200–500 psi overpressure for silo-killing missions.

    • EMP: nuclear-hardened electronics; radiation-hardened semiconductors.

    Fuzing for air burst (\(\sim\)2 km altitude) uses radar altimetry and barometric sensing to optimise blast radius against area targets. Ground penetration fuze (e.g., B61-11 earth-penetrating bomb) delays detonation until the weapon has penetrated 1–3 m of soil, coupling more energy into the ground shock for hardened-target defeat.

    22.11 Lethality Assessment Methodology¶

    22.11.1 Vulnerable Area Method¶

    The standard lethality assessment methodology uses the target vulnerable area \(A_v\)—the presented area of components whose damage is sufficient to achieve the defined kill criterion (K-kill, M-kill, F-kill):

    \[P_k = 1 - \exp\!\left(-A_v\,\cdot\phi\right)\tag{22.40}\]

    where \(\phi\) is the fragment (or other effect) density (fragments/m\(^2\)) at the target.

    For a fragmentation warhead, \(\phi\) at range \(r\) and warhead design angle \(\Omega_{spray}\):

    \[\phi(r) = \frac{N_{frags}\,p_{let}(r)}{4\pi r^2\,\Omega_{spray}/(4\pi)} = \frac{N_{frags}\,p_{let}(r)}{\Omega_{spray}\,r^2}\tag{22.41}\]

    where \(p_{let}(r)\) is the probability that a single fragment penetrates the target skin to reach the vulnerable component.

    22.11.2 Pk Chain: System-Level Kill Probability¶

    The overall kill probability from launch to kill is the product of conditional probabilities at each stage:

    \[P_{k,system} = P_{detect}\cdot P_{acquire}\cdot P_{track}\cdot P_{launch}\cdot P_{intercept}\cdot P_{fuze}\cdot P_{k|hit}\tag{22.42}\]

    where:

    • \(P_{detect}\): probability target is detected by sensor

    • \(P_{acquire}\): probability seeker locks on given detection

    • \(P_{track}\): probability track is maintained to intercept

    • \(P_{launch}\): probability launch is within envelope (safety, arming)

    • \(P_{intercept}\): probability missile reaches kill box (miss distance \(<\) warhead radius)

    • \(P_{fuze}\): probability fuze functions correctly

    • \(P_{k|hit}\): probability of kill given functioning detonation

    22.11.3 Salvo Theory¶

    When a single missile \(P_k\) is insufficient for the required mission \(P_{k,req}\), multiple missiles are fired in a salvo. The kill probability of \(N\) independent missiles each with \(P_k\):

    \[P_{k,salvo} = 1 - (1 - P_k)^N\tag{22.43}\]

    Solving for \(N\):

    \[N = \frac{\ln(1 - P_{k,req})}{\ln(1 - P_k)}\tag{22.44}\]

    For \(P_k = 0.5\) per missile and \(P_{k,req} = 0.95\): \(N = \ln(0.05)/\ln(0.5) = 4.3\), so 5 missiles are required.

    22.11.4 JMEM and Target Hardness¶

    The Joint Munitions Effectiveness Manual (JMEM) is the US DoD classified database of weapon-target pair effectiveness. The methodology defines:

    • Target hardness: Expressed in psi overpressure required for kill; ranges from \(<\)1 psi (personnel in open) to \(>\)1000 psi (hardened bunker).

    • P\(_k\)(R) curves: Kill probability as a function of burst range for each weapon-target pair; accounts for fragment density, blast pressure, and fuzing.

    • Lethal radius: The range at which \(P_k = 0.5\); used for quick salvo calculations.

    22.11.5 Fragment Lethality Against Personnel¶

    The Wound Ballistics criterion for fragment lethality (casualty production) uses minimum specific kinetic energy:

    \[E_{sp,min} = \frac{\frac{1}{2}m_f\,v_f^2}{A_{frag}} \geq E_{sp,crit} \approx 80\;\text{J/cm}^2\tag{22.45}\]

    where \(m_f\) is fragment mass, \(v_f\) its velocity, and \(A_{frag}\) its presented area. The lethal fragment velocity at range \(r\) accounting for drag deceleration:

    \[v_f(r) = V_0\,\exp\!\left(-\frac{C_D\,\rho_{air}\,A_f}{2\,m_f}\,r\right)\tag{22.46}\]

    where the exponential decay constant: \(\lambda_f = C_D\rho_{air}A_f/(2m_f)\). The lethal radius against unprotected personnel:

    \[R_{let,pers} = \frac{1}{\lambda_f}\ln\!\left(\frac{V_0}{v_{let}}\right)\tag{22.47}\]

    where \(v_{let}\) is the minimum lethal velocity (\(\sim\)120 m/s for standard 1–2 g steel fragments).

    22.11.6 Fuze-Warhead Optimisation¶

    The fuze functions at a specific burst point relative to the target. For a fragmentation warhead, the optimal burst range \(r_{opt}\) maximises the fragment density on the target:

    \[r_{opt} = r_L\,\sqrt{\frac{1}{\lambda_f\,r_L}}\tag{22.48}\]

    (approximate, from calculus of variations on \(\phi(r)\cdot p_{let}(r)\)). For proximity-fuzed warheads, the RF or laser fuze must trigger at this range with miss distance uncertainty \(\sigma_{MD}\) such that:

    \[P_{fuze} = \Phi\!\left(\frac{r_{fuze,max} - r_{det}}{\sigma_{r}}\right) - \Phi\!\left(\frac{r_{fuze,min} - r_{det}}{\sigma_{r}}\right)\tag{22.49}\]

    where \(\Phi\) is the normal CDF and \(r_{det}\) is the detection range of the fuze sensor.

    22.13 Summary and Design Trade Space¶

    Warhead selection is driven by the target set, delivery platform, accuracy, and collateral damage constraints. The dominant modern trend is toward precision effects: smaller warheads delivering lethal energy to specific aim points, enabled by CEP \(<\)3 m guidance accuracy. This allows a 250 lb class warhead to achieve effects previously requiring 2000 lb area weapons, with dramatically reduced collateral damage.

    The second dominant trend is insensitive munitions (IM): replacing high-performance but sensitive explosives (RDX, HMX) with safer formulations (PBXN-109, TATB) that do not detonate on accidental stimulus. IM compliance is now mandatory for all new US DoD acquisitions under USD(AT&L) policy.

    Multi-effect warheads (simultaneously delivering blast, fragmentation, HEAT, and incendiary effects) in a compact package remain the gold standard for general-purpose strike weapons, exemplified by the BLU-111/B JDAM fill, which uses a shaped steel case for fragmentation, PBXH-135 insensitive explosive, and an OPT (optional proximity and impact time) fuze providing both air-burst and point-detonating fuzing modes.
