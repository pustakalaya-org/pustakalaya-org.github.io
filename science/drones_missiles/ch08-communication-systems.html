    Chapter 8: Communication Systems¶

    8.1 RF Fundamentals¶

    Reliable communication between an unmanned vehicle and its operators — or between vehicles in a swarm — is the enabling technology for virtually all drone and missile applications. This chapter develops the theoretical foundations of radio-frequency propagation, links all major control, telemetry, video, datalink, and satellite systems to those foundations, and addresses the military imperatives of jam-resistance, low probability of intercept, and cybersecurity.

    8.1.1 Maxwell’s Equations and Wave Propagation¶

    All electromagnetic communication systems obey Maxwell’s equations, in SI units and differential form:

    \[\begin{align*} \nabla \cdot \mathbf{D} &= \rho_f \\ \nabla \cdot \mathbf{B} &= 0 \\ \nabla \times \mathbf{E} &= -\frac{\partial \mathbf{B}}{\partial t} \\ \nabla \times \mathbf{H} &= \mathbf{J}_f + \frac{\partial \mathbf{D}}{\partial t} \end{align*}\]

    where \(\mathbf{E}\) is the electric field intensity (V/m), \(\mathbf{H}\) is the magnetic field intensity (A/m), \(\mathbf{D} = \varepsilon\mathbf{E}\) is the electric flux density (C/m\(^2\)), \(\mathbf{B} = \mu\mathbf{H}\) is the magnetic flux density (T), \(\rho_f\) is the free charge density, and \(\mathbf{J}_f\) is the free current density.

    In a source-free, homogeneous, isotropic medium, combining Faraday’s and Ampère’s laws yields the vector wave equation:

    \[\nabla^2\mathbf{E} - \mu\varepsilon\frac{\partial^2\mathbf{E}}{\partial t^2} = 0\tag{8.5}\]

    Solutions of the form \(\mathbf{E} = E_0\,\hat{\mathbf{x}}\,e^{j(\omega t - kz)}\) describe plane waves propagating in the \(+z\) direction with wavenumber \(k = \omega\sqrt{\mu\varepsilon} = 2\pi/\lambda\) and phase velocity \(v_p = 1/\sqrt{\mu\varepsilon}\) (equal to \(c = 3 \times 10^8\) m/s in free space).

    8.1.2 Free-Space Path Loss¶

    As an isotropic radiator transmits power \(P_t\), that power spreads over a sphere of area \(4\pi d^2\) at range \(d\). The power density at range \(d\) is \(S = P_t/(4\pi d^2)\). An isotropic receive antenna with effective area \(A_e = \lambda^2/(4\pi)\) intercepts:

    \[P_r = P_t \cdot \frac{\lambda^2}{(4\pi d)^2}\tag{8.6}\]

    For directional antennas with gains \(G_t\) and \(G_r\), the Friis transmission equation is:

    \[\boxed{ P_r = P_t \cdot G_t \cdot G_r \cdot \left(\frac{\lambda}{4\pi d}\right)^2 }\tag{8.7}\]

    The free-space path loss (FSPL) in dB, independent of antenna gains:

    \[\text{FSPL}_{dB} = 20\log_{10}\!\left(\frac{4\pi d}{\lambda}\right) = 20\log_{10}(d) + 20\log_{10}(f) + 20\log_{10}\!\left(\frac{4\pi}{c}\right)\tag{8.8}\]

    Numerically: \(\text{FSPL}_{dB} = 20\log_{10}(d_{\text{km}}) + 20\log_{10}(f_{\text{MHz}}) + 32.45\). At 5.8 GHz and 1 km: FSPL = \(20\log_{10}(1) + 20\log_{10}(5800) + 32.45 = 0 + 75.27 + 32.45 = 107.7\) dB.

    8.1.3 Link Budget¶

    A link budget calculates the received signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) or \(E_b/N_0\) (energy per bit over noise spectral density) to determine link viability. In tabular form:

    Table 8.1. Example Link Budget: 915 MHz SiK Telemetry Radio at 5 km LOS
    Parameter Value Units
    Transmit power (\(P_t\)) +20.0 dBm
    Transmit antenna gain (\(G_t\)) +3.0 dBi
    EIRP +23.0 dBm
    Free-space path loss (5 km, 915 MHz) \(-\)105.2 dB
    Atmospheric absorption \(-\)0.5 dB
    Polarisation mismatch loss \(-\)1.0 dB
    Receive antenna gain (\(G_r\)) +3.0 dBi
    Received power (\(P_r\)) \(-\)80.7 dBm
    Receiver noise figure 5.0 dB
    Thermal noise floor (\(kTB\), \(B\)=125 kHz) \(-\)122.1 dBm
    Effective noise floor \(-\)117.1 dBm
    SNR +36.4 dB
    Required \(E_b/N_0\) (GFSK, BER\(=10^{-6}\)) 13.5 dB
    Link margin +22.9 dB

    The EIRP (Equivalent Isotropically Radiated Power) is the product of transmit power and transmit antenna gain: \(\text{EIRP}_{dBm} = P_t + G_t\). The link margin is the surplus SNR above the minimum required for the target BER; margins of 10–20 dB are typical for reliable drone links.

    8.1.4 Multipath Propagation¶

    Near the ground or over water, reflected signals arrive at the receiver with delays and phase shifts relative to the direct path. Destructive interference creates fades:

    \[P_r(\phi) = P_{r,LOS} \cdot |1 + \Gamma\,e^{j\phi}|^2\tag{8.9}\]

    where \(\Gamma\) is the ground reflection coefficient and \(\phi = 2\pi(d_1 - d_2)/\lambda\) is the differential path phase. For Rayleigh fading (no dominant direct path), the envelope \(r\) of the received signal follows:

    \[f(r) = \frac{r}{\sigma^2}\,\exp\!\left(-\frac{r^2}{2\sigma^2}\right),\quad r \geq 0\tag{8.10}\]

    For Rician fading (with a dominant direct/LOS component of power \(s^2\)):

    \[f(r) = \frac{r}{\sigma^2}\,\exp\!\left(-\frac{r^2 + s^2}{2\sigma^2}\right)\, I_0\!\left(\frac{r\,s}{\sigma^2}\right)\tag{8.11}\]

    The Rician \(K\)-factor \(K = s^2/(2\sigma^2)\) quantifies the ratio of direct to scattered power. Drone links at 915 MHz typically experience \(K = 5\)–15 dB (moderate Rician), while links below tree canopy may approach Rayleigh (\(K \approx 0\) dB).

    Diversity techniques mitigate fading: space diversity uses two antennas separated by \(\geq\lambda/2\); polarisation diversity uses orthogonally-polarised receive antennas; frequency diversity uses FHSS (see Section 8.2.5); time diversity uses forward error correction and interleaving.

    8.1.5 Doppler Shift¶

    When the drone moves with velocity component \(v_r\) towards the transmitter, the received frequency increases:

    \[f_d = \frac{v_r}{c}\,f_0 \approx \frac{2v_r\,f_0}{c}\tag{8.12}\]

    (the factor 2 applies to active radar; for one-way links it is simply \(v_r/c \cdot f_0\)). For a drone flying at 100 m/s on a 5.8 GHz video link, \(f_d = 100/3\times10^8 \times 5.8\times10^9 \approx 1933\) Hz. For narrow-bandwidth coherent demodulators, this frequency offset degrades demodulation performance; OFDM systems (like DJI O3 and COFDM military links) handle Doppler by inserting pilot subcarriers for phase tracking and using cyclic-prefix guard intervals that absorb both Doppler spread and multipath delay spread.

    8.2 RC Control Links¶

    8.2.1 ExpressLRS Architecture¶

    ExpressLRS (ELRS) is an open-source, high-performance RC control link based on the Semtech SX1276/SX1280 LoRa modem chips. Two modulation schemes are available:

    LoRa (Long Range): Uses Chirp Spread Spectrum (CSS), where the carrier frequency is linearly swept across a bandwidth \(B\) in each symbol period \(T_s\). The spreading factor SF determines the chip count (\(2^{SF}\) chips/symbol) and hence the processing gain:

    \[\text{PG}_{dB} = 10\log_{10}(2^{SF}) = SF \times 10\log_{10}(2) \approx 3.01 \times SF\tag{8.13}\]

    SF7 gives \(\approx 21\) dB processing gain at 125 kHz bandwidth and 5.47 kbps; SF12 gives \(\approx 36\) dB gain at 293 bps. ELRS uses SF6 on the 2.4 GHz band (SX1280, 800 kHz BW) or SF7–9 on 915 MHz (SX1276) depending on packet rate.

    FLRC (Fast Long-Range Communication): An SX1280 feature offering higher throughput (\(\leq\)1.3 Mbps) with shorter-range performance. ELRS 2.4 GHz uses FLRC for packet rates above 250 Hz (D250, F500, F1000 modes).

    Key ELRS packet rates and performance:

    • 25 Hz / LoRa: \(\sim\)130 dBm link budget, \(>\)50 km theoretical LOS range.

    • 250 Hz / LoRa: \(\sim\)112 dBm, 3–10 km typical, 4 ms latency.

    • 500 Hz / FLRC: \(\sim\)104 dBm, 2–5 km, 2 ms latency.

    • 1000 Hz / FLRC: \(\sim\)98 dBm, 1–3 km, 1 ms latency.

    Binding uses a UID (unique identifier) from which the FHSS seed and packet CRC polynomial are derived, preventing cross-link interference between co-located pilots. The connection process: transmitter broadcasts bind packets on a fixed channel; receiver listens, matches UID, and both switch to FHSS pattern.

    Telemetry uplink (from drone to transmitter): ELRS carries basic telemetry (RSSI, LQ, SNR, GPS, battery voltage) in the downlink phase of the TDMA frame at 1/n packet rate (every \(n\) control packets), typically 4–10 Hz for OpenTX/EdgeTX sensors.

    8.2.2 TBS Crossfire¶

    Crossfire (TBS, 868/915 MHz) uses a proprietary TDMA frame structure with adaptive power levels from 10 mW to 2 W and an encrypted, self-healing mesh approach:

    • TDMA frame: 10 ms total; 4 ms TX slot (uplink to drone); 4 ms RX slot (downlink telemetry); 2 ms guard time.

    • FEC: Convolutional coding rate 1/2, constraint length 7 (Viterbi decoded) for forward error correction over noisy channels.

    • Failsafe: configurable per-channel positions triggered after 120 ms of no valid packet; options: hold last, fixed values, no pulses (motor cut).

    • Range: up to 150 km LOS at 2 W with directional Yagi antenna.

    8.2.3 SBUS and CRSF Protocols¶

    SBUS (Futaba) is a serial digital RC protocol running at 100 kbps, 8E2 (8 data bits, even parity, 2 stop bits), inverted UART logic (idle high = logic 0). Each 25-byte frame carries 16 analogue channels (11-bit resolution, 172–1811 value range), digital flags, and frame-lost/failsafe bits. Frame period: 14 ms (fast mode: 7 ms). The receiving FC must have hardware-inverted UART or apply software bit inversion.

    CRSF (Crossfire Serial Protocol, used by both TBS Crossfire and ExpressLRS) is a bidirectional protocol at 400 kbps, non-inverted UART. The frame structure:

    • Byte 0: Sync byte (0xC8).

    • Byte 1: Frame length (payload + type + CRC).

    • Byte 2: Frame type (0x16 = RC channels packed; 0x14 = link statistics).

    • Bytes 3–N: Payload (packed 11-bit channels or telemetry data).

    • Last byte: CRC-8 (polynomial 0xD5 over type+payload).

    The CRSF RC channels frame packs 16 \(\times\) 11-bit values into 22 bytes using bitfield packing. CRSF’s bidirectionality allows the FC to inject telemetry frames that ELRS relays back to the handset.

    8.2.4 Antenna Design for RC Links¶

    Quarter-wave monopole: The simplest practical antenna — a vertical conductor of length \(\lambda/4\) above a ground plane. Feedpoint impedance \(\approx 36\,\Omega\); a ferrite choke or sleeve balun corrects this to \(\approx 50\,\Omega\) for coaxial feed. Gain: 0 dBi (omnidirectional in azimuth). At 915 MHz, \(\lambda/4 = 8.2\) cm.

    Half-wave dipole: Two quarter-wave elements fed at centre; gain \(\approx 2.15\) dBi; radiation pattern toroidal (doughnut), null at tips. Commonly used on SiK radios and RC transmitters.

    Patch (microstrip) antenna: A resonant metallic patch on a grounded substrate of thickness \(h\) and permittivity \(\varepsilon_r\). The resonant length is approximately:

    \[L_{patch} = \frac{c}{2f\sqrt{\varepsilon_r}}\tag{8.14}\]

    Gain: 6–9 dBi with a half-power beamwidth of \(\sim60\)–\(80\)°. Used in directional tracking antennas on GCS systems (e.g., TBS Crossfire Ground Station, Yaapu panel).

    Helical antenna: For circular polarisation in FPV systems. Axial-mode helix provides right-hand circular polarisation (RHCP) with 10–14 dBic gain, greatly reducing multipath fading compared to linear polarisation (random polarisation mismatch up to 3 dB loss with linear, vs \(< 1\) dB for circular-to-circular same-sense).

    8.2.5 Frequency Hopping Spread Spectrum¶

    FHSS divides the available band into \(N_h\) hop channels of width \(B_h\) and hops the carrier pseudo-randomly at the hop rate \(R_h\) (hops/second), dwellling on each channel for \(T_{dwell} = 1/R_h\) seconds. The spread signal occupies \(N_h \cdot B_h\) total bandwidth (e.g., 50 channels \(\times\) 500 kHz = 25 MHz). An interferer on a single channel degrades only \(1/N_h\) of packets:

    \[\text{Processing gain}_{FHSS} = 10\log_{10}(N_h)\tag{8.15}\]

    For \(N_h = 50\): PG = 17 dB. ISM band management at 2.4 GHz (IEEE 802.11b/g/n occupies 22 MHz channels): ELRS at 2.4 GHz hops across the full 2400–2500 MHz band in 80 channels (1.25 MHz spacing), using adaptive hop sequences that avoid channels with persistent interference detected via background RSSI scanning.

    8.3 Telemetry Systems¶

    8.3.1 MAVLink Protocol¶

    MAVLink (Micro Air Vehicle Link) is the standard protocol for UAV telemetry and ground control communications. MAVLink v2 packet structure (total 12–280 bytes):

    • STX: 0xFD (1 byte) — start-of-frame marker.

    • LEN: payload length in bytes (1 byte, 0–253).

    • INC_FLAGS: incompatibility flags (1 byte; bit 0 = MAVLINK_IFLAG_SIGNED for message signing).

    • CMP_FLAGS: compatibility flags (1 byte).

    • SEQ: rolling counter for packet loss detection (1 byte).

    • SYS_ID: source system ID, 1–255 (1 byte).

    • COMP_ID: source component ID (1 byte).

    • MSG_ID: 3-byte little-endian message type identifier.

    • Payload: message-specific data.

    • CHECKSUM: CRC-16/MCRF4XX + message-specific CRC_EXTRA byte (2 bytes).

    Heartbeat (MSG_ID = 0) is transmitted at 1 Hz by all MAVLink nodes. Its payload carries vehicle type, autopilot type, base mode, custom mode, and system status. Absence of heartbeat for 3 seconds triggers GCS alarm.

    Key telemetry message families:

    • GLOBAL_POSITION_INT (#33): 1 Hz – 10 Hz; lat/lon/alt/relative alt; velocity.

    • ATTITUDE (#30): 10–100 Hz; roll/pitch/yaw and rates.

    • SYS_STATUS (#1): 1 Hz; battery voltage/current/remaining; sensor health bitmask.

    • GPS_RAW_INT (#24): 5 Hz; GPS fix type, satellites, HDOP.

    • RADIO_STATUS (#109): 1 Hz; RSSI, remote RSSI, noise, errors, fixed packets.

    8.3.2 SiK Radio and RFD900+¶

    The SiK radio (firmware by Michael Oborne and Simon Chambers, open-source) runs on a Silicon Labs Si1000 mixed-signal SoC (8051 core + Si4432 RF transceiver). Key parameters: frequency-hopping GFSK, 50 hop channels, over-the-air baud rates of 2400–250000 bps (air data rate; serial baud rate to FC is independent), output power up to +20 dBm (100 mW), sensitivity \(-\)117 dBm at 9.6 kbps.

    The RFD900x (RFDesign, Australia) replaces the Si1000 with a faster AT32 ARM Cortex-M4 processor and a Si446x RF transceiver, reaching +30 dBm (1 W) and dual-antenna MIMO diversity. Firmware supports transparent serial mode, MAVLink injection (where the radio parses MAVLink to prioritise critical messages under congestion), and configurable hop rates. At +30 dBm with 5 dBi antennas and 9.6 kbps air data rate, link margin at 40 km LOS is approximately 10 dB.

    8.3.3 4G/LTE and Mesh Connectivity¶

    4G LTE connectivity for drone command and control uses standard cellular infrastructure, enabling BVLOS operations without dedicated spectrum licensing. The GCS and drone each run a VPN client (ZeroTier or WireGuard) creating a Layer 2 or Layer 3 tunnel between GCS and drone IP addresses regardless of network topology. MAVLink runs over UDP on this tunnel.

    Latency components on a 4G MAVLink link:

    \[t_{total} = t_{LTE,up} + t_{internet} + t_{VPN} + t_{LTE,down} \approx 50\text{--}150\text{ ms}\tag{8.16}\]

    This latency is too high for inner-loop attitude control but is acceptable for position setpoint updates (\(>\)1 m position tolerance), mission command/acknowledge, and video streaming. OpenHD and WFB-NG (Wifibroadcast NG) repurpose 802.11 cards in injection/monitor mode for low-latency, low-overhead UDP video streaming at 20–100 Mbps, with FEC (forward error correction) replacing TCP reliability. This provides video latency of 50–80 ms vs 200–500 ms for H.264 over RTSP/RTP.

    Mesh networking (Doodle Labs Smart Radio, Persistent Systems MPU5, FreqLink): Each drone node is both a router and an endpoint, relaying packets for other nodes using AODV (Ad-hoc On-Demand Distance Vector) or OLSR (Optimised Link State Routing) routing protocols. In a 5-node mesh at 2 km spacing, a ground station can reach 8–10 km (hop range). Doodle Labs RM-1800M achieves 100+ Mbps aggregate throughput at 900 MHz with 1 W TX power.

    8.4 Video Downlinks¶

    8.4.1 Analogue FPV¶

    Analogue FPV transmits a composite NTSC or PAL video signal (720\(\times\)480 NTSC, 720\(\times\)576 PAL) at 5.8 GHz (most common), 2.4 GHz, or 1.2 GHz. Transmit power options: 25 mW, 100 mW, 200 mW, 600 mW, 1000 mW (1 W). The signal is VSB-AM modulated (vestigial sideband amplitude modulation) onto the carrier. Latency is essentially zero (one field period = 16.7 ms for NTSC), limited only by sensor and display refresh rates. Video quality degrades gracefully as SNR decreases (snow/static) rather than freezing (as in digital systems), which pilots often prefer for safety awareness.

    5.8 GHz band plan (FPV): 40 channels covering 5645–5945 MHz with typical 8 defined bands (Raceband, Fat Shark, ImmersionRC, etc.) of 8 channels each. Channel spacing: 37 MHz (some channels overlap, requiring coordination at multi-pilot events).

    Receiver options:

    • Diversity receiver: monitors two antennas (e.g., omnidirectional + patch), selects stronger RSSI per field.

    • Analogue goggles: integrated displays at 800\(\times\)480 or 1280\(\times\)960 resolution; FOV 30–42 degrees.

    • Analog to digital video (A/D): DVR (digital video recorder) at 60 fps captures flight footage.

    8.4.2 Digital FPV¶

    DJI O3 Air Unit: DJI’s third-generation digital FPV system operates at 5.8 GHz using OFDM with H.264 (1080p) and H.265 (1080p/60fps) encoding. Latency: 18–24 ms (FPV mode). Range: 10 km in open conditions. The OFDM system uses 64-QAM in good SNR, degrading to QPSK at edge of range. Channel width: 20 MHz OFDM. Built-in AES-128 encryption.

    HDZero: HD Zero uses QPSK modulation and 10 bit raw transmission without buffering for minimum latency: claimed 0-frame-delay architecture achieving 24 ms total system latency from camera to goggle display. Resolution: 720p at 60fps or 1080p at 60fps. Frequency: 5.8 GHz. FEC: Reed-Solomon (255, 239) per frame for packet loss recovery without retransmission latency.

    Walksnail Avatar: DJI-Caddx joint venture offering H.265 encoding at 1080p/60fps and 1440p/60fps with Gyroflow stabilisation. Utilises the same 5.8 GHz band with OFDM; latency 22 ms. Dual-frequency (2.4/5.8 GHz) auto-selection.

    8.4.3 Video Compression¶

    H.264 (MPEG-4 AVC) and H.265 (HEVC) are block-based transform codecs using:

    • Intra-frame compression: DCT on 4\(\times\)4 or 8\(\times\)8 blocks, quantisation, Huffman/CABAC entropy coding. I-frames are independently decodable.

    • Inter-frame prediction: motion vectors (\(\Delta x\), \(\Delta y\), \(\Delta z\) per block) from P-frames (one reference) and B-frames (bi-directional). Residual error is DCT-coded.

    • H.265 adds CTU (Coding Tree Units) up to 64\(\times\)64, allowing 40–50% better compression than H.264 at equivalent quality.

    • AV1 (open, royalty-free): further 20–30% improvement over H.265, at higher encoder complexity. Used on bandwidth-constrained LTE video links.

    PSNR (Peak Signal-to-Noise Ratio) quantifies video quality:

    \[\text{PSNR} = 10\log_{10}\!\left(\frac{255^2}{\text{MSE}}\right) \text{ dB}\tag{8.17}\]

    For broadcast quality, PSNR \(>\) 40 dB is desired; H.265 at 4 Mbps for 1080p/30 achieves PSNR \(\approx\) 42 dB.

    8.4.4 Long-Range Encrypted Video: COFDM¶

    Military and law-enforcement UAV video links use COFDM (Coded OFDM), where an outer convolutional code or LDPC code provides FEC before OFDM modulation. This provides robust performance in multipath environments (delay spread up to 1/\(B_{sc}\) where \(B_{sc}\) is the subcarrier spacing). Systems like Silvus StreamCaster 4200 MIMO operate at 2.4/4.4/5.8 GHz, achieve 400+ Mbps aggregate throughput with \(2\times2\) MIMO, and use AES-256 encryption at the link layer. Persistent Systems MPU5 adds a full IP router capability (802.11ac waveform) enabling video, voice, and data on the same mesh network.

    8.5 Military Datalinks¶

    8.5.1 Link 16 (JTIDS/MIDS)¶

    Link 16 is the primary tactical data link of NATO forces, standardised in MIL-STD-6016 and STANAG 5516. Physical layer characteristics:

    • Frequency: 960–1215 MHz (L-band); 51 pseudo-random frequency hops per second across the full band.

    • Modulation: MSK (Minimum Shift Keying) on each hop frequency.

    • Access: TDMA (Time Division Multiple Access); the time axis is divided into epochs (12.8 s) \(\rightarrow\) frames (12 s) \(\rightarrow\) time slots (7.8125 ms, 128 per frame). Each platform is assigned slots.

    • Data rate: 28.8 kbps (single pulse) or 57.6/115.2 kbps (double/packed-2 pulse).

    • Message sets: J-series (J0.0 – J29.5) covering aircraft tracks, navigation, weapons, electronic warfare, IFF.

    • Anti-jam: frequency hopping provides \(\sim\)13 dB process gain; voice-grade encryption; interleaved with Reed-Solomon FEC.

    • Platforms: F-22, F-35, E-3 AWACS, MQ-9 (Link 16 datalink pod), Eurofighter Typhoon.

    The MIDS-LVT (Multifunctional Information Distribution System – Low Volume Terminal) is the airborne implementation of Link 16 for fighter-class aircraft, weighing \(\approx\)4 kg in a standard ARINC 600 box. For the MQ-9 Reaper, a MIDS pod is carried on a wing station.

    8.5.2 Common Data Link (CDL)¶

    CDL (MIL-STD-2106) is the US DoD wideband aerial ISR (Intelligence, Surveillance, Reconnaissance) downlink standard. Characteristics:

    • Frequency: Ku-band (14–14.5 GHz uplink, 10.7–11.7 GHz downlink) primarily; X-band and Ka-band variants exist.

    • Data rates: 10.71, 21.4, 42.9, 137, 274 Mbps (defined in MIL-STD-2106C); 274 Mbps supports full-motion HD video plus secondary data.

    • Range: GEO satellite relay (TSAT notional) or point-to-point at 300+ km.

    • Platforms: U-2, Global Hawk, MQ-9, RC-135, E-8 JSTARS.

    • Terminals: Raytheon Advanced CDL (ACDL), L3Harris Terminal LXR.

    8.5.3 ROVER and Battlefield Video Relay¶

    ROVER (Remotely Operated Video Enhanced Receiver) is a receive-only terminal carried by ground troops (JTAC and Special Forces) to view full-motion video (FMV) from MALE UAVs (Predator, Reaper), ISR aircraft, and targeting pods (Sniper, Litening). ROVER 6 weighs 1.8 kg, supports CDL and commercial (COFDM) video formats, and runs on a ruggedised tablet interface. ROVER allows the ground unit to independently steer the UAV sensor via cursor-on-target (CoT) data and provides positive target identification before close air support (CAS) clearance.

    BACN (Battlefield Airborne Communications Node) is a broadband airborne relay using the E-11A (Bombardier Global Express variant) or EQ-4 (Block 20 Global Hawk), which carries a payload translating between multiple waveforms (Link 16, UHF SATCOM, MANET, VHSB) and extending communication range over mountainous terrain where direct LOS is blocked.

    8.5.4 Anti-Jam Techniques¶

    A jammer of power \(P_j\) at distance \(d_j\) creates a jam-to-signal ratio (JSR):

    \[\text{JSR} = \frac{P_j\,G_j}{P_t\,G_t} \cdot \left(\frac{d_t}{d_j}\right)^2\tag{8.18}\]

    where \(G_j\) is jammer antenna gain, \(d_t\) is the transmitter-to-receiver range.

    Countermeasures:

    • Frequency Hopping (FHSS): jammer must cover entire hopping band simultaneously; barrage jammer power per channel reduced by \(N_h\) (processing gain as in Eq. (8.15)).

    • Direct Sequence Spread Spectrum (DSSS): code gain \(G_c = B_{ss}/B_{info}\) rejects narrow-band jammers; GPS C/A code provides 43 dB code gain.

    • Null-steering antenna: adaptive phased array places a null in the direction of the jammer; \(> 30\) dB cancellation with a 4-element array.

    • Increased transmit power (burnthrough): receiver SNR improves faster than JSR for stand-off jammers as the communication link shortens at LOS.

    • Inertial coast: autopilot continues flying stored mission using IMU during GPS/comms outage (Section 7.4.5).

    8.6 Satellite Communications¶

    8.6.1 Iridium¶

    The Iridium LEO constellation (66 operational + 9 spare satellites at 781 km altitude, six orbital planes, 86.4° inclination) provides true global coverage including poles. Key characteristics:

    • Frequency: L-band (1616–1626.5 MHz) for user links; Ka-band (23.18–23.38 GHz) for inter-satellite links (ISLs).

    • Data rate: 2.4 kbps (9-channel TDMA/FDMA); Short Burst Data (SBD) packets of 1960 bytes maximum.

    • Latency: \(\sim\)400 ms round trip (ISL routing).

    • Terminal: 150 g antenna + modem. Iridium 9603N SBD modem: 11.4 g, 90 mm \(\times\) 54 mm.

    Iridium NEXT (2019+) adds L-band spot beams for IoT and 1.4 Mbps broadband (Iridium Certus service) on the same 66-satellite constellation.

    8.6.2 Inmarsat BGAN¶

    Inmarsat’s L-band (1525–1559 MHz down, 1626.5–1660.5 MHz up) geostationary satellites (I-4, I-5 series) support Broadband Global Area Network (BGAN) service offering 492 kbps background IP data and streaming QoS classes (32, 64, 128, 176 kbps).

    GEO orbit at 35,786 km yields one-way propagation delay:

    \[t_{prop} = \frac{d_{GEO}}{c} = \frac{35786 \times 10^3}{3 \times 10^8} \approx 119 \text{ ms}\tag{8.19}\]

    Round-trip latency: \(\approx 600\) ms. For drone command links, this latency requires the onboard autopilot to handle flight stabilisation autonomously; the satellite link is used only for high-level mission commands, status updates, and non-real-time video.

    8.6.3 Starlink for UAV Applications¶

    SpaceX Starlink operates a Walker constellation of \(> 6000\) LEO satellites at 340–550 km (Shell 1–5), providing global Ku/Ka-band (downlink 10.7–12.7/17.8–18.6 GHz; uplink 14.0–14.5/27.5–29.1 GHz) coverage with one-way latency:

    \[t_{LEO} = \frac{550 \times 10^3}{c} \approx 1.83 \text{ ms (slant range at zenith)}\tag{8.20}\]

    Actual round-trip latency: 20–40 ms, well suited to responsive UAV command and HD video. Throughput: 50–250 Mbps (user terminal dependent).

    The Starlink flat-panel terminal (Gen 3) uses a 2D AESA (Active Electronically Scanned Array) phased array (32 cm \(\times\) 19 cm) for electronic beam steering without mechanical pointing. Key parameters: 96 dBHz \(G/T\), 12 W idle / 45 W active power, 1.1 kg mass. UAV integration uses the Starlink Mobility plan (aviation version, FAA/ICAO certified terminal) on platforms such as the Airbus A380 (SAS airline), L3Harris Lightning II, and experimental UAS platforms. For small drones, Starlink mini (29 cm \(\times\) 25 cm, 370 g, \(>\)50 Mbps) was released in 2024.

    8.6.4 Wideband Global SATCOM (WGS)¶

    WGS (Boeing 702HP bus) is the US DoD’s primary high-capacity military satellite constellation (11 satellites as of 2024) at GEO. Characteristics:

    • Frequency: X-band (7.9–8.4 GHz up, 7.25–7.75 GHz down) and Ka-band (30 GHz up, 20 GHz down).

    • Capacity: \(\sim\)3.6 Gbps per satellite (uplink + downlink combined).

    • Bandwidth: 2.1 GHz X-band + 1 GHz Ka-band; digital channeliser allows flexible bandwidth allocation (5–125 MHz channels).

    • Anti-jam: X-band spot beam; null-steering; jamming resistance classified.

    • Platforms: Global Hawk, MQ-9 (via Block 50+ multi-band SATCOM), B-2, E-8, P-8.

    A WGS-capable UAV terminal (e.g., Raytheon Multi-Band Terminal, MBT) uses a 0.6 m Ka-band steerable dish or AESA flat panel, consuming 250–400 W RF power. Data throughput: up to 274 Mbps for a dedicated CDL-over-WGS link.

    8.6.5 SATCOM Terminal Design for UAVs¶

    Fixed dish antennas require mechanical 2-axis pointing with \(< 0.1\)° tracking accuracy. A servo gimbal with rate gyro feedback achieves this, but adds mass (0.5–2 kg for a 0.45 m Ku-band dish on a MALE UAV). Advantages: high gain, narrowband interference rejection.

    Electronically Steered Antennas (ESA) replace mechanical pointing with a phased array of hundreds to thousands of antenna elements, each with an individual phase shifter (PIN diode or liquid crystal phase shifter). The array steering vector for angle \(\theta\) (from boresight) in a uniform linear array (ULA) of \(N\) elements with spacing \(d = \lambda/2\):

    \[\mathbf{a}(\theta) = \begin{bmatrix}1,\,e^{j\pi\sin\theta},\,e^{j2\pi\sin\theta},\,…,\,e^{j(N-1)\pi\sin\theta}\end{bmatrix}^\top\tag{8.21}\]

    The array gain in direction \(\theta_0\) is:

    \[G(\theta_0) = N \cdot G_e \cdot |\mathbf{a}^\dagger(\theta_0)\mathbf{w}|^2\tag{8.22}\]

    where \(\mathbf{w}\) is the beamforming weight vector and \(G_e\) is single-element gain. For \(N = 256\) elements, maximum gain is \(24\) dBi above element gain. ESA terminals for Starlink aviation weigh \(< 2\) kg with no moving parts, achieving \(<\) 100 ms beam handover between satellites.

    8.7 Encryption and Cybersecurity¶

    8.7.1 Command Link Encryption¶

    Unencrypted RC and telemetry links are vulnerable to eavesdropping, replay attacks, and command injection. Best practice for civilian drone operators and mandatory for military systems:

    AES-256 (Advanced Encryption Standard, 256-bit key, FIPS PUB 197) is the standard cipher for data-link encryption. In GCM (Galois/Counter Mode), it provides both confidentiality and authentication (AEAD — Authenticated Encryption with Associated Data) with a 128-bit authentication tag, preventing bit-flip attacks. AES-256 in GCM achieves \(> 1\) Gbps throughput on ARM Cortex-M4 with hardware crypto acceleration.

    Key exchange: Pre-shared keys are impractical for dynamic deployments. ECDH (Elliptic Curve Diffie-Hellman) on Curve25519 provides \(\sim\)128-bit security equivalent with a 256-bit key, needing only 32 bytes per public key. The key exchange:

    \[\text{Shared secret} = [a]\,\mathbf{B} = [b]\,\mathbf{A} = [ab]\,\mathbf{G}\tag{8.23}\]

    where \(\mathbf{A} = [a]\mathbf{G}\), \(\mathbf{B} = [b]\mathbf{G}\) are public keys and \(\mathbf{G}\) is the generator point. Each power-cycle can use ephemeral ECDH (ECDHE) with X25519, providing perfect forward secrecy: compromise of the current session key does not expose past sessions.

    8.7.2 Low Probability of Intercept and Detection (LPI/LPD)¶

    LPI (Low Probability of Intercept) and LPD (Low Probability of Detection) are military requirements that make the control link difficult for adversaries to detect or exploit:

    • Spread spectrum: FHSS or DSSS raises the noise floor of the transmitted signal below the ambient electromagnetic noise background to a signal-intercept receiver.

    • Low transmit power: minimum power for link closure; reduces intercept range. A link operating at exactly the minimum viable power (0 dB link margin) is hardest to intercept; operational systems use 5–10 dB margin above this minimum.

    • Directional antenna on transmitter: concentrates power toward the receiver, reducing sidelobes detectable by intercept receivers at other angles.

    • Short burst transmissions: modem-on-demand; drone transmits telemetry in short bursts (0.1 s) every 1–5 seconds, reducing intercept exposure.

    8.7.3 Anti-Spoofing¶

    GPS spoofing — broadcasting fake GPS signals to mislead a drone’s navigation — is an increasingly significant threat. Countermeasures:

    • Galileo OSNMA (Open Service Navigation Message Authentication): cryptographic signatures on the navigation message, verified on-receiver. A spoofed signal cannot produce valid OSNMA signatures without the private key.

    • Multi-constellation cross-check: independent GPS and Galileo position solutions should agree within \(\sim\)3 m; sudden disagreement indicates spoofing of one.

    • RAIM (Receiver Autonomous Integrity Monitoring): uses overdetermined pseudorange solution to detect and isolate faulty satellites. ARAIM (Advanced RAIM) extends this to dual-constellation.

    • IMU dead-reckoning cross-check: velocity implied by GPS position change is compared to IMU-integrated velocity; discrepancy \(>\)2 m/s flags spoofing.

    8.7.4 Jamming Detection and Response¶

    Jamming detection combines multiple indicators:

    1. RSSI drop: SiK/ELRS report RSSI; a sudden drop from \(-\)75 to \(-\)110 dBm while no range increase is occurring indicates jamming.

    2. GPS signal strength: AGC (Automatic Gain Control) setting in GNSS receiver increases when broadband noise (jamming) is present; abnormal AGC is a flag.

    3. Packet error rate (PER) spike: MAVLink sequence number gaps indicate lost packets consistent with jamming.

    Response: 1) switch to IMU dead-reckoning + pre-programmed failsafe mission; 2) ascend to improve LOS path; 3) activate backup communication system (satellite link); 4) execute emergency landing at nearest safe coordinates.

    8.7.5 Zero-Trust Architecture¶

    Traditional avionics assumed a trusted network boundary. Modern drone systems, especially those using 4G/LTE and internet-connected GCS, must adopt zero-trust principles:

    • Per-aircraft PKI certificates: each aircraft has an asymmetric key pair issued by a root CA (Certificate Authority) specific to the operator’s fleet. Commands are signed by the GCS using its private key and verified by the aircraft’s public key, preventing command injection even if the VPN tunnel is compromised.

    • Message authentication codes (MAC): HMAC-SHA-256 on every MAVLink packet (exploiting the MAVLINK_IFLAG_SIGNED bit in MAVLink v2).

    • OTA (Over-the-Air) firmware update authentication: firmware images are signed with a manufacturer RSA-4096 key; the bootloader verifies before flashing, preventing supply-chain injection.

    8.8.2 ICAO BVLOS Requirements¶

    ICAO Annex 2 (Rules of the Air) and Doc 10019 (Manual on Remotely Piloted Aircraft Systems) define requirements for RPAS operating Beyond Visual Line of Sight:

    • The C2 (Command and Control) link must maintain continuous, reliable two-way communications between pilot and RPA throughout the operation.

    • Performance requirements: C2 link must achieve \(\leq 200\) ms end-to-end latency, \(\geq 99.9\)% availability, and include automatic failsafe (contingency procedure triggered on comms loss).

    • Frequency: C2 links must use licensed spectrum unless within authorised ISM band limitations; LDACS (L-band Digital Aeronautical Communications System) at 960–975 MHz is the ICAO-recommended standard for BVLOS C2 being standardised in EUROCAE ED-303.

    • Detect-and-Avoid (DAA): BVLOS aircraft must carry ADS-B Out (1090 MHz, 1090ES format, or equivalent DAA sensor suite) to be visible to crewed traffic. ADS-B Out requirement: position accuracy \(< 0.05\) NM NACp \(\geq\) 9.

    8.8.3 Military Spectrum Deconfliction¶

    Military spectrum management uses a frequency-coordination hierarchy:

    1. National: Each nation’s military frequency management office (e.g., DoD frequency assignment authority: NTIA/IRAC in the USA) maintains a master frequency register of active assignments.

    2. Operational: The Theatre Electronic Warfare Coordinator (TEWC) and Joint Spectrum Management Officer (JSMO) deconflict assignments within the joint operations area (JOA), issuing a Restricted Frequency List (RFL) and Taboo Frequency List for EMCON.

    3. Platform: Each UAV datalink is programmed with an SSE (Spectrum Supportability Evidence) approval and assigned specific channels/nets before flight.

    8.8.4 Cognitive Radio and Dynamic Spectrum Access¶

    Cognitive radio (CR) is an intelligent radio that senses the spectral environment and adapts its operating parameters (frequency, power, modulation, protocol) to maximise spectrum utilisation while avoiding interference to primary users.

    The primary user / secondary user model (IEEE 802.22 for TV white space; CBRS 3.5 GHz Citizens Broadband Radio Service in the USA) allows CR secondary users to operate in licensed bands when the primary user is absent:

    \[P(D) \geq P_D^{min},\quad P_{FA} \leq P_{FA}^{max}\tag{8.24}\]

    where \(P(D)\) is detection probability (\(\geq 0.9\)) and \(P_{FA}\) is false alarm probability (\(\leq 0.1\)) for the CR’s spectrum sensing function.

    CR technologies applicable to military drone links:

    • Dynamic frequency selection (DFS): already mandated in 5.8 GHz equipment to vacate on radar detection (ETSI EN 301 893).

    • Machine learning spectrum sensing: Deep-learning classifiers (ResNet-based) trained on RF signal spectrograms can identify emitter type with \(> 95\)% accuracy at SNR \(> 5\) dB, enabling intelligent interference avoidance.

    • MANET with adaptive waveform: Software-Defined Radio (SDR) platforms (Epiq Solutions Sidekiq, Ettus USRP B200) allow real-time waveform switching between Link 16, MUOS, and commercial waveforms based on spectrum availability.

    8.9 Integrated Communications Example: MQ-9 Reaper¶

    The MQ-9 Reaper illustrates integration of all communication subsystems discussed in this chapter on a single airframe:

    • LOS datalink: KU-band (Ku CDL); 50 km radius from GCS; 137 Mbps video downlink.

    • SATCOM: Ku-band SATCOM terminal (Inmarsat or WGS); BLOS operations over Afghanistan from operators at Creech AFB, Nevada.

    • Link 16: MIDS-LVT pod for TAD (Tactical Air Direction) and ID.

    • ATC/IFF: UHF voice radio (AN/ARC-210, 108–512 MHz); IFF Mode 1/2/3A/C/S/4.

    • Video relay: CDL-format Predator ISR video downlink; ROVER receive across JTAC ground teams.

    • Spectrum: uplink \(\leq\)14.5 GHz (Ku), downlink 10.7–11.7 GHz; coordinated via JSMO before each sortie.

    • Encryption: Type 1 NSA-approved (classified algorithm, KMI key management); mission key loaded at pre-flight via KSD-64 or KIK-30 crypto loader.

    The GCS (Ground Control Station, AN/MPS-46 or Portable Flight Control System) connects to the Ku terminal via coaxial cable in the shelter; satellite uplink latency of 600 ms requires operators to anticipate manoeuvres and the onboard autopilot to stabilise independently.

    8.10 Future Directions in Drone Communications¶

    8.10.1 5G NR for UAV C2¶

    3GPP Release 17 (2022) standardised NR-U2X (New Radio UAV-to-X) communications, including specific 5G NR features for low-latency (\(< 10\) ms), high-reliability (\(99.999\)%) command links for BVLOS drone operations. 5G NR at 3.5 GHz (mid-band) provides \(\sim\)100 Mbps aggregate per cell with network slicing to guarantee QoS for drone C2 independently of consumer traffic. By 2026, UTM (Unmanned Traffic Management) providers including NASA and EASA are piloting 5G drone corridors in major European cities.

    8.10.2 Quantum Key Distribution (QKD)¶

    QKD (BB84 protocol, NIST SP 1800-38) distributes encryption keys using quantum photon states that cannot be eavesdropped without disturbance, providing information-theoretically secure key exchange. Satellite-based QKD (Chinese Micius satellite, 2016) demonstrated 1200 km quantum key distribution. Miniaturised QKD terminals for UAVs (\(< 5\) kg) are under development at Toshiba Research (UK) and QuiX Quantum (Netherlands), targeting practical deployment by the late 2020s for the most sensitive military UAV platforms.

    8.10.3 Laser / Free-Space Optical Communications¶

    High-data-rate laser communications (lasercom) at 1550 nm wavelength offer 1–10 Gbps throughput in narrow beams (\(< 0.01\)° divergence), with essentially zero probability of intercept. DARPA’s PACE (Persistent Aquatic Living Sensors) and LCRD (Laser Communications Relay Demonstration, NASA) have demonstrated Gbps optical links from GEO. For drone-to-ground lasercom, adaptive optics must compensate atmospheric turbulence — the phase distortion coherence diameter \(r_0\) (Fried parameter):

    \[r_0 = 0.185\left(\frac{\lambda^2}{\bar{C}_n^2 L}\right)^{3/5}\tag{8.25}\]

    where \(C_n^2\) is the refractive index structure parameter and \(L\) is path length. Typical \(r_0 = 5\)–20 cm at 1550 nm for 1 km slant path; deformable mirrors with 100–1000 actuators can correct down to \(r_0\) scale.

    Summary¶

    This chapter has presented a comprehensive treatment of drone and missile communication systems, grounded in the fundamental physics of Maxwell’s equations and the Friis transmission equation. The link budget framework unifies the analysis of all communication links, from the simplest ExpressLRS RC control link to the Wideband Global SATCOM system. RC control links (ELRS, Crossfire) and telemetry systems (SiK, RFD900+) serve the short-to-medium range civilian and commercial drone market; military platforms require the anti-jam, authentication, and wideband capabilities of Link 16, CDL, and WGS. Video downlinks span analogue FPV (zero-latency, degraded quality) to COFDM encrypted HD systems. Satellite communications using Iridium, Inmarsat, Starlink, and WGS enable global BVLOS and persistent ISR operations. Cybersecurity — AES-256 encryption, ECDH key exchange, GNSS anti-spoofing, and zero-trust PKI architectures — is now inseparable from communications system design. Cognitive radio, 5G NR C2, quantum key distribution, and free-space optical links represent the next technology frontier for drone communications.
