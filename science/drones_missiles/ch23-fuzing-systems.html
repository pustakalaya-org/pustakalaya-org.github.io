    Chapter 23: Fuzing Systems¶

    23.1 Fuze Fundamentals¶

    A fuze is the electromechanical, electronic, or purely mechanical device responsible for initiating the explosive train of a warhead at precisely the correct time, place, and physical condition. The fuze must discriminate between the operational firing environment and all other situations — including transportation, storage, accidental shock, and electromagnetic interference — and must initiate only when the target engagement criteria are satisfied. This dual demand — extreme reliability of function combined with extreme resistance to inadvertent initiation — makes fuze engineering one of the most tightly regulated disciplines in the defence industry.

    23.1.1 Safety and Reliability Requirements¶

    The governing standard in the United States and many allied nations is MIL-STD-1316, which specifies the safety philosophy for fuze design. The primary quantitative safety requirement is:

    \[P_{\text{inadvertent}} \leq 1 \times 10^{-6}\tag{23.1}\]

    That is, the probability of inadvertent initiation under any out-of-design environment shall not exceed one in one million. This is achieved through the use of multiple, independent safety devices arranged in series so that all must be defeated simultaneously before the fuze can arm. If each device has an individual failure probability \(p_i\), the combined probability satisfies:

    \[P_{\text{combined}} = \prod_{i=1}^{N} p_i\tag{23.2}\]

    For \(N = 3\) devices each with \(p_i = 10^{-2}\), the product yields \(10^{-6}\), meeting the requirement. In practice, high-reliability components drive individual \(p_i\) far below \(10^{-2}\).

    The complementary requirement is reliability of function: once the fuze is armed and target-engagement criteria are met, initiation must occur with a probability of no less than \(0.999\) (three nines). These two requirements — safety and reliability — form opposing design drivers that must be balanced through careful engineering.

    23.1.2 The Arming Sequence¶

    Every fuze passes through a defined sequence of states:

    1. Safe: The detonator is mechanically or electronically isolated from the warhead explosive train. The weapon may be handled, transported, and loaded.

    2. Armed: All safety devices have been released by the detection of authorised environmental stimuli. The detonator is in line with the explosive train. The fuze actively monitors for firing criteria.

    3. Fired: The initiator has been triggered and energy has propagated through the fuze train to detonate the warhead.

    Transition from Safe to Armed requires the simultaneous satisfaction of all independent arming conditions. Transition from Armed to Fired requires the detection of target-engagement criteria (impact, proximity, time-on-target, or command).

    23.1.3 Fuze Classification¶

    Fuzes are classified according to their method of initiating detonation:

    • Point Detonating (PD): Initiates on physical contact with the target surface. Sub-types: superquick (\(<0.1\) ms), delay (10–100 ms), and graze (functions at shallow angle of impact).

    • Proximity (VT): Initiates when the projectile or missile passes within a preset standoff distance of the target, without physical contact. Sub-types: radio frequency (RF), laser, optical shadow, acoustic.

    • Time: Initiates after a preset elapsed time from launch. Mechanical clockwork or electronic timing.

    • Command: Initiates upon receipt of a coded signal from an external platform (aircraft, ground station, or datalink).

    • Delay: Allows the projectile to penetrate before detonation. Used against hardened bunkers and reinforced concrete.

    • Combination: Implements two or more modes in a single body (e.g., PD + delay selectable, or proximity + PD back-up).

    23.1.4 The Fuze Train¶

    The explosive energy path from the fuze initiator to the warhead main charge is called the fuze train:

    \[\text{Initiator} \;\rightarrow\; \text{Booster (secondary explosive)} \;\rightarrow\; \text{Lead / Relay charge} \;\rightarrow\; \text{Main detonator charge} \;\rightarrow\; \text{Warhead main fill}\]

    Each component in the train amplifies the detonation energy while reducing sensitivity to accidental initiation. The initiator (stab, electric, or piezoelectric) is the most sensitive; the warhead main fill (RDX, HMX, PBXN) is the least sensitive. The booster (typically PETN or RDX-based composition) bridges the gap.

    The critical parameter is the gap sensitivity: the maximum air gap over which the detonation wave can propagate reliably. For military boosters this is typically 2–6 mm. The out-of-line initiator concept exploits this: by misaligning the initiator by more than the gap sensitivity distance, inadvertent initiation cannot propagate to the warhead even if the initiator fires.

    Booster PETN / RDX 1.65 / 1.77 8,300 / 8,750 Medium
    Lead charge RDX / HMX 1.77 / 1.89 8,750 / 9,100 Low

    23.2 Safe and Arm Devices¶

    Safe and Arm (S&A) devices are the hardware implementations of the safety philosophy. They physically prevent the detonation train from being complete until all arming conditions are met.

    23.2.1 Electromechanical Rotor S&A¶

    The electromechanical rotor is the most widely used S&A design. A cylindrical rotor contains the initiator charge in a bore perpendicular to its rotation axis. In the safe position, the initiator bore is misaligned with the booster column by \(90°\) (or other angle), ensuring an air gap greater than the detonation gap sensitivity. An electric motor (or clockwork spring in older designs) rotates the rotor to the armed position when all sensor inputs are satisfied.

    The rotation is controlled by a logic circuit monitoring environmental sensors. A mechanical detent latches the rotor in both the safe and the armed positions, preventing intermediate states. The rotor also carries an environmental sensor that must detect the prescribed launch stimuli — setback acceleration, spin rate, time delay, or altitude — before the motor is energised.

    Arming distance (minimum travel before arming):

    \[D_{\text{arm}} = V_0 \cdot t_{\text{arm}}\tag{23.3}\]

    where \(V_0\) is the muzzle or launch velocity and \(t_{\text{arm}}\) is the minimum arming time (typically 20–60 m for artillery fuzes, 1–5 m for guided missiles depending on the platform).

    23.2.2 MEMS Safe and Arm Devices¶

    Micro-Electromechanical Systems (MEMS) S&A devices are silicon micro-machined equivalents of the rotor S&A. Fabricated using standard semiconductor photolithography, they achieve very small size (2–10 mm die) with inherently reliable performance due to lack of wear-prone mechanical assemblies.

    A typical MEMS S&A consists of:

    • A MEMS accelerometer sensing setback \(g\) (the launch-phase inertial load).

    • A micro-rotor or slide mechanism driven by comb-drive electrostatic actuator.

    • A micro-initiator (semiconductor bridge or thin-film resistor) in the detonation channel, which becomes in-line only after the mechanism reaches the armed position.

    The silicon micro-machined slide typically requires a sustained acceleration above a threshold \(a_{\text{arm}}\) for a minimum time \(t_{\min}\) to overcome a restoring spring force:

    \[\int_0^{t_{\min}} a(t)\, dt \geq v_{\text{threshold}}\tag{23.4}\]

    This impulse criterion prevents single-shock events (drops, handling shocks) from arming the device, since their duration is too short to accumulate sufficient velocity.

    23.2.3 Environmental Sensing and Arming Conditions¶

    Multiple independent environmental conditions are sensed to confirm the munition is in a legitimate launch profile:

    1. Setback acceleration: For gun-launched projectiles, setback \(g\) values of \(1{,}000\)–\(20{,}000\,g\) for 1–10 ms are characteristic and extremely difficult to replicate by accident. For rocket-launched missiles, setback may be \(5\)–\(50\,g\).

    2. Spin rate (centrifugal arming): For spin-stabilised projectiles, a centrifugal arming pin is displaced outward by spin rate above a threshold \(\omega_{\text{arm}}\):

      \[F_{\text{centrifugal}} = m_{\text{pin}} \cdot \omega^2 \cdot r \geq F_{\text{spring}}\tag{23.5}\]

      Typically \(\omega_{\text{arm}} \sim 50\)–\(200\,\text{rev/s}\) for spin-stabilised rounds.

    3. Time delay: An electronic or mechanical timer prevents arming before a minimum elapsed time, ensuring safe separation from the launch platform.

    4. Altitude or pressure: For air-dropped munitions, a barometric switch confirms that the munition has descended (or ascended to target altitude), preventing premature arming at the aircraft.

    5. Velocity: Pitot-static measurement confirms the munition is in supersonic or high-subsonic flight.

    23.2.4 Thermal Battery Arming¶

    Missile fuzes and electronic warhead systems require electrical power. The preferred power source is a thermal battery: a lithium/iron disulphide (Li/FeS\(_2\)) or lithium/calcium chromate (Li/CaCrO\(_4\)) primary cell that is activated by a pyrotechnic heat pellet. In the unactivated state, the electrolyte is solid and non-conductive; no power is available to the fuze. On launch, the squib (small pyrotechnic charge) ignites the heat pellet, melting the electrolyte, and the battery delivers full voltage within 0.5–2 s:

    \[V_{\text{batt}}(t) = V_{\text{OC}} \left(1 - e^{-t / \tau_{\text{batt}}}\right)\tag{23.6}\]

    where \(V_{\text{OC}}\) is the open-circuit voltage (28 V typical) and \(\tau_{\text{batt}}\) is the electrochemical activation time constant. The thermal battery itself constitutes an arming event: without it, no electrical energy reaches the electronic fuze, rendering it inert.

    23.3 Point Detonating Fuzes¶

    23.3.1 Superquick (Instantaneous) PD Fuzes¶

    The superquick PD fuze initiates within \(0.1\) ms of impact. At an impact velocity of \(400\) m/s, this corresponds to a penetration depth of only \(40\) mm before detonation — effectively surface burst. This is desired for anti-personnel and soft-target fragmentation effects.

    The functional sequence is:

    \[\text{Nose impact (crush element)} \rightarrow \text{Stab initiator fires} \rightarrow \text{Booster} \rightarrow \text{Detonation}\]

    The crush element (also called the nose piece or ogive) deforms on impact, driving a firing pin into the stab initiator. The force required to initiate the stab initiator is typically \(5\)–\(15\,\text{N}\) at impact velocities exceeding \(30\,\text{m/s}\).

    23.3.2 Delay Fuzes¶

    For penetration of hardened targets (reinforced concrete, armour, hardened bunkers), a delay fuze allows the projectile to penetrate before detonation. The delay element is a pyrotechnic column (e.g., barium chromate/boron mixture) or an electronic RC-timer circuit.

    Pyrotechnic delay column burn time:

    \[t_d = \frac{L_{\text{col}}}{\dot{r}_{\text{burn}}}\tag{23.7}\]

    where \(L_{\text{col}}\) is the column length and \(\dot{r}_{\text{burn}}\) is the linear burn rate (typically 20–100 mm/s). Electronic delay timers achieve accuracy of \(\pm 1\,\mu\text{s}\), far superior to pyrotechnic elements (\(\pm 5\%\) of nominal).

    Penetration depth before detonation (simplified):

    \[d_{\text{pen}} = V_{\text{impact}} \cdot t_d - \frac{1}{2} a_{\text{decel}} \cdot t_d^2\tag{23.8}\]

    where \(a_{\text{decel}}\) is the mean deceleration during penetration, estimated from cavity expansion theory (Forrestal–Warren model).

    23.3.3 Graze Fuzes¶

    A graze fuze initiates when the projectile impacts at a very shallow angle of attack (graze angle \(\gamma < 5°\)). Standard nose-impact fuzes fail to function at graze because the axial crush force is insufficient. Graze fuzes use a side-acting or base-acting initiator that responds to the lateral deceleration pulse during oblique impact.

    23.3.4 Piezoelectric Initiators¶

    Piezoelectric crystals (PZT, quartz, or tourmaline) generate a high-voltage pulse on mechanical impact without requiring any electrical power supply. The open-circuit voltage on impact is:

    \[V_{\text{piezo}} = \frac{g_{33} \cdot \sigma \cdot L}{t_{\text{crystal}}}\tag{23.9}\]

    where \(g_{33}\) is the piezoelectric voltage constant (\(\approx 24 \times 10^{-3}\,\text{V·m/N}\) for PZT-5A), \(\sigma\) is the applied stress, \(L\) is the crystal length, and \(t_{\text{crystal}}\) is the crystal thickness. Voltages of 500–5,000 V are achievable, far exceeding the 50–100 V required to fire an electric detonator. The self-powered nature of piezoelectric fuzes makes them attractive for simple PD applications where no battery is available.

    Type Initiation Delay Penetration Target Example
    Superquick PD \(< 0.1\) ms Negligible (\(< 0.05\) m) Soft / area M557
    Short delay PD 10–25 ms 0.2–1.0 m Masonry, earth M739A1

    23.4 Proximity Fuzes¶

    Proximity fuzes detonate the warhead at a standoff distance from the target, maximising the damage radius of the fragmentation or blast effect without requiring a direct hit. This is particularly important for air-defence missiles engaging high-speed manoeuvring aircraft, where a direct hit is geometrically improbable.

    23.4.1 Radio Frequency Proximity Fuzes¶

    The RF proximity fuze transmits a continuous-wave (CW) or pulsed RF signal and detects the Doppler shift or time-of-flight of the return signal reflected from the target. The classic Doppler proximity fuze (first employed in the VT fuze of World War II) detects the beat frequency between the transmitted and received signals:

    \[f_D = \frac{2 V_r}{\lambda}\tag{23.10}\]

    where \(V_r\) is the radial closure velocity between the fuze and target and \(\lambda\) is the RF wavelength. For a missile approaching a target at \(V_r = 500\,\text{m/s}\) with \(\lambda = 0.15\,\text{m}\) (2 GHz), \(f_D \approx 6.7\,\text{kHz}\) — well within the audio range and easily detected by simple electronics.

    The fuze fires when the Doppler signal exceeds a threshold amplitude, corresponding to a miss distance of 3–10 m depending on the antenna pattern and signal processing. Modern RF fuzes use pulsed or FMCW waveforms to measure range directly:

    \[R_{\text{target}} = \frac{c \cdot \Delta t}{2}\tag{23.11}\]

    where \(\Delta t\) is the round-trip propagation time. The fuze fires when \(R_{\text{target}}\) falls below \(R_{\text{opt}}\) (optimal burst radius).

    23.4.2 Active Millimetre-Wave Proximity Fuzes¶

    Operating at 35–94 GHz, millimetre-wave (mmW) fuzes achieve narrow beamwidths (\(\theta \sim 5°\)–\(10°\)) that reduce ground-clutter interference. They are used on modern surface-to-air missiles (e.g., Patriot PAC-3) where the missile must discriminate between the target aircraft and ground returns at low elevation angles.

    The radar cross-section of the target at mmW frequencies:

    \[\sigma_{\text{target}}(\text{dBsm}) = \sigma_0 + 20\log_{10}\left(\frac{R_{\text{ref}}}{R}\right)\tag{23.12}\]

    The received power at range \(R\) follows the radar range equation:

    \[P_r = \frac{P_t G_t G_r \lambda^2 \sigma}{(4\pi)^3 R^4 L_s}\tag{23.13}\]

    where \(P_t\) is transmit power, \(G_t\) and \(G_r\) are transmit and receive antenna gains, \(\sigma\) is target radar cross-section, and \(L_s\) is system losses.

    23.4.3 Laser Proximity Fuzes¶

    Laser fuzes transmit a pulsed or modulated infrared beam and measure time-of-flight to the target. The narrow beam (\(<2°\) full angle) rejects off-axis clutter and is inherently immune to RF jamming. Detection range:

    \[R_{\text{max}} = \left( \frac{P_t \cdot A_r \cdot \rho_t \cdot \tau_a^2 \cdot \eta_{\text{sys}}}{4 \pi \cdot E_{\text{min}}} \right)^{1/2}\tag{23.14}\]

    where \(P_t\) is peak pulse power, \(A_r\) is receiver aperture area, \(\rho_t\) is target reflectance, \(\tau_a\) is one-way atmospheric transmissivity, \(\eta_{\text{sys}}\) is optical efficiency, and \(E_{\text{min}}\) is minimum detectable energy.

    Multiple laser beams arranged around the missile body allow \(360°\) azimuthal coverage. The fuze fires when any beam returns within the arming gate time window.

    23.4.4 Acoustic Proximity Fuzes¶

    Used primarily in underwater torpedoes, acoustic proximity fuzes detect the broadband noise signature of a ship’s propellers or machinery, or the pressure perturbation in the ship’s wake. Wake-homing fuzes detect the reduced dissolved oxygen content or the micro-bubble density of the wake. Target detection range in acoustic fuzes is typically 2–8 m depending on target noise level and ambient sea noise.

    23.4.5 Optical Shadow Fuze¶

    The optical shadow fuze uses a photodiode array or phototransistor to detect the reduction in solar irradiance when an aircraft passes between the fuze and the sun. It is passive and therefore undetectable by electronic warfare. The irradiance change:

    \[\Delta E = E_{\text{solar}} \cdot \frac{A_{\text{shadow}}}{4\pi R^2}\tag{23.15}\]

    where \(A_{\text{shadow}}\) is the projected shadow area of the target aircraft at range \(R\).

    23.4.6 Optimal Burst Height¶

    The optimal burst height (OBH) maximises the fragment density (fragments per unit area) incident on the target. For a cylindrical warhead with fragment spray half-angle \(\alpha\) and total fragment number \(N_f\), the fragment surface density at range \(R\) from detonation:

    \[\rho_f(R) = \frac{N_f}{2\pi R^2 (1 - \cos\alpha)}\tag{23.16}\]

    The optimum standoff \(R_{\text{opt}}\) balances fragment density against lateral dispersion over the target projected area \(A_t\):

    \[R_{\text{opt}} = \sqrt{\frac{N_f \cdot A_t}{2\pi \cdot n_{\text{kill}} \cdot (1-\cos\alpha)}}\tag{23.17}\]

    where \(n_{\text{kill}}\) is the required number of fragments on target for a kill. For typical SAM fuzes engaging aircraft, OBH is in the range of 3–15 m depending on warhead size and target type.

    23.5 Electronic and Programmable Fuzes¶

    23.5.1 Programmable Height-of-Burst Fuzes¶

    Modern artillery and air-delivered weapons employ programmable fuzes that receive their settings via a fuze setter (inductive or contact coupling) immediately before firing. The fuze stores the selected mode — superquick, delay, time-to-burst, or height-of-burst — in a non-volatile register. The electronic timer counts elapsed time from launch:

    \[t_{\text{HOB}} = t_{\text{flight}} - \Delta t_{\text{margin}}\tag{23.18}\]

    where \(\Delta t_{\text{margin}}\) provides a safety margin before ground impact. For height-of-burst fuzing, the fuze incorporates a radar altimeter that measures altitude above ground and triggers at a commanded AGL height.

    23.5.2 Smart Fuzes with Target Recognition¶

    Smart fuzes incorporate a microprocessor and sensor suite (accelerometer, deceleration profile measurement) that infers the target type from the penetration signature. The deceleration pulse during target penetration is diagnostic:

    • Soil/earth: gradual deceleration, \(50\)–\(200\,g\), duration \(>5\) ms.

    • Masonry: sharp deceleration spike, \(200\)–\(500\,g\), then residual.

    • Reinforced concrete: \(500\)–\(2{,}000\,g\) with distinct rebar signature pulses.

    • Steel armour: Very sharp spike, \(2{,}000\)–\(10{,}000\,g\), short duration.

    Based on the signature, the fuze selects the appropriate delay (or switches to a different detonation mode). Pattern recognition is implemented via a trained threshold classifier or simple peak-detection with duration gating in firmware.

    23.5.3 GPS-Aided Fuzes¶

    Precision-guided munitions carry a GPS receiver in the fuze body. The fuze tracks GPS position throughout flight and calculates predicted impact time from the current state vector. Burst point can be commanded as a GPS coordinate:

    \[\mathbf{r}_{\text{burst}} = \mathbf{r}_{\text{target}} + \Delta h \cdot \hat{z}\tag{23.19}\]

    where \(\Delta h\) is the commanded burst height. Combined with an inertial measurement unit (IMU), the GPS-aided fuze can achieve burst-point accuracy with CEP \(< 5\) m even after GPS is denied in terminal phase (GPS coasting with IMU).

    23.5.4 Multi-Option Fuze for Artillery (MOFA)¶

    The MOFA (adopted by the US Army as M782) provides four functions in a single fuze body: (1) superquick PD, (2) delay PD, (3) proximity airburst, and (4) time-of-flight airburst. The operator selects the mode via a portable fuze setter (PFS) which communicates with the fuze via a 1553B or RS-422 serial interface. The fuze stores up to four different burst settings simultaneously.

    Table 23.3. Multi-option fuze operational modes
    Mode Initiation Trigger Timing Accuracy Application
    Superquick PD Impact (\(< 0.1\) ms) N/A Anti-personnel, vehicles
    Delay PD Impact + timer (10–100 ms) \(\pm 1\,\mu\)s Bunkers, masonry
    Proximity airburst Radar/laser HOB 4–10 m \(\pm 0.5\) m Anti-personnel in defilade
    Time airburst Elapsed time from firing \(\pm 5\) ms Suppression, illumination

    23.5.5 Wind Correction in the Fuze Computer¶

    For precision air-burst fuzing, ballistic drift must be accounted for. A nose-mounted anemometer or inertially-derived wind estimate feeds a fuze-resident ballistic computer. The lateral displacement due to cross-wind over time of flight \(T\):

    \[y_{\text{drift}} = \frac{1}{2} \left(\frac{W_{\text{cross}}}{V_0}\right) g T^2\tag{23.20}\]

    where \(W_{\text{cross}}\) is the cross-wind component and \(V_0\) is initial velocity. The fuze computer applies a correction to the burst time or proximity threshold to account for this drift, maintaining target engagement geometry.

    23.6 Fuze Testing and Qualification¶

    Fuze qualification follows a structured program combining environmental, functional, and safety testing.

    23.6.1 Acceleration and Setback Testing¶

    An acceleration sled or air gun reproduces launch setback loads without propellant, allowing instrumented fuze function testing at controlled \(g\) levels. Typical test parameters:

    • Peak acceleration: 100 g to 20,000 g (depending on platform).

    • Pulse duration: 1–50 ms (half-sine or trapezoidal profile).

    • Required result: fuze does NOT arm below minimum stimulus; DOES arm above.

    The probability of arming as a function of stimulus level follows a logistic (sigmoid) function:

    \[P_{\text{arm}}(a) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-k(a - a_{50})}}\tag{23.21}\]

    where \(a_{50}\) is the 50% arming acceleration and \(k\) is the slope parameter. The fuze must have a sharp transition (large \(k\)) to maximise the discrimination between safe and armed stimuli.

    23.6.2 Spin Table Testing¶

    For spin-arming fuzes, a centrifuge or spin table applies controlled angular velocity. The fuze is mounted with its spin axis aligned with the table axis. Spin arming must occur above \(\omega_{\text{arm}}\) and must NOT occur below \(\omega_{\text{safe}}\), providing a defined no-arm band below the arming threshold.

    23.6.3 MIL-STD-810 Environmental Tests¶

    All fuzes must survive the following environmental conditions without malfunction or degradation of safety:

    • Temperature: \(-54°C\) to \(+71°C\) operational; \(-62°C\) to \(+85°C\) storage (MIL-STD-810 Method 501/502).

    • Humidity: 95% RH at \(40°C\) for 240 hours (Method 507).

    • Vibration: Frequency sweep 5–2,000 Hz; power spectral density per platform specification (Method 514).

    • Shock: Half-sine 40 g, 11 ms (transport shock); sawtooth 100 g, 6 ms (handling drop) (Method 516).

    • Salt fog: 5% NaCl solution at \(35°C\) for 48 hours (Method 509).

    23.6.4 Insensitive Munition (IM) Fuze Tests¶

    Under MIL-STD-2105, munitions (including fuzes) must not undergo mass detonation (Reaction Level 1) when subjected to the following Insensitive Munition stimuli:

    • Slow cook-off (SCO): 3.3°C/hour ramp from ambient to function.

    • Fast cook-off (FCO): Pool fire at \(800°C\) for minimum 10 minutes.

    • Bullet impact (BI): 12.7 mm projectile at 850 m/s.

    • Fragment impact (FI): 16.6 g FSP at 2,530 ft/s.

    • Shaped charge jet (SCJ): 84 mm HEAT warhead.

    • Sympathetic detonation (SYD): Adjacent donor detonation.

    Fuze designs must minimise the probability of inducing mass detonation in these events, using insensitive explosive compositions and protective structural features.

    23.6.5 Statistical Proof Testing and Lot Acceptance¶

    Lot acceptance testing uses statistical sampling to demonstrate compliance with the reliability specification. For a required reliability \(R = 0.999\) (one failure per thousand) with confidence level \(C = 0.95\), the required sample size \(n\) for zero-failure acceptance is derived from the binomial distribution:

    \[n = \frac{\ln(1 - C)}{\ln(R)} = \frac{\ln(0.05)}{\ln(0.999)} \approx 2{,}995\tag{23.22}\]

    This means that if \(n = 2{,}995\) fuzes are tested with zero failures, we can be 95% confident that the true reliability is at least 0.999. In practice, round-to-fire testing is supplemented by component-level testing and analysis to reduce the total number of complete system firings.

    The binomial confidence interval on observed reliability \(\hat{R} = 1 - k/n\) (where \(k\) is the number of failures):

    \[P\left(R \geq 1 - \frac{\chi^2_{1-\alpha,\,2(k+1)}}{2n}\right) = 1 - \alpha\tag{23.23}\]

    23.6.6 Electromagnetic Compatibility and RF Hazard¶

    Fuzes containing electric initiators must demonstrate immunity to RF-induced firing (HERO — Hazards of Electromagnetic Radiation to Ordnance). The test methodology per MIL-STD-1310 includes:

    • CW field exposure at frequencies 14 kHz to 40 GHz.

    • EMP simulation (MIL-STD-461 RS105): 50,000 V/m field rise \(\leq 2.5\) ns.

    • Lightning indirect effects (DO-160 Section 22).

    • The firing current threshold for the initiator must not be reached under any plausible ambient EM environment.

    The no-fire current for a standard M55 electric initiator is \(0.45\) A (all-fire at \(1.0\) A). The induced current in the firing circuit under an external field \(E\) (V/m) through a loop of area \(A\) and circuit resistance \(Z\):

    \[I_{\text{induced}} = \frac{E \cdot \sqrt{A}}{\lambda \cdot Z}\tag{23.24}\]

    Fuze circuit designs incorporate spark gaps, ferrite beads, bypass capacitors, and twisted-pair wiring to limit \(I_{\text{induced}}\) to no more than \(20\%\) of the no-fire current.

    23.6.7 Fuze Development Programme Timeline¶

    A typical fuze development programme from concept to qualification spans 5–8 years:

    1. Technology Maturation: 12–18 months (component breadboards, lethality modelling).

    2. Engineering Development Model (EDM): 18–24 months (first hardware, DT&E).

    3. System Development and Demonstration (SDD): 24–36 months (qualification testing).

    4. Production Qualification Testing (PQT): 6–12 months (first lot acceptance).

    Cost drivers include the large number of explosive function tests required, the specialised range and sled facilities needed, and the strict configuration management of explosive compositions and component tolerances.
