    Chapter 15: Special Drone Categories¶

    15.1 Introduction to Special-Category Unmanned Systems¶

    The taxonomy of unmanned systems extends far beyond the conventional fixed-wing reconnaissance aircraft or consumer multi-rotor quadcopter. Operational requirements spanning the full spectrum of physical environments—from abyssal ocean trenches to the stratosphere, from congested urban airspace to electromagnetic-warfare corridors—have given rise to highly specialised unmanned platforms, each engineered to overcome a unique set of physical, regulatory, and mission-specific constraints. This chapter provides a comprehensive technical treatment of ten special categories: Underwater Unmanned Vehicles (UUV), Unmanned Surface Vessels (USV), High-Altitude Long-Endurance (HALE) platforms, Urban Air Mobility (UAM) eVTOL aircraft, Stratospheric Pseudo-Satellites (HAPS), Combat Unmanned Air Systems (UCAV), Nano and Micro UAVs, Logistics and Cargo Drones, Agricultural Drones, and Electronic Warfare Drones.

    Each section provides governing equations, system-level performance data, and commentary on representative programmes, enabling the reader to undertake first-principles design analysis for any category.

    15.2 Underwater Unmanned Vehicles (UUV)¶

    15.2.1 Classification¶

    The term Unmanned Underwater Vehicle (UUV) is the overarching designation for all uncrewed submersible platforms. The principal sub-categories are:

    • Remotely Operated Vehicle (ROV): Tethered to a surface vessel or platform via an umbilical cable carrying power (3-phase AC up to 3 kV step-down) and fibre-optic communications. Real-time pilot control. Unlimited endurance while tethered. Applications include deep-sea infrastructure inspection, cable lay, and ROV-assisted salvage. Representative systems: Schilling Robotics HD ROV (4,000 m rated), Oceaneering Magnum PLUS (3,000 m), NOAA Deep Discoverer (6,000 m).

    • Autonomous Underwater Vehicle (AUV): Self-contained; pre-programmed mission or on-board AI decision-making; no umbilical. Endurance limited by battery capacity. Applications include mine countermeasures (MCM), oceanographic survey, submarine pipeline inspection, and intelligence collection. Representative systems: Bluefin-21, REMUS 100/600, Iver3 AUV.

    • Underwater Glider: AUV sub-class exploiting variable buoyancy to generate vertical motion, which fixed wings convert to horizontal displacement. Extremely low power consumption (hotel load \(\sim\)1–3 W) enables weeks-to-months endurance. Representative systems: Slocum G3 (Teledyne Webb), Seaglider (Kongsberg), Spray (SIO/WHOI).

    • Extra-Large UUV (XLUUV): Long-range, high-payload, nominally fully autonomous vehicles with diesel-AIP propulsion and multi-month endurance. Designed for covert ISR, mine laying, and payload delivery. Boeing Orca XLUUV (15.5 m, 45 tonnes) is the defining current programme.

    15.2.2 Hydrodynamics¶

    The dominant resistance on a submerged body is viscous and pressure drag. For a body of revolution at speed \(V\) through a fluid of density \(\rho\):

    \[F_D = \frac{1}{2} \rho \, C_D \, A_{ref} \, V^2\tag{15.1}\]

    where \(C_D\) is the drag coefficient (0.15–0.35 for streamlined torpedo-form hulls) and \(A_{ref}\) is the maximum cross-sectional area. The fundamental challenge of underwater operation is the density ratio:

    \[\frac{\rho_{\text{seawater}}}{\rho_{\text{air, SL}}} = \frac{1025\ \text{kg/m}^3}{1.225\ \text{kg/m}^3} \approx 837\tag{15.2}\]

    Consequently, at identical speeds the hydrodynamic drag on a submerged body is approximately \(800\times\) greater than the aerodynamic drag on an identical body in air. UUVs therefore operate at much lower speeds (1–5 kt) compared to their aerial counterparts to maintain manageable propulsive power demands.

    The Reynolds number governs boundary-layer character:

    \[Re = \frac{\rho V L}{\mu}\tag{15.3}\]

    For a 2 m AUV at 2 kt (\(\approx\)1.03 m/s), with dynamic viscosity of seawater \(\mu = 1.08\times10^{-3}\) Pa\(\cdot\)s: \(Re \approx 1.95\times10^6\), placing operation in the transitional-to-turbulent regime. Hull-form design employs the Myring (1976) parametric equations to define nose profile (power-law) and tail geometry (degree-\(n\) polynomial), minimising flow separation and associated pressure drag.

    The propulsive power required to maintain speed \(V\) against drag \(F_D\):

    \[P_{thrust} = \frac{F_D \cdot V}{\eta_{prop}}\tag{15.4}\]

    where \(\eta_{prop}\) is the overall open-water propulsive efficiency (typically 0.55–0.70 for ducted thrusters with Kort nozzle). Worked example for a REMUS 600 (body diameter 32.4 cm, \(C_D \approx 0.25\), \(A_{ref} = 0.0825\) m\(^2\)) at 3 kt (1.543 m/s):

    \[\begin{align*} F_D &= 0.5 \times 1025 \times 0.25 \times 0.0825 \times 1.543^2 \approx 24.9\ \text{N} \\ P_{thrust} &= \frac{24.9 \times 1.543}{0.62} \approx 61.9\ \text{W} \end{align*}\]

    This power budget is modest, allowing the REMUS 600’s 15.3 kWh Li-Ion battery to deliver over 70 hours endurance at cruise.

    15.2.3 Buoyancy Control and Glider Dynamics¶

    Static buoyancy is governed by Archimedes’ principle. For a submerged body of displaced volume \(\mathcal{V}\):

    \[F_B = \rho_w \, g \, \mathcal{V}\tag{15.7}\]

    A vehicle is positively buoyant when \(F_B > W\), negatively buoyant when \(F_B < W\), and neutrally buoyant at \(F_B = W\). The variable buoyancy engine (VBE) modifies \(\mathcal{V}\) by transferring oil between an internal low-pressure reservoir and an external compliant bladder (piston VBE), or by actuating a direct-drive piston exposed to seawater. The net buoyancy change:

    \[\Delta F_B = \rho_w \, g \, \Delta \mathcal{V}\tag{15.8}\]

    For a Slocum G3 glider (\(\Delta\mathcal{V}_{max} \approx 400\) cm\(^3\)): \(\Delta F_B = 1025 \times 9.81 \times 4\times10^{-4} \approx 4.02\) N. This force is sufficient to drive vertical ascent/descent at \(\sim\)0.1 m/s.

    For the glider, the descent/ascent angle \(\gamma\) relative to horizontal and the horizontal-to-vertical speed ratio are related through the hydrodynamic glide ratio:

    \[\frac{V_x}{V_z} = \frac{L}{D} = \frac{C_L}{C_D}\tag{15.9}\]

    Typical underwater gliders achieve \(L/D \approx 10\)–20, translating 1 m of vertical motion into 10–20 m of horizontal range. Energy consumed per unit range is:

    \[E_{range} = \frac{P_{hotel} + P_{VBE}}{V_x}\tag{15.10}\]

    where \(P_{hotel}\) covers electronics, sensors, and communications (2–6 W typical), and \(P_{VBE}\) is the averaged hydraulic pump power over a full dive-climb cycle. A Slocum G3 with 800 Wh primary battery can travel \(\approx\)3,000 km over 30+ days—a capability utterly unmatched by any propeller-driven AUV.

    15.2.4 Propulsion Systems¶

    Ducted Thruster (Kort Nozzle).

    The standard UUV propulsor pairs a brushless DC motor with a fixed-pitch propeller inside a Kort 19A accelerating duct. The nozzle increases thrust at low advance ratios (bollard pull condition) by 30–40% relative to an open propeller of equal diameter, as characterised by Wageningen systematic series tests. Thrust for a thruster in the actuator-disk model:

    \[T = \left(\frac{\rho \pi D_t^2}{8}\right)^{1/3} (2 P \eta_{motor})^{2/3}\tag{15.11}\]

    Undulating Fin Propulsion.

    Bio-inspired systems mimic the fin locomotion of cuttlefish (Sepia officinalis) or manta rays. A flexible fin of length \(L_{fin}\) running longitudinally along the vehicle body is driven by \(N\) servos at successive phase offsets \(\Delta\phi = 2\pi/N\), creating a sinusoidal travelling wave. The kinematic relationship:

    \[y(x,t) = A_{fin} \sin\!\left(\frac{2\pi}{\lambda_{fin}} x - 2\pi f_{fin} t\right)\tag{15.12}\]

    Thrust and efficiency peak at a reduced frequency \(k = 2\pi f_{fin} c_{fin} / V \approx 0.5\)–1.2. Undulating fins offer near-zero acoustic signature and precise 6-DOF manoeuvrability at speeds \(<\)2 kt.

    Magnetohydrodynamic (MHD) Drive.

    A Lorentz-force thruster passes electrical current density \(\mathbf{J}\) through seawater in a strong transverse magnetic field \(\mathbf{B}\), producing volumetric body force \(\mathbf{f} = \mathbf{J}\times\mathbf{B}\) (N/m\(^3\)). Net thrust:

    \[T_{MHD} = \int_{\mathcal{V}_{duct}} (\mathbf{J}\times\mathbf{B})\, d\mathcal{V} = \sigma_w (E + v_f B) B A_{duct} L_{duct}\tag{15.13}\]

    where \(\sigma_w \approx 4\) S/m is seawater conductivity. MHD drives have no moving parts and zero acoustic noise but suffer from low efficiency (\(<15\%\)) and require superconducting magnets to achieve adequate field strength (\(>\)2 T). They remain of interest for ultra-quiet intelligence-gathering vehicles.

    15.2.5 Navigation and Positioning¶

    GPS signals are absorbed within the first metre of water (\(e\)-folding depth \(\approx\)7–30 m at 1.575 GHz). UUV navigation systems:

    • Inertial Navigation System (INS): Integrates accelerometer and gyroscope data. Error grows with time. MEMS-grade: \(\sim\)1 nmi/hr drift. Tactical fibre-optic gyro (FOG) INS: \(<\)0.1 nmi/hr. Ring-laser gyro (RLG): \(<\)0.01 nmi/hr.

    • Doppler Velocity Log (DVL): Acoustic transducer pinging the seabed at four beams at \(\sim\)150 kHz, measuring Doppler shift \(f_D = 2f_0 V_z/c\) on each beam. Four-beam Janus configuration resolves 3D ground-referenced velocity to \(\pm\)0.2% of speed. Coupled to INS via extended Kalman filter, position accuracy improves to \(<\)0.1% of distance travelled in bottom-lock mode.

    • Ultra-Short Baseline (USBL): Surface vessel or buoy interrogates the UUV via acoustic modem; the reply allows calculation of range (time-of-flight) and bearing (phase-difference between hydrophone elements separated by \(\lambda/2\)). Accuracy 0.5–2% of slant range.

    • Long Baseline (LBL): Array of three or more calibrated transponders on the seabed defines an acoustic baseline of 1–10 km. UUV ranges to all transponders; position is resolved by trilateration. Accuracy \(<\)1 m in low-multipath conditions.

    • Simultaneous Localisation and Mapping (SLAM): Forward-looking sonar (900 kHz sector-scan) builds an occupancy map of the environment; vehicle state is estimated by scan-matching. Used for close-proximity operations (harbour survey, under-ice).

    The 15-state Kalman filter state vector standard for DVL-aided INS:

    \[\mathbf{x} = \bigl[\underbrace{x,y,z}_\text{position},\ \underbrace{V_x,V_y,V_z}_\text{velocity},\ \underbrace{\phi,\theta,\psi}_\text{attitude},\ \underbrace{b_{ax},b_{ay},b_{az}}_\text{acc. bias},\ \underbrace{b_{gx},b_{gy},b_{gz}}_\text{gyro bias}\bigr]^T\tag{15.14}\]

    15.2.6 Underwater Communication¶

    Acoustic Modems.

    Acoustic communication is the only practical long-range underwater channel. The JANUS standard (NATO STANAG 4748, also ITU recommendation) defines interoperable signalling at centre frequency 11.52 kHz with OFDM modulation achieving 80–9,600 bps at 1–10 km range. Transmission loss in shallow water:

    \[TL = 20 \log_{10}(R) + \alpha_{abs} \cdot R\tag{15.15}\]

    where \(R\) is range (m) and \(\alpha_{abs}\) is absorption coefficient (dB/km)—approximately 0.001 dB/km at 10 kHz. In shallow-water channels, multipath interference from surface/bottom reflections causes inter-symbol interference; OFDM and equalisation are essential.

    Blue-Green Optical Communication.

    Seawater exhibits minimal absorption in the 470–532 nm (blue-green) window, where the diffuse attenuation coefficient \(K_d \approx 0.05\)–0.3 m\(^{-1}\) in clear ocean. Achievable data rates exceed 1 Gbps, but practical range is 30–100 m due to scattering. Used for AUV-to-ship or AUV-to-ROV high-bandwidth video links during docking or close operation.

    15.2.7 Pressure Housing and Material Selection¶

    At depth \(d\), external hydrostatic pressure:

    \[P_{ext} = P_{atm} + \rho_w g d \approx 101,325 + 10,046 \, d\ [\text{Pa}]\tag{15.16}\]

    At 6,000 m (hadal zone): \(P_{ext} \approx 60.4\) MPa. The wall thickness of a cylindrical pressure housing subject to \(P_{ext}\) (thin-wall approximation, \(t \ll r\)) for a factor of safety \(FS\):

    15.2.8 Power Systems¶

    • Lithium-Ion (rechargeable): 150–280 Wh/kg cell-level; dominant in modern AUVs and ROVs. REMUS 600: 15.3 kWh; Bluefin-21: 17 kWh.

    • Lithium Thionyl Chloride (Li-SOCl\(_2\)) primary: 590 Wh/kg; non-rechargeable; \(V_{nom}\) = 3.67 V; used in long-endurance gliders (e.g., Slocum G3: 800 Wh primary pack).

    • Aluminium-Seawater semi-fuel cell: Dissolving Al anode produces \(\sim\)800 Wh/kg; seawater electrolyte; Al electrode plates replaced between missions. Tested in DARPA LDUUV programme.

    • PEM Hydrogen Fuel Cell (AIP): H\(_2\)O\(_2\) or H\(_2\)/air; 700–2,000 Wh/kg (H\(_2\) gravimetric density in metal-hydride store); used in Boeing Orca for extended mission endurance.

    15.3 Unmanned Surface Vessels (USV)¶

    15.3.1 Hull Forms and Seakeeping¶

    USVs operate at the air-sea interface and must maintain mission effectiveness in open-ocean conditions. Three hull forms dominate:

    Monohull.

    Single displacement or semi-planing hull with well-understood stability. Operability typically to Sea State 4 (\(H_{1/3}\) = 1.25–2.5 m, wind 5.5–7.9 m/s). Compact; simple construction. The wave-added resistance in irregular seas:

    \[R_{wave} = \frac{1}{2} \rho g \zeta_a^2 B^2 \bar{C}_w\tag{15.18}\]

    where \(\zeta_a\) is significant wave amplitude, \(B\) is beam, and \(\bar{C}_w\) is a mean wave resistance coefficient (0.4–0.8 depending on heading).

    Catamaran.

    Twin hulls provide high beam-to-length ratio for deck stability; wide working deck for payload. Wetted surface higher than monohull, increasing friction drag, but form drag lower. Operability to Sea State 5 (\(H_{1/3}\) = 2.5–4.0 m). Sea Hunter ACTUV is a 40 m trimaran variant achieving Sea State 5+ capability.

    SWATH (Small Waterplane Area Twin Hull).

    Submerged torpedo-form hulls support slender surface-piercing struts. The waterplane area—and therefore wave-exciting force—is minimised. The heave natural frequency:

    \[\omega_n = \sqrt{\frac{\rho g A_{WP}}{M + a_{33}}}\tag{15.19}\]

    is very low (0.1–0.3 rad/s), placing the resonance below most ocean wave energy. SWATH achieves Sea State 6 operability but at high structural weight; used in oceanographic vessels and dedicated MCM craft.

    15.3.2 Propulsion and Power¶

    • Diesel-electric: Diesel generator charges battery/capacitor bank; electric motors drive waterjets or propellers. Quiet loiter mode (battery only); sprint on generator. Used in Protector USV.

    • Waterjet: Hamilton Jet or Rolls-Royce series; no exposed shaft or propeller below keel; improved shallow-water operation; thrust direction controlled by vectored deflector nozzle. Preferred for high-speed (25–50 kt) USVs.

    • Wind-solar hybrid: Saildrone Explorer uses a 5 m rigid composite wing-sail generating 150–300 N thrust from wind; 96 solar panels (650 W peak) power instruments and communications. Unlimited endurance at 3–10 kt.

    15.3.3 Autonomous Navigation and COLREGS Compliance¶

    The International Regulations for Preventing Collisions at Sea (COLREGS, IMO 1972, as amended) govern all vessels including autonomous craft. Key rules for USV autonomy:

    • Rule 5 (Look-out): Fulfilled by AIS, X-band radar, EO/IR cameras, lidar.

    • Rule 8 (Action to avoid collision): Action must be taken in ample time; alterations large enough to be readily apparent to another vessel.

    • Rule 13–17 (Overtaking, head-on, crossing, give-way, stand-on): Assigns right of way; USV must comply with same obligations as crewed vessel of same size and class.

    The Velocity Obstacle (VO) method is widely deployed in USV collision avoidance. The velocity obstacle of vessel \(B\) as seen by vessel \(A\):

    \[VO_{A|B} = \left\{ \mathbf{v}_A : \exists\, t > 0,\ \|\mathbf{p}_A + \mathbf{v}_A t - \mathbf{p}_B - \mathbf{v}_B t\| < r_A + r_B \right\}\tag{15.20}\]

    Any velocity \(\mathbf{v}_A\) inside \(VO_{A|B}\) leads to collision; the USV planner selects a velocity in the complement set consistent with COLREGS hierarchy. AIS on VHF channels 87B/88B provides identity, position, COG, SOG for cooperative vessels; X-band radar at 9.3 GHz detects non-cooperative targets.

    15.4 High-Altitude Long-Endurance (HALE) UAS¶

    15.4.1 Operating Environment Characteristics¶

    HALE vehicles operate nominally between 15,000 and 25,000 m altitude, within the stratosphere. The environment is characterised by:

    • Air density: At 20 km, ISA gives \(\rho \approx 0.0889\) kg/m\(^3\) — approximately 7.3% of sea-level density. Generating adequate lift requires very large wing area, very high airspeed, or very high lift coefficient—all of which drive structural and aerodynamic challenges.

    • Temperature: The stratosphere is approximately isothermal at \(T \approx -56.5^\circ\)C (216.65 K) between 11 and 20 km. Above 20 km, temperature increases slowly to \(\sim -40^\circ\)C at 25 km due to ozone absorption. All structures, lubricants, electronics, and batteries must function from \(-70^\circ\)C to ground-ambient.

    • Reynolds number: At a given speed and chord, Re scales with \(\rho/\mu\). At 20 km, \(\mu \approx 1.42\times10^{-5}\) Pa\(\cdot\)s (nearly the same as SL), but \(\rho\) is \(\sim\)13\(\times\) lower, so Re is \(\sim\)13\(\times\) lower. HALE sections operate at \(Re\) = 300,000–800,000—the troublesome transitional regime requiring careful low-Re airfoil selection (Drela AG series, Eppler E387, Selig S1223 family).

    The minimum level-flight airspeed at altitude \(h\):

    \[V_{min}(h) = \sqrt{\frac{2 m g}{\rho(h) \, C_{L,max} \, S}}\tag{15.21}\]

    For RQ-4B Global Hawk at 19 km (\(\rho = 0.132\) kg/m\(^3\)), \(m = 14,628\) kg, \(S = 130.9\) m\(^2\), \(C_{L,max} = 1.5\):

    \[V_{min} = \sqrt{\frac{2 \times 14628 \times 9.81}{0.132 \times 1.5 \times 130.9}} = \sqrt{\frac{286,954}{25.97}} \approx 105\ \text{m/s} \quad (204\ \text{kt})\]

    15.4.2 Structural Design for High-Altitude Flight¶

    HALE wings have high aspect ratios (AR = \(b^2/S\) = 25–35) to minimise induced drag:

    \[C_{D,i} = \frac{C_L^2}{\pi \, e \, AR}\tag{15.23}\]

    where \(e\) is the Oswald span efficiency (0.85–0.92). At AR = 30 and \(C_L\) = 1.2: \(C_{D,i} = 1.44 / (30\pi \times 0.88) \approx 0.0174\).

    High AR wings are susceptible to geometric (torsional) and aerodynamic (flutter) aeroelastic instabilities. The Scanlan flutter speed scales as:

    \[V_F \propto \sqrt{\frac{GJ \cdot EI_{bend}}{m \, b^4 \, q_\infty}}\tag{15.24}\]

    where \(GJ\) is torsional stiffness, \(EI_{bend}\) is bending stiffness, \(m\) is linear mass density, and \(b\) is semi-span. CFRP structures—specific stiffness \(E/\rho \approx 70\) GPa·m\(^3\)/Mg—enable meeting stiffness targets at minimum weight. Active load alleviation (spoilers, differential ailerons) reduces gust bending moments by 20–40%, allowing a lighter primary structure.

    15.4.3 Propulsion and Energy Balance¶

    Solar-Electric HALE.

    Available solar power:

    \[P_{solar}(t) = \eta_{cell} \cdot \eta_{MPPT} \cdot G_{irr}(t, \text{lat, season}) \cdot A_{cell}\tag{15.25}\]

    Above the atmosphere at 20 km, \(G_{irr} \approx 1,361\) W/m\(^2\) (solar constant); ground-level reference is 1,000 W/m\(^2\). Triple-junction GaInP/GaAs/Ge cells achieve \(\eta_{cell} = 29\)–30% under concentrated illumination. MPPT efficiency \(\eta_{MPPT} \approx 0.97\).

    The energy balance condition for eternal stratospheric flight—energy stored in daytime (\(t_d\)) must cover night-time (\(t_n\)) load at consumption power \(P_c\):

    \[\int_{t_{rise}}^{t_{set}} \bigl(P_{solar}(t) - P_c\bigr) \, dt \geq P_c \cdot t_n\tag{15.26}\]

    This drives the requirement to minimise \(P_c\) (hence the ultra-low airframe mass of Zephyr S at 75 kg) and to maximise cell area. Airbus Zephyr S achieved 26 consecutive days in flight over Arizona (August 2022), at a peak altitude of 21.4 km, validating perpetual-flight operation.

    Turbofan HALE (RQ-4 Global Hawk).

    The Rolls-Royce AE3007H turbofan produces 31.4 kN at sea-level. Thrust lapse with altitude:

    \[T_{alt} = T_{SL} \cdot \left(\frac{\rho_{alt}}{\rho_{SL}}\right)^{0.85}\tag{15.27}\]

    At 19 km, \(\rho_{alt}/\rho_{SL} = 0.132/1.225 = 0.108\): \(T_{alt} = 31,400 \times 0.108^{0.85} \approx 3,870\) N. Cruise at altitude is near the thrust-drag crossover; the vehicle operates at high \(L/D\) (\(\sim\)20) and low speed to remain within available thrust.

    15.4.4 HALE as Persistent ISR and Communications Relay¶

    At 20 km altitude, the geometric horizon distance:

    \[d_{hor} = \sqrt{2 R_E h + h^2} \approx \sqrt{2 \times 6.371\times10^6 \times 20,000} \approx 504\ \text{km}\tag{15.28}\]

    This gives a circular radio-line-of-sight footprint of \(\sim\)1,008 km diameter, supporting ADS-B relay for all aircraft in the coverage volume, cellular 4G/5G backhauling, and ISR with SAR/EO/SIGINT sensors. The free-space path loss at 20 km slant range and frequency \(f\):

    \[FSPL = \left(\frac{4\pi R f}{c}\right)^2 = 20\log_{10}\!\left(\frac{4\pi \times 20000 \times f}{3\times10^8}\right)\ [\text{dB}]\tag{15.29}\]

    At \(f\) = 2 GHz, FSPL \(\approx\) 124.5 dB—well within the link budget for modern phased-array terminals.

    15.5 Urban Air Mobility (UAM) and eVTOL Aircraft¶

    15.5.1 eVTOL Configurations¶

    Electric Vertical Take-Off and Landing (eVTOL) aircraft are defined by the mechanism generating lift and thrust. Three primary configurations dominate the current certification landscape:

    Multirotor.

    All rotors generate both vertical and horizontal thrust by differential speed. Simple, inherently fail-safe, but hover power scales unfavourably with disc loading (DL = \(W/A_{disk}\)). Hover power:

    \[P_{hover} = \frac{W^{3/2}}{\eta_{FM} \sqrt{2 \rho A_{disk}}}\tag{15.30}\]

    where \(\eta_{FM}\) is the rotor Figure of Merit (0.70–0.80). For an 800 kg multirotor with \(A_{disk,total} = 12\) m\(^2\): \(P_{hover} = (800\times9.81)^{3/2} / (0.75\times\sqrt{2\times1.225\times12}) \approx 31.7\) kW.

    Lift+Cruise.

    Dedicated fixed-pitch lift rotors for VTOL; fixed wing plus pusher/tractor propeller for cruise. Archer Midnight uses 12 lift rotors + 1 pusher. Cruise efficiency approaches conventional fixed-wing:

    \[P_{cruise} = \frac{D \cdot V}{\eta_{prop}} = \frac{W}{L/D} \cdot \frac{V}{\eta_{prop}}\tag{15.31}\]

    At \(L/D = 12\), \(W = 24,000\) N, \(V = 65\) m/s, \(\eta_{prop} = 0.82\): \(P_{cruise} = 24000/12 \times 65/0.82 \approx 158\) kW.

    Tiltrotor.

    Rotors transition from vertical (hover) to horizontal (cruise) orientation via nacelle tilting. Joby S4 uses five tilting prop-rotors on canard + main wing configuration. Unique ability to hover efficiently and cruise at high speed simultaneously. Transition dynamics governed by the nacelle tilt schedule \(\alpha_n(V)\) and individual rotor speed/pitch commands.

    15.5.2 Noise Requirements and Acoustics¶

    Urban acceptance of air taxis requires far quieter operation than helicopters. Targets:

    • FAA/Joby target: \(<65\) dB(A) at 500 ft (152 m) directly below

    • Helicopter (e.g., Bell 206) at same distance: \(\approx\)87–92 dB(A)

    • Typical urban background noise at pavement level: 65–75 dB(A)

    Rotor noise sources: thickness noise (monopole, due to blade volume displacing air), loading noise (dipole, due to lift fluctuations), and impulsive noise (blade-vortex interaction, BVI). A-weighted level from thickness noise scales approximately as:

    \[L_{A,thick} \propto 20\log_{10}(V_{tip}) + 10\log_{10}(N_b) - 20\log_{10}(R_{obs})\tag{15.32}\]

    Noise reduces by \(\sim\)50 dB per halving of tip speed. eVTOL designs target \(V_{tip} < 150\) m/s (vs. helicopter 215 m/s); distributed rotors at lower tip speed spread the acoustic energy and reduce tonal components.

    15.5.3 Certification Pathways¶

    • FAA Powered-Lift Special Class (14 CFR §21.17(b)): Joby Aviation received the first-ever powered-lift Type Certificate in May 2024. Basis set agreed in AC 21.17-1, incorporating applicable airworthiness standards from Parts 27 (rotorcraft), 23 (small airplane), and novel failure-mode provisions.

    • EASA Special Condition VTOL (SC-VTOL): Three categories — Category Enhanced (Basic), Category Basic (Reduced), and a full airworthiness-code equivalent. Volocopter VC2X received experimental permit; Lilium and Vertical Aerospace pursuing Category Enhanced.

    • Battery safety: DO-311A (minimum operational performance for rechargeable Li-Ion batteries in aviation) is the accepted means of compliance for battery systems.

    15.5.4 Battery Technology Gap¶

    The range and payload competitiveness of eVTOL is constrained by battery energy density. Mission energy requirement for a 5-seat air taxi, 60-mile route:

    \[E_{mission} = \frac{P_{VTOL} \cdot t_{hover} + P_{cruise} \cdot t_{cruise}}{\eta_{bat} \cdot DoD_{max}} + E_{reserve}\tag{15.33}\]

    At current Li-Ion cell energy density of 280 Wh/kg, pack-level \(\approx\) 200 Wh/kg (including BMS, thermal management, structure), a 100 kWh pack masses 500 kg. For a 5-seat aircraft with MTOW \(\approx\) 2,000 kg, this is a 25% battery mass fraction—marginal. Competitive 60-mile operations require \(\geq\)350 Wh/kg at pack level, achievable with silicon-anode lithium-ion (\(\sim\)2028) or solid-state lithium (\(\sim\)2030).

    15.6 Stratospheric Pseudo-Satellites (HAPS)¶

    15.6.1 Definition and Station-Keeping Requirements¶

    A High-Altitude Platform Station (HAPS), per ITU Radio Regulations No. 1.66A, operates at an altitude of 20–50 km at a specified, nominal fixed point relative to the Earth. Unlike LEO satellites, HAPS platforms maintain quasi-geostationary coverage from a fraction of geostationary altitude (\(\approx\)35,786 km), providing dramatically lower path loss and latency.

    Station-keeping requires the platform to overcome stratospheric winds—the quasi-biennial oscillation (QBO) and polar night jets can exceed 50 m/s at 20 km. Required station-keeping propulsive power:

    \[P_{SK} = \frac{F_{D,wind} \cdot V_{wind}}{\eta_{prop} \cdot \eta_{motor}} = \frac{\tfrac{1}{2}\rho C_D A V_{wind}^2 \cdot V_{wind}}{\eta_{prop} \eta_{motor}}\tag{15.34}\]

    For Zephyr S in a 30 m/s wind at 20 km (\(C_D = 0.028\), \(A_{ref} = 3.5\) m\(^2\), \(\rho = 0.0889\) kg/m\(^3\)):

    \[\begin{align*} F_{D} &= 0.5 \times 0.0889 \times 0.028 \times 3.5 \times 30^2 = 3.96\ \text{N} \\ P_{SK} &= \frac{3.96 \times 30}{0.85 \times 0.95} \approx 147\ \text{W} \end{align*}\]

    Total power budget (propulsion + avionics + payload) must be met by the solar cells during daylight plus stored battery energy at night.

    15.6.2 Applications¶

    • 5G/IMT broadband relay: ITU WRC-19 allocated 2 GHz bands (2.1 GHz) for HAPS; single platform covers \(10^6\) km\(^2\); latency \(\sim\)0.13 ms one-way (\(2\times20\) km/\(c\)); effective for rural and maritime connectivity.

    • Earth Observation: Sub-metre resolution sensors at 20 km; persistent area monitoring impossible for satellites.

    • Border and maritime surveillance: Wide-area persistent ISR; complements satellite revisit.

    • Disaster communications: Rapidly deployable after infrastructure loss; Loon demonstrated post-disaster 4G service in Puerto Rico (2017 hurricane).

    15.6.3 Representative HAPS Systems¶

    • Airbus Zephyr S: Solar HALE/HAPS; 75 kg; 25 m span; triple-junction GaAs cells; Li-S (lithium-sulfur) battery for night; altitude record 21.4 km (2022); target weeks-to-months mission.

    • SoftBank HAPSMobile HAWK30: Fixed-wing; 78 m span; 10 tractor propellers; GaAs solar cells; 5G NR femtocell payload; station-keeping demonstrated at WSMR.

    • Google Loon (2011–2021): Polyethylene super-pressure balloons at 18–25 km altitude; navigated by pumping helium between inner and outer chambers to change buoyancy level, exploiting differential wind vectors at different altitudes. Provided LTE relay; retired January 2021.

    • Airbus Zephyr T: Next-generation successor to Zephyr S; larger platform; increased payload capacity (\(>\)15 kg); enhanced 5G relay and ISR package; development ongoing as of 2025.

    15.7 Combat Unmanned Air Systems (UCAV)¶

    15.7.1 Stealth Integration¶

    UCAV airframes are designed from the outset for low observability (LO) across radar, infrared, and visual/acoustic spectra. Key radar stealth measures:

    Planform Shaping.

    All leading edges, trailing edges, nozzle lips, inlet lips, and doors are aligned to a minimum number of angular directions (typically 4). Outside these specular sectors, the RCS drops by 20–40 dB. RCS of a flat plate of area \(A\) in the specular direction:

    \[\sigma_{plate} = \frac{4\pi A^2}{\lambda^2}\tag{15.37}\]

    At \(X\)-band (\(\lambda = 3\) cm), a 0.1 m\(^2\) panel: \(\sigma = 4\pi(0.1)^2/(0.03)^2 = 1,396\) m\(^2\) = 31.4 dBsm — enormous. Canting the panel 30\(^\circ\) off-normal reduces \(\sigma\) by \(\cos^4(30^\circ)\) \(\approx\) 56% even without RAM.

    Radar-Absorbing Material (RAM).

    Graded-impedance or Salisbury screen absorbers reduce the surface reflection coefficient:

    \[\Gamma = \left|\frac{Z_{in} - Z_0}{Z_{in} + Z_0}\right|\tag{15.38}\]

    Target: \(\Gamma < -20\) dB (1% power reflected) over \(X\)-band. Achieved with \(\lambda/4\)-thick ferrite-loaded epoxy layers (3–5 mm at 10 GHz).

    Infrared Suppression.

    The exhaust nozzle temperature drives IRST and IR missile detectability. Diluting the exhaust with bypass air lowers exit temperature from \(\sim\)700\(^\circ\)C (bare engine) to \(<\)350\(^\circ\)C. Plume spectral radiance at temperature \(T\):

    \[L_\lambda = \frac{2hc^2}{\lambda^5}\frac{1}{\exp(hc/\lambda k T)-1} \cdot \varepsilon(\lambda)\tag{15.39}\]

    Reducing \(T\) from 700 K to 400 K decreases \(L_{8\mu m}\) by \(\approx\)95%.

    15.7.2 Loyal Wingman Concept¶

    The loyal wingman (LW) executes semi-autonomous tactical tasks under direction of a manned aircraft acting as mission commander. The crewed–uncrewed teaming (CUT) architecture distributes risk-taking to the unmanned element while keeping human authority at the mission level:

    • The LW accepts target assignments, airspace routing, and engagement authority commands via encrypted tactical datalink (Link 16 extension or proprietary).

    • On-board autonomy (ML-based threat assessment, sensor fusion) enables independent execution of the assigned task.

    • SAE AS-6081 Autonomy Levels 2–3 are targeted.

    Key programmes: Boeing MQ-28A Ghost Bat (Australia; RAAF service entry expected 2026); Kratos UTAP-22 Mako (US; affordable expendable concept); USAF Collaborative Combat Aircraft (CCA) — two teams under contract (Anduril and General Atomics); UK Mosquito (BAE Systems); Dassault nEUROn (technology demonstrator for European 6th-gen integration).

    15.7.3 Carrier-Based UCAV Operations¶

    Carrier aviation imposes stringent constraints on airframe design:

    • Catapult launch (EMALS): \(\sim\)4 g longitudinal acceleration over 90 m stroke. Structural attachment lug designed to \(\times\)1.5 ultimate factor.

    • Arrested landing: Tailhook engages 3-wire; \(\sim\)3 g deceleration. Airframe fatigue life assessed per MIL-A-8866 carrier-based spectrum.

    • Wing fold: Span reduced from \(\sim\)14 m to \(\sim\)8 m for hangar/elevator compatibility. Electrically actuated fold latched with mechanical lock pin.

    • Corrosion protection: MIL-PRF-85285 topcoat + MIL-PRF-23377 epoxy primer; Ti-6Al-4V fasteners to prevent galvanic corrosion with CFRP skin.

    The MQ-25A Stingray (Boeing) achieved first-ever unmanned carrier-based tanking in 2021, transferring fuel to an F/A-18. Northrop Grumman X-47B demonstrated autonomous carrier traps and catapult launches in 2013, including a fully autonomous bolter/go-around. UCLASS (now CBARS) remains the long-term combat UCAV programme for the US Navy.

    15.8.3 Low-Reynolds-Number Aerodynamics¶

    At nano UAV chord scales of 10–50 mm and speeds of 2–8 m/s:

    \[Re = \frac{\rho V c}{\mu} \approx \frac{1.225 \times 5 \times 0.025}{1.81\times10^{-5}} \approx 8,500\tag{15.40}\]

    At \(Re < 10^4\), viscous effects dominate over inertial; conventional cambered sections fail due to large laminar separation bubbles that dramatically increase effective camber and reduce \(L/D\). Bio-inspired flapping-wing approaches offer significant advantages through unsteady aerodynamic mechanisms:

    Clap-and-Fling Mechanism.

    At the end of each half-stroke, the wings clap together (extinguishing bound circulation) and subsequently fling apart, generating a strong leading-edge vortex (LEV) on each wing. Time-averaged \(C_L > 2.5\) is achievable at \(Re \sim 10^4\), far exceeding quasi-steady attached-flow limits.

    The Strouhal number governs propulsive efficiency of flapping flight:

    \[St = \frac{f \cdot A_{stroke}}{U_\infty}\tag{15.41}\]

    where \(f\) is flapping frequency, \(A_{stroke}\) is peak-to-peak wingtip stroke amplitude, and \(U_\infty\) is forward speed. Biological flyers and efficient engineered FMAVs operate at \(St = 0.2\)–0.4.

    Quasi-steady flapping-wing lift model (Ellington 1984 extended):

    \[F_{lift}(t) = \frac{1}{2}\rho C_L(\alpha(t)) \hat{c} \int_0^b (r \dot{\phi}(t))^2 \, dr = \frac{1}{2}\rho C_L \hat{c} \,r_2^2(S) \dot{\phi}^2(t)\tag{15.42}\]

    where \(\hat{c}\) is mean chord, \(r_2(S)\) is the non-dimensional second moment of area of the wing, and \(\dot\phi\) is instantaneous angular velocity about the stroke-plane normal.

    15.8.4 Power and Energy Budget at Nano Scale¶

    Hover power scales with weight and disc area:

    \[P_{hover} = \frac{W^{3/2}}{\sqrt{2\rho A_{disk}}} \cdot \frac{1}{\eta_{FM}}\tag{15.43}\]

    For Black Hornet 3 vehicle (16 g, \(A_{disk} \approx 2\times\pi(0.06)^2 = 0.0226\) m\(^2\) coaxial), \(\eta_{FM} = 0.60\):

    \[P_{hover} = \frac{(0.016 \times 9.81)^{3/2}}{\sqrt{2 \times 1.225 \times 0.0226} \times 0.60} = \frac{0.00618}{0.163} \approx 0.38\ \text{W}\]

    Practical system power (motors + avionics + comms): \(\approx\) 0.8 W. Battery energy: \(\approx\) 0.8 Wh; therefore endurance \(\approx\) 0.8/0.8 = 60 min theoretical; \(\sim\)25 min actual accounting for inefficiencies and forward flight.

    15.10 Agricultural Drones¶

    15.10.1 Aerial Application System Design¶

    Agricultural spray drones are the largest commercial drone segment in Asia by revenue. The critical agronomic and engineering parameters:

    Droplet Size.

    Characterised by Volume Median Diameter (VMD, \(D_{V0.5}\)) — the droplet diameter at which 50% of total spray volume is in smaller droplets. ASABE S572.3 categories:

    • Very Fine (VF): \(D_{V0.5} < 136\ \mu\)m — excellent coverage; unacceptable drift in field conditions

    • Fine (F): 136–177 \(\mu\)m — good coverage; high drift risk

    • Medium (M): 177–218 \(\mu\)m — balanced performance; most applications

    • Coarse (C): 218–349 \(\mu\)m — low drift; reduced surface coverage

    Hollow-cone pressure nozzles at 2–4 bar pressure produce Medium to Coarse at 0.5–2 L/min per nozzle. Rotary atomisers (spinning disk) allow precise \(D_{V0.5}\) control by adjusting disk RPM:

    \[D_{V0.5} = \frac{K_{atomiser} \cdot \sigma^{1/3}}{(\rho_L \cdot n_{disk}^2 \cdot r_{disk})^{1/3}}\tag{15.48}\]

    Effective Field Capacity.

    The area covered per unit time:

    \[FC_e = v_{ground} \cdot W_{boom} \cdot E_f \cdot 3600 \times 10^{-4} \quad [\text{ha/hr}]\tag{15.49}\]

    where \(v_{ground}\) is ground speed (m/s), \(W_{boom}\) is effective boom width (m), and \(E_f\) is field efficiency (0.65–0.80). For DJI Agras T50 at 8 m/s, 9 m boom, \(E_f = 0.75\):

    \[FC_e = 8 \times 9 \times 0.75 \times 3600 \times 10^{-4} = 19.4\ \text{ha/hr}\]

    Application rate (L/ha) at system flow rate \(\dot{Q}\) (L/min):

    \[AR = \frac{60 \, \dot{Q}}{v_{ground} \times W_{boom} \times 10^{-4}} \quad [\text{L/ha}]\tag{15.51}\]

    15.10.2 Remote Sensing and Precision Agriculture¶

    The Normalised Difference Vegetation Index (NDVI) is computed from reflectances \(\rho_{NIR}\) (750–900 nm) and \(\rho_{Red}\) (630–680 nm):

    \[NDVI = \frac{\rho_{NIR} - \rho_{Red}}{\rho_{NIR} + \rho_{Red}} \in [-1,+1]\tag{15.52}\]

    Healthy dense canopy: NDVI \(\geq\) 0.6. Stressed or sparse: 0.2–0.5. Bare soil: 0.1–0.2. Water: \(<\) 0.

    The Normalised Difference Red Edge index (NDRE) is more sensitive to chlorophyll content at high biomass:

    \[NDRE = \frac{\rho_{NIR} - \rho_{RedEdge}}{\rho_{NIR} + \rho_{RedEdge}}\tag{15.53}\]

    where Red Edge is 700–730 nm. Ground sampling distance at altitude \(H\) with sensor pixel size \(p\) and focal length \(f\):

    \[GSD = \frac{H \cdot p}{f}\tag{15.54}\]

    At \(H\) = 30 m, \(p\) = 3.75 \(\mu\)m, \(f\) = 5.5 mm: GSD = 30 \(\times\) 3.75\(\times\)10\(^{-6}\) / 5.5\(\times\)10\(^{-3}\) = 0.020 m (2 cm/pixel).

    15.11 Electronic Warfare (EW) Drones¶

    15.11.1 Electronic Warfare Taxonomy¶

    Electronic Warfare is subdivided per DOD JP 3-13.1:

    • Electronic Attack (EA): Offensive employment of EM energy — active jamming, decoy emission, directed-energy weapons, anti-radiation missiles.

    • Electronic Protection (EP): Defensive measures — frequency hopping, spread spectrum, notch filters, shielding, ECCM.

    • Electronic Support (ES): Passive collection — ESM, ELINT, COMINT, signal intelligence for targeting and situational awareness.

    Drones extend each of these functions by placing EW payloads forward in contested airspace, improving geometry, and accepting attrition risk.

    15.11.2 Standoff and Escort Jamming¶

    An EW drone jams an enemy receiver by transmitting high-power noise or deceptive signals on its operating frequency. The Jamming-to-Signal ratio (J/S) at the victim receiver:

    \[\frac{J}{S} = \frac{P_J G_J}{P_S G_S} \cdot \left(\frac{R_S}{R_J}\right)^2\tag{15.55}\]

    where \(P_J, G_J\) are jammer power and antenna gain; \(P_S, G_S\) are signal-source (radar) power and gain; \(R_J, R_S\) are jammer-to-victim and source-to-victim ranges. For the escort jammer co-located with the strike package, \(R_J = R_S\) and \(J/S = P_J G_J / P_S G_S\).

    Effective Radiated Power of the jamming system:

    \[ERP_J = P_J \cdot G_J \quad [\text{W or dBW}]\tag{15.56}\]

    Burn-through range.

    The range at which the radar signal regains adequate SNR despite jamming:

    \[R_{BT} = \left(\frac{P_T G_T^2 \lambda^2 \sigma_{target}}{(4\pi)^3 (J/S)_{min} P_J G_J}\right)^{1/4}\tag{15.57}\]

    Reducing \(R_J\) (closer escort) is more effective than increasing \(P_J\) because of the \(R^{-4}\) radar detection dependence vs. \(R^{-2}\) jammer loss.

    15.11.3 SEAD and Anti-Radiation Missions¶

    Suppression of Enemy Air Defences (SEAD) removes the adversary’s ability to effectively employ Surface-to-Air Missiles. SEAD drones may carry anti-radiation missiles (ARM) or execute kinetic strikes. The ARM seeker tracks the radar emission and homes on the transmitter. Angular tracking accuracy:

    \[\sigma_\theta = \frac{\theta_{3dB}}{SNR^{1/2} \sqrt{2}}\tag{15.58}\]

    Even without a kill, forcing the radar to stop transmitting achieves the SEAD objective. A typical SEAD campaign generates a “keep-off-air” window correlated to ARM time-of-flight (30–120 s), creating windows for strike packages to transit defended airspace.

    15.11.4 Decoy Drones — ADM-160 MALD¶

    The Miniature Air-Launched Decoy (MALD) ADM-160B is a powered decoy designed to replicate the radar cross-section, flight profile, and electronic emissions of strike aircraft:

    • Mass: 136 kg; length 2.82 m; wingspan 1.74 m (folding)

    • Propulsion: Williams International F121-WR-100 turbojet; 58 N thrust

    • Range: \(>\)900 km; speed: \(\leq\)0.9 Ma; altitude 15–15,000 m

    • RCS augmentation: active DRFM transponder transmits pre-recorded waveforms to mimic specific aircraft EM signatures

    • MALD-J variant: 72 kg jamming payload added for active EA role

    The DRFM (Digital Radio Frequency Memory) technology captures an incoming radar pulse, digitises it at high fidelity (14-bit, 4+ GHz bandwidth), modifies it to create desired false target, and retransmits. Deception J/S at the victim radar:

    \[J/S_{DRFM} = \frac{P_{retransmit} G_{DRFM}}{P_{radar} G_{radar} \sigma_{target} / (4\pi R_{MALD}^2)}\tag{15.59}\]

    15.11.5 SIGINT Collection Drone Architecture¶

    A tactical SIGINT drone carries a broadband Electronic Support Measures (ESM) suite. Typical receiver chain:

    1. Wideband ESM antenna: Spiral or log-periodic array covering 0.5–18 GHz; mounted on wing trailing edge or fuselage blister for 360\(^\circ\) coverage.

    2. Instantaneous Frequency Measurement (IFM) receiver: Measures pulse carrier frequency to \(<\)1 MHz accuracy; \(<\)100 ns response time.

    3. Digital receiver bank: Software-defined radio with 1–4 GHz instantaneous bandwidth; 14-bit ADC at 4 GSPS; FFT-based spectrum analysis.

    4. Direction-Finding (DF): Interferometric phase comparison between antennas separated by baseline \(d\):

    \[\theta_{AoA} = \arcsin\!\left(\frac{\lambda \Delta\phi}{2\pi d}\right)\tag{15.60}\]

    where \(\Delta\phi\) is the measured phase difference. Accuracy \(\sigma_\theta \approx 1\)–\(2^\circ\) RMS with baseline \(d = \lambda/2\) to \(3\lambda\). Geolocation via Time Difference of Arrival (TDOA) between two drones at positions \(\mathbf{r}_1\) and \(\mathbf{r}_2\):

    \[\Delta t_{12} = \frac{\|\mathbf{r}_{emitter} - \mathbf{r}_1\| - \|\mathbf{r}_{emitter} - \mathbf{r}_2\|}{c}\tag{15.61}\]

    15.11.6 GPS Spoofing and Cyber EW¶

    A GPS spoofing drone transmits counterfeit GPS L1 C/A signals to deceive adversary UAS or precision-guided munitions into navigating to a false position. Signal level at ground from GPS SV: \(-130\) dBm. Required spoofer transmit power at 50 m range:

    \[P_{spoof} = P_{GPS,SL} + FSPL(50\ \text{m}, 1575.42\ \text{MHz})\tag{15.62}\]
    \[FSPL = 20\log_{10}\!\left(\frac{4\pi \times 50 \times 1.575\times10^9}{3\times10^8}\right) \approx 70.4\ \text{dB}\]

    Required \(P_{spoof} = -130 + 70.4 \approx -60\) dBm = 1 nW: trivially achievable. Commercial-off-the-shelf SDR (\(<\)USD 200) can provide this. Anti-spoofing countermeasures include multi-constellation GNSS, receiver autonomous integrity monitoring (RAIM), and IMU/DVL aiding (position jump detection).

    15.12 Cross-Category Design Considerations and Technology Trends¶

    15.12.1 Energy as the Universal Constraint¶

    Across all special drone categories, energy density is the dominant design driver. The Breguet endurance equation adapted for electric-propulsion systems:

    \[t_{endurance} = \frac{\eta_{prop} \cdot \eta_{motor}}{g} \cdot \frac{L}{D} \cdot \frac{e_{bat} \cdot m_{bat}}{W_{total}}\tag{15.64}\]

    where \(e_{bat}\) is battery specific energy (Wh/kg) and \(m_{bat}/W_{total}\) is the battery mass fraction. Every doubling of battery energy density directly doubles endurance—hence the transformative impact expected from solid-state and lithium-metal batteries on the 2028–2035 timeframe.

    15.12.2 Common Autonomy Architecture¶

    Despite environmental diversity, the autonomy stack is functionally identical across categories: sense \(\rightarrow\) perceive \(\rightarrow\) plan \(\rightarrow\) act. The Model Predictive Control (MPC) formulation is applied universally, from UUV path planning to eVTOL urban obstacle avoidance to UCAV target engagement:

    \[\min_{\mathbf{u}_{0:N-1}} \sum_{k=0}^{N-1} \!\left[\mathbf{x}_k^T \mathbf{Q} \mathbf{x}_k + \mathbf{u}_k^T \mathbf{R} \mathbf{u}_k\right] + \mathbf{x}_N^T \mathbf{P}_f \mathbf{x}_N\tag{15.65}\]

    subject to dynamics \(\mathbf{x}_{k+1} = f(\mathbf{x}_k, \mathbf{u}_k)\), control limits \(\mathbf{u}_{min} \leq \mathbf{u}_k \leq \mathbf{u}_{max}\), and state constraints \(\mathbf{g}(\mathbf{x}_k) \leq \mathbf{0}\).

    Chapter Summary¶

    This chapter has presented technically rigorous treatments of thirteen special unmanned-system categories. Several themes emerge:

    1. Physics determines architecture. The 837\(\times\) water-to-air density ratio forces UUVs to low speeds and acoustic communications. The \(\sim\)13\(\times\) Reynolds number drop at 20 km altitude forces HALE wings to high aspect ratio and low-Re airfoils. Urban noise requirements force eVTOL to low-tip-speed distributed rotors.

    2. Energy density is the universal limiter. From 16 g nano UAVs to 15 t XLUUVs, the ratio of energy store to vehicle weight dictates endurance. Advances in lithium-metal, silicon-anode, and solid-state batteries will unlock step-changes across every category on the 2028–2035 horizon.

    3. Autonomy architectures converge. INS/Kalman/SLAM/MPC/reinforcement learning chains are deployed across UUVs, USVs, HALE, UCAV, and logistics drones with only the sensor inputs and actuator outputs differing.

    4. Regulatory maturity lags technology. COLREGS compliance for USVs, FAA powered-lift certification for eVTOL, and spectrum allocation for HAPS are all active regulatory frontiers that will shape the commercial trajectory of these platforms as much as engineering innovation.

    5. EW is integral, not an add-on. Every military special-category drone must address EW both offensively (UCAV, dedicated EW drone) and defensively (HALE SIGINT awareness, UCAV self-protection, UUV acoustic counter-detection). The boundary between ISR, communications, and EW drone categories is increasingly dissolved.
