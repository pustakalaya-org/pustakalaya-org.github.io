    Chapter 24: Missile Airframe and Structures¶

    24.1 Structural Design Requirements¶

    The missile airframe is a precision structure whose primary function is to provide a rigid, lightweight shell that carries all internal subsystems, transmits aerodynamic and propulsive loads, and survives the thermal and mechanical environment of its complete flight profile. Unlike aircraft structures designed for many thousands of flight hours, the missile airframe is optimised for a single use — though its storage life must span 10–25 years.

    24.1.1 Load Cases¶

    Structural analysis identifies the critical load cases that size each airframe section:

    • Launch axial load: Motor thrust minus aerodynamic drag minus inertial resistance of the missile mass. At ignition the net axial force on the airframe is:

      \[F_{\text{ax}} = T_{\text{motor}} - D_{\text{aero}} - m \ddot{x}\tag{24.1}\]

      Peak axial load typically occurs at peak thrust (end of the boost phase for a regressive-burning motor) when drag has not yet reached its maximum because the missile is still accelerating.

    • Manoeuvre lateral loads: During a pulling manoeuvre at load factor \(n\), the net lateral force is:

      \[F_{\text{lat}} = n \cdot W = n \cdot m g\tag{24.2}\]

      Applied at the centre of pressure (CP), reacted at the centre of mass (CM), this produces a bending moment along the body that is maximum at the CM station. Design load factors for tactical missiles range from \(n = 20\,g\) (SAMs and AAMs) to \(n = 40\,g\) for highly agile short-range interceptors.

    • Aerodynamic pressure: Dynamic pressure \(q = \tfrac{1}{2}\rho V^2\) acts on all external surfaces. For a missile at Mach 3 at sea level:

      \[q = \frac{1}{2} \times 1.225 \times (3 \times 340)^2 \approx 635{,}000\,\text{Pa}\tag{24.3}\]

      This drives fin bending loads and radome pressure differential.

    • Thermal loads: Aerodynamic heating produces temperature gradients that introduce thermal stresses, particularly at material interfaces with different coefficients of thermal expansion.

    24.1.2 Safety Factors and Design Margins¶

    Structural design safety factors per MIL-HDBK-310 and AIAA S-110:

    • Limit load: Maximum load expected in service.

    • Design limit load factor: 1.0 (structure must not yield at limit load).

    • Ultimate load factor: 1.5 × limit load (structure must not fracture).

    • Fatigue/damage tolerance: 1.4 additional factor on repeated-load components.

    The margin of safety (MS) is defined as:

    \[MS = \frac{F_{\text{allow}}}{F_{\text{applied}} \times FS} - 1 \geq 0\tag{24.4}\]

    where \(F_{\text{allow}}\) is material allowable (yield or ultimate), \(F_{\text{applied}}\) is the applied load, and \(FS\) is the factor of safety (1.0 for yield check, 1.5 for ultimate). A positive MS indicates adequate structural margin; design targets MS \(= +0.10\) to \(+0.20\) to allow for manufacturing variability.

    24.1.3 Mass Fraction and Structural Efficiency¶

    The structural mass fraction defines the fraction of total launch mass devoted to the airframe (excluding motor case, which is counted separately in propulsion):

    \[f_{\text{struct}} = \frac{m_{\text{airframe}}}{m_{\text{launch}}} \approx 0.05 \text{ to } 0.15\tag{24.5}\]

    Advanced CFRP airframes achieve \(f_{\text{struct}} \approx 0.05\)–\(0.08\), while all-metal designs are typically \(0.10\)–\(0.15\). The structural efficiency of a thin-walled cylinder under axial compression is characterised by the knockdown factor \(\gamma_{\text{KD}}\), which accounts for geometric imperfections reducing buckling load below the classical Euler–Donnell prediction:

    \[F_{\text{crit}} = \gamma_{\text{KD}} \cdot \frac{2\pi E t^2}{\sqrt{3(1-\nu^2)}} \cdot \frac{t}{R}\tag{24.6}\]

    where \(E\) is Young’s modulus, \(t\) is skin thickness, \(R\) is body radius, \(\nu\) is Poisson’s ratio, and \(\gamma_{\text{KD}} \approx 0.3\)–\(0.7\) depending on \(R/t\) and manufacturing quality.

    24.2 Airframe Sections and Interfaces¶

    A tactical missile body is divided into longitudinal sections assembled at mechanical interfaces. Each section encapsulates one or more subsystems:

    24.2.1 Nose Section and Radome¶

    The nose section houses the seeker or sensor head. The outer shell (radome) must be transparent to the seeker’s operating wavelength (RF, laser, optical, or infrared) while sustaining high aerodynamic loads. The nose section typically carries:

    • The radome (RF or IR transparent material).

    • Seeker gimbal assembly.

    • Nose boom (if pitot-static port is required).

    • Forward antenna for command guidance datalink.

    Mechanical interface to the guidance section is typically a threaded ring or bayonet coupling with a positive lock. Electrical connections route through a multi-way connector at the interface plane.

    24.2.2 Guidance Section¶

    The guidance section contains the avionics — navigation computer, signal processors, RF receivers, inertial measurement unit — packaged on printed circuit boards (PCBs) mounted on a chassis inside the pressure shell. Key design features:

    • Shock isolation: The chassis is mounted on silicone elastomer mounts to attenuate high-frequency vibration and shock from propulsion and flight.

    • Thermal control: Potted electronics, thermal interface materials, and metallic heat spreaders conduct waste heat to the airframe skin.

    • EMC shielding: Aluminium or copper-plated electronics chassis forms a Faraday cage; filtered feedthroughs for all external signals.

    24.2.3 Warhead Section¶

    The warhead section houses the explosive fill and fuze. Structural requirements include:

    • Mechanical isolation from guidance electronics (blast partition).

    • Fuze pocket with electrical connector and sealing.

    • Sufficient structural rigidity to prevent deformation that would compromise fuze alignment.

    • Bore-sight accuracy of fuze initiator axis relative to body axis: \(< 0.5°\).

    24.2.4 Motor Section¶

    The motor section is the structural backbone. It is a pressure vessel (the motor case) that also serves as the primary load-carrying member in axial compression during boost. The motor section must:

    • Withstand maximum expected operating pressure (MEOP) with safety factor 1.25 (burst pressure test at 1.5 × MEOP).

    • Accept propellant loading access (inhibitor panels or spin-cast closure).

    • Provide flange interfaces fore and aft for attachment to adjacent sections.

    • Carry TVC nozzle loads at the aft closure.

    24.2.5 Fin and Tail Section¶

    The tail section carries the fin assembly and the TVC nozzle. Critical requirements:

    • Fin attachment must withstand maximum fin root bending moment without yielding.

    • Fin-fold mechanisms (for tube-launched weapons) must deploy reliably in \(< 0.2\) s.

    • Tail can provide the interface for the launch rail ejector shoe.

    • Nozzle-to-tail interface must be airtight to prevent hot gas leakage.

    24.3 Structural Materials¶

    Material selection for missile airframes involves trade-offs among specific strength, specific stiffness, high-temperature capability, density, machinability, and cost.

    24.3.1 Aluminium Alloys¶

    7075-T73 aluminium is the workhorse material for missile airframe sections not subject to elevated temperature. Its properties:

    • Density: \(\rho = 2.81\,\text{g/cm}^3\).

    • Yield strength: \(\sigma_y = 435\,\text{MPa}\).

    • Ultimate tensile strength: \(\sigma_u = 505\,\text{MPa}\).

    • Young’s modulus: \(E = 71\,\text{GPa}\).

    • Maximum service temperature: \(\approx 120°C\) (beyond which stress-corrosion properties degrade).

    The T73 temper provides superior stress-corrosion cracking (SCC) resistance compared to the stronger T6 temper — critical for long-term storage. Body rings, interface flanges, and guidance section housings are typically CNC-machined from 7075-T73 billet or forgings.

    24.3.2 High-Strength Steels¶

    300M (AMS 6257) and D6AC steels are used where very high strength is required in a smaller cross-section: bolt flanges, pin-loaded lugs, motor aft closures:

    • 300M: \(\sigma_y = 1,655\,\text{MPa}\); \(\sigma_u = 2,000\,\text{MPa}\); KIC = 60–90 MPa\(\sqrt{\text{m}}\).

    • D6AC: \(\sigma_y = 1,480\,\text{MPa}\); used in motor cases; excellent fracture toughness.

    • Density: \(7.83\,\text{g/cm}^3\) — approximately \(2.8\times\) heavier than aluminium per unit volume, but used in small highly-loaded sections where section geometry is constrained.

    24.3.3 Titanium Ti-6Al-4V¶

    Titanium Ti-6Al-4V (Grade 5) offers the best strength-to-weight ratio of metallic alloys at temperatures up to \(315°C\), and it is non-magnetic — important for proximity fuze function:

    • Density: \(4.43\,\text{g/cm}^3\).

    • Yield strength: \(\sigma_y = 880\,\text{MPa}\) (annealed).

    • Ultimate strength: \(\sigma_u = 950\,\text{MPa}\).

    • Young’s modulus: \(E = 114\,\text{GPa}\).

    • Fatigue strength (10\(^7\) cycles): \(\approx 500\,\text{MPa}\) (R = 0.1).

    Typical applications: seeker support structure, TVC actuator brackets, high-temperature fin attachment fittings, and nose tip structure for Mach 3–4 flight.

    24.3.4 Carbon Fibre Reinforced Polymer (CFRP)¶

    Filament-wound or prepreg CFRP provides the best specific strength of any airframe material:

    • Unidirectional IM7/8552 laminate: \(\sigma_{\text{tensile}} = 2,724\,\text{MPa}\); \(E_1 = 164\,\text{GPa}\); \(\rho = 1.57\,\text{g/cm}^3\).

    • Quasi-isotropic layup (\([0/\pm45/90]_s\)): \(\sigma_u \approx 600\,\text{MPa}\); \(E \approx 55\,\text{GPa}\).

    • Specific strength advantage over 7075-T6 Al: approximately \(3.5\times\) in tension.

    CFRP body tubes are fabricated by filament winding over a mandrel. The winding angle is optimised to balance hoop and axial strength:

    \[\frac{N_\theta}{N_x} = \tan^2\theta_{\text{wind}}\tag{24.7}\]

    where \(N_\theta\) is hoop load per unit length, \(N_x\) is axial load per unit length, and \(\theta_{\text{wind}}\) is the helix angle of the filament.

    24.3.5 Graphite-Phenolic and Ablative Composites¶

    Carbon/phenolic and silica/phenolic composites are used for high-temperature components exposed to motor exhaust plumes: nozzle exit cones, TVC vanes, and ablative nose tips. Their primary property is controlled ablation — the resin pyrolyses and chars, absorbing heat and protecting the underlying structure.

    Ablation rate (simplified):

    \[\dot{s} = \frac{\dot{q}”_{\text{aero}} - \dot{q}”_{\text{re-rad}}}{\rho_c \cdot \left(H_{\text{pyro}} + c_p \Delta T\right)}\tag{24.8}\]

    where \(\dot{q}”_{\text{aero}}\) is the incident heat flux, \(\dot{q}”_{\text{re-rad}}\) is the re-radiated flux (\(\epsilon \sigma T^4\)), \(\rho_c\) is composite density, \(H_{\text{pyro}}\) is the pyrolysis enthalpy, \(c_p\) is specific heat, and \(\Delta T\) is the temperature rise.

    24.3.6 Maraging Steel 350¶

    Maraging 350 steel (\(\sigma_u = 2{,}400\,\text{MPa}\)) is used for motor case closures and high-pressure fittings in boost motors where extreme burst pressure resistance is required. The material achieves its strength through martensite aging rather than carbon hardening, resulting in very high fracture toughness despite extreme strength.

    24.3.7 Beryllium (Strategic Applications)¶

    Beryllium’s unique combination of low density (\(1.85\,\text{g/cm}^3\)), high Young’s modulus (\(287\,\text{GPa}\)), and good thermal stability makes it attractive for inertial instrument flexures, mirror substrates, and precision structure in seeker assemblies. However, beryllium dust is highly toxic (berylliosis), requiring specialised machining and handling facilities. Strategic use is limited to components where no alternative meets the performance requirement.

    24.4 Aerodynamic Heating and Thermal Protection¶

    Aerodynamic heating is a critical design driver for missiles operating above Mach 2.5, and the dominant challenge for hypersonic vehicles above Mach 5.

    24.4.1 Stagnation Point Heat Rate¶

    The Sutton–Graves correlation estimates the stagnation point convective heat flux:

    \[\dot{q}_s = C \cdot \sqrt{\frac{\rho_\infty}{R_n}} \cdot V_\infty^3\tag{24.9}\]

    where \(C = 1.741 \times 10^{-4}\) (SI units, \(\dot{q}_s\) in W/m\(^2\)), \(\rho_\infty\) is free-stream density (kg/m\(^3\)), \(R_n\) is nose radius (m), and \(V_\infty\) is free-stream velocity (m/s). For a missile with \(R_n = 0.01\) m at Mach 5 at sea level (\(V_\infty = 1{,}700\,\text{m/s}\), \(\rho_\infty = 1.225\,\text{kg/m}^3\)):

    \[\dot{q}_s \approx 1.741\times10^{-4} \times \sqrt{1.225/0.01} \times (1700)^3 \approx 10\,\text{MW/m}^2\]

    This underscores the severe requirement for thermal protection at hypersonic speeds.

    24.4.2 Flat Surface Heating¶

    Along the body surface away from the stagnation point, the turbulent flat-plate reference enthalpy method provides heating rate distribution:

    \[\dot{q}_x = 0.0296 \cdot \rho_e^{0.8} \mu_e^{0.2} V_e \cdot c_{pe} \cdot (T_{\text{aw}} - T_w) \cdot x^{-0.2}\tag{24.10}\]

    where \(\rho_e\), \(\mu_e\), \(V_e\), \(c_{pe}\) are edge-condition properties, \(T_{\text{aw}}\) is adiabatic wall temperature, \(T_w\) is wall temperature, and \(x\) is distance from leading edge.

    24.4.3 Adiabatic Wall Temperature¶

    The adiabatic wall temperature (recovery temperature) is:

    \[T_{\text{aw}} = T_\infty \left(1 + r \cdot \frac{\gamma - 1}{2} M_\infty^2\right)\tag{24.11}\]

    where \(r\) is the recovery factor (\(r = \sqrt{Pr}\) for laminar; \(r = Pr^{1/3}\) for turbulent), \(\gamma = 1.4\) for air, and \(M_\infty\) is free-stream Mach number. At Mach 4, \(T_{\text{aw}} \approx 3.7 \times 288 = 1{,}065\,\text{K}\) — far exceeding the melting point of aluminium.

    24.4.4 Ablative Nose Tips¶

    Carbon-phenolic (C/Ph) and PICA (Phenolic Impregnated Carbon Ablator) are the primary ablative materials for missile nose tips:

    • PICA: Density 0.26 g/cm\(^3\); \(H_{\text{pyro}} \approx 8\,\text{MJ/kg}\); operating up to 3,000 K surface temperature.

    • Carbon-phenolic: Density 1.45 g/cm\(^3\); high ablation resistance; used in ICBM re-entry vehicles.

    • Silica-phenolic: Lower performance but cheaper; used for moderate heat flux (\(< 1\,\text{MW/m}^2\)) applications.

    Shape change during ablation must be predicted (coupled aero-thermal-ablation model) to ensure the nose maintains acceptable radar signature and aerodynamic drag characteristics.

    24.4.5 Seeker Dome Thermal Limits¶

    The seeker dome material must maintain electromagnetic transparency while surviving the thermal environment:

    • Sapphire (Al\(_2\)O\(_3\)): IR transparent 0.2–5.5 \(\mu\)m; service temperature to 1,900°C; excellent rain erosion resistance; expensive.

    • ZnS (Zinc sulphide): IR transparent to 12 \(\mu\)m; limited to \(300°C\); used on FLIR-seeker missiles with modest speed.

    • Zinc selenide (ZnSe): Transparent 0.5–22 \(\mu\)m; similar temp limit to ZnS.

    • Spinel (MgAl\(_2\)O\(_4\)): 0.2–5.5 \(\mu\)m; 1,600°C; better mechanical toughness than sapphire.

    24.5 Load Analysis¶

    24.5.1 Axial Load Distribution¶

    The axial load at any cross-section \(x\) along the body (measured from nose) is the net of thrust, drag, and inertia of the missile mass aft of station \(x\):

    \[F_{\text{ax}}(x) = T(t) \cdot \delta(x - x_{\text{nozzle}}) - D \cdot \frac{x}{L} - m_{\text{aft}}(x) \cdot \ddot{x}\tag{24.12}\]

    Peak compressive axial load typically occurs at the motor-to-guidance interface during maximum thrust, where the full motor thrust minus only the forward-body drag must be reacted.

    24.5.2 Body Bending Moment¶

    Treating the missile as a free-free beam under distributed aerodynamic load \(w(x)\) (N/m) and with lateral acceleration \(\ddot{y}\) applied at every mass element, the shear force \(V(x)\) and bending moment \(M(x)\) are obtained by integration:

    \[\frac{dV}{dx} = m'(x) \ddot{y} - w(x)\tag{24.13}\]
    \[\frac{dM}{dx} = V(x)\tag{24.14}\]

    where \(m'(x)\) is mass per unit length. For a first-order estimate, treating the missile as a uniform beam of length \(L\) with point lateral force \(F_{\text{lat}} = nW\) at \(x = x_{\text{CP}}\):

    \[M_{\max} = F_{\text{lat}} \cdot (x_{\text{CP}} - x_{\text{CM}}) \cdot \frac{x_{\text{CM}}}{L}\tag{24.15}\]

    The resulting bending stress at the outer fibre (radius \(R\), second moment of area \(I\)):

    \[\sigma_{\text{bend}} = \frac{M_{\max} \cdot R}{I}\tag{24.16}\]

    For a thin-walled tube: \(I = \pi R^3 t\) where \(t\) is wall thickness.

    24.5.3 Fin Root Bending Moment¶

    Each fin generates lift \(L_{\text{fin}}\) and experiences a bending moment at its root:

    \[M_{\text{fin\,root}} = q \cdot C_{N_\alpha}^{\text{fin}} \cdot \alpha \cdot S_{\text{fin}} \cdot \bar{c}_{\text{fin}}\tag{24.17}\]

    where \(q\) is dynamic pressure, \(C_{N_\alpha}^{\text{fin}}\) is fin normal force slope (per radian), \(\alpha\) is angle of attack, \(S_{\text{fin}}\) is fin planform area, and \(\bar{c}_{\text{fin}}\) is the span-wise moment arm from root to aerodynamic centre.

    24.5.4 Vibration and Acoustic Loads¶

    The launch vibration environment is specified as a power spectral density (PSD) in units of g\(^2\)/Hz. The root-mean-square (RMS) acceleration response of the structure at frequency \(f_n\):

    \[a_{\text{rms}} = \sqrt{\frac{\pi}{2} f_n Q \cdot W(f_n)}\tag{24.18}\]

    where \(Q = 1/(2\zeta)\) is the quality factor and \(W(f_n)\) is the input PSD at the natural frequency. This is Miles’ equation, widely used for single-degree-of-freedom response to broadband random vibration.

    24.6 Fin Design and Folding Mechanisms¶

    24.6.1 Fixed Fin Configuration¶

    Fixed fins are bonded and bolted into slots machined in the tail section. Bonding uses structural film adhesive (Redux 312 or equivalent); bolting provides positive mechanical retention and redundant load path. Fin planform shapes used in practice:

    • Delta: Simple manufacturing; high aspect ratio at transonic; common on SAMs.

    • Clipped delta: Removes inefficient tip region; favoured for Mach 2+.

    • Double-tapered (diamond section): Minimises wave drag at supersonic speed.

    • Rectangular: Simplest; acceptable for subsonic and low supersonic.

    24.6.2 Fin Flutter Analysis¶

    Fin flutter occurs when the aerodynamic forces excite the bending and torsional modes in phase. The simplified two-degree-of-freedom flutter speed (Garrick–Bisplinghoff approach for thin wings):

    \[V_F = \pi \omega_\theta b \sqrt{\frac{2\mu}{\rho_\infty C_{L_\alpha}}} \cdot \sqrt{1 - \left(\frac{\omega_h}{\omega_\theta}\right)^2}\tag{24.19}\]

    where \(\omega_\theta\) is the first torsional frequency (rad/s), \(\omega_h\) is the first bending frequency (rad/s), \(b\) is semi-chord (m), \(\mu = m/(\rho_\infty \pi b^2 s)\) is the mass ratio (\(m\) is fin mass, \(s\) is fin span), and \(C_{L_\alpha}\) is the lift curve slope (per radian). The design requirement is \(V_F > 1.15 \times V_{\max}\) (structural speed limit).

    24.6.3 Roll Stabilisation by Canted Fins¶

    Canted fins at angle \(\delta_{\text{cant}}\) to the body meridional plane produce a rolling moment:

    \[\dot{p} = \frac{M_{\text{roll}}}{I_x} = \frac{4 \cdot q \cdot S_{\text{fin}} \cdot r_{\text{fin}} \cdot C_{L_\alpha} \cdot \delta_{\text{cant}}}{I_x}\tag{24.20}\]

    where \(r_{\text{fin}}\) is the radial distance of the fin aerodynamic centre from the missile centreline and \(I_x\) is the roll moment of inertia. Equilibrium spin rate is achieved when roll damping equals roll driving moment.

    24.6.4 Folding Fin Mechanisms¶

    Tube-launched weapons (from man-portable launchers, aircraft pods, ship VLS cells) require fins to fold into the tube cross-section. Typical fold mechanisms:

    • Spring-loaded pivot: Torsion spring drives fin to deployed position; spring is pre-loaded at assembly; positive detent locks fin when deployed.

    • Wrap-around fins (WAF): Fins curve around the body during storage; spring or aerodynamic force deploys them.

    • Gas-driven actuator: Gas generator drives a piston that pivots fin to deployed position; faster than spring but adds complexity.

    Deployment time must be \(< 0.2\) s (typically \(50\)–\(100\) ms) to ensure roll stabilisation before the missile exits the launch tube or clears the aircraft pylon.

    24.7 Radome Design¶

    24.7.1 Electrical Design Fundamentals¶

    For a monolithic radome wall with dielectric constant \(\varepsilon_r\) and thickness \(d\), the transmission coefficient for normal incidence is:

    \[T = \frac{1}{1 + \frac{1}{4}\left(\sqrt{\varepsilon_r} - \frac{1}{\sqrt{\varepsilon_r}}\right)^2 \sin^2\left(\frac{2\pi d}{\lambda_d}\right)}\tag{24.21}\]

    where \(\lambda_d = \lambda_0 / \sqrt{\varepsilon_r}\) is the wavelength in the dielectric. Perfect transmission (\(T = 1\)) occurs when \(d = n\lambda_d/2\) (half-wave or full-wave wall). The design equation for half-wave wall thickness:

    \[d_{\text{HW}} = \frac{\lambda_0}{2\sqrt{\varepsilon_r}}\tag{24.22}\]

    For Si\(_3\)N\(_4\) (\(\varepsilon_r = 7.8\)) at \(10\) GHz (\(\lambda_0 = 30\) mm): \(d_{\text{HW}} = 30/(2\sqrt{7.8}) = 5.37\) mm — a practical structural thickness.

    24.7.2 Boresight Error¶

    The boresight error (BSE) is the angular difference between the true target direction and the apparent direction due to radome refraction. For an ogive radome, BSE is a function of seeker gimbal angle \(\theta_g\):

    \[\text{BSE}(\theta_g) \approx K_{\text{BSE}} \cdot \theta_g\tag{24.23}\]

    where \(K_{\text{BSE}}\) is the BSE slope (mrad/deg). Requirement: \(|K_{\text{BSE}}| < 0.1\) mrad/deg (i.e., \(< 0.3\) mrad at \(\theta_g = 3°\)). Boresight error rate (BSER) — the derivative \(d(\text{BSE})/d\theta_g\) — couples with the guidance loop and can cause limit cycles if excessive.

    24.7.3 Rain Erosion¶

    At supersonic speeds, impact with rain drops generates a water-hammer pressure pulse at the radome leading edge:

    \[p_{\text{WH}} = \rho_w \cdot c_w \cdot V_{\text{impact}}\tag{24.24}\]

    where \(\rho_w = 1{,}000\,\text{kg/m}^3\), \(c_w = 1{,}500\,\text{m/s}\), and \(V_{\text{impact}}\) is the relative velocity. At Mach 3 (\(V = 1{,}020\,\text{m/s}\)): \(p_{\text{WH}} \approx 1.5\,\text{GPa}\) — exceeding the compressive strength of most ceramics. Erosion mitigation:

    • Hard coatings: PVD alumina or titanium nitride coating 50–200 \(\mu\)m thick; dissipates energy before bulk material damage.

    • Nose shape optimisation: Sharper ogive (Von Kármán profile) reduces drop impact rate.

    • Material selection: Si\(_3\)N\(_4\) best resistance; PTFE/fibreglass worst.

    24.8 Manufacturing and Assembly¶

    24.8.1 Body Tube Fabrication¶

    • Spun aluminium: Hot-spinning of aluminium billet on mandrel; forms seamless tube with good mechanical properties; suitable for up to 300 mm diameter.

    • Roll-formed steel: Sheet material roll-formed into cylinder and seam-welded; lower cost for large-diameter motor cases.

    • Filament winding (CFRP): Continuous carbon fibre tow impregnated with epoxy, wound at controlled angle over mandrel, cured at 120–180°C in autoclave. Void content \(< 1\%\) required for structural credibility.

    Filament-wound motor cases are proof-pressure tested after manufacture at 1.1 × MEOP using an inert hydraulic fluid (water/glycol mixture). This verifies structural integrity without risk of propellant ignition.

    24.8.2 CNC Machining¶

    Interface rings and flanges are CNC-turned from 7075-T73 or D6AC billet. Key tolerances:

    • Bore diameter: \(\pm 0.025\) mm (running fit for O-ring sealing).

    • Flange flatness: \(< 0.05\) mm over diameter (ensures even bolt load distribution).

    • Thread form: ISO metric fine; lubricated with Molykote.

    24.8.3 Interface Control and Cleanliness¶

    The Interface Control Document (ICD) defines mechanical, electrical, and environmental requirements at every section joint. Cleanliness control is essential:

    • Particle contamination \(> 500\,\mu\)m must not be present near the fuze detonation train.

    • Seeker optics: Class 100 cleanroom for assembly (ISO 5).

    • Electronics: ESD-protected environment; relative humidity 40–60%.

    24.9 Shock and Vibration Isolation¶

    24.9.1 Isolator Design¶

    Silicone elastomer shock mounts isolate the guidance electronics from the missile airframe. The isolator is characterised by its static stiffness \(k_s\) and damping ratio \(\zeta\). The natural frequency of the isolated system:

    \[f_n = \frac{1}{2\pi}\sqrt{\frac{k_s}{m_{\text{payload}}}}\tag{24.25}\]

    Typical design targets: \(f_n = 15\)–\(30\) Hz so that launch environment energy (concentrated above 50 Hz) is attenuated by the isolation system. Transmissibility above \(f_n\):

    \[T_R(f) = \sqrt{\frac{1 + (2\zeta f/f_n)^2}{(1 - (f/f_n)^2)^2 + (2\zeta f/f_n)^2}}\tag{24.26}\]

    At \(f \gg f_n\), \(T_R \rightarrow (f_n/f)^2 / (2\zeta)\), showing attenuation proportional to \((f_n/f)^2\).

    24.9.2 Shock Response Spectrum¶

    Pyroshock events (separation charges, fin deployment squibs) generate extremely high amplitude, short-duration shocks. The shock environment is characterised by the Shock Response Spectrum (SRS), which represents the peak response of a single-degree-of-freedom oscillator of natural frequency \(f_n\) to the measured shock:

    \[\text{SRS}(f_n) = \max_t \left| \omega_n \int_0^t \ddot{x}(\tau) e^{-\zeta \omega_n (t-\tau)} \sin[\omega_n(t-\tau)]\, d\tau \right|\tag{24.27}\]

    Equipment is designed to survive the SRS plus 3 dB margin. Fragility levels for typical electronics: 30–100 g depending on construction (through-hole vs SMD, potted vs un-potted).

    24.10 Aeroelasticity¶

    24.10.1 Body Bending Mode Coupling with Guidance¶

    If the missile body bending natural frequency \(\omega_b\) is insufficiently separated from the guidance loop bandwidth \(\omega_g\), the rate gyros sense structural bending motion as flight path rate, feeding spurious signals into the autopilot. This coupling can cause instability.

    Design requirement:

    \[\omega_b > 3 \omega_g\tag{24.28}\]

    For a guidance bandwidth \(\omega_g = 10\) rad/s, the first body bending mode must exceed \(30\) rad/s (4.8 Hz). This requirement drives minimum wall thickness and material selection for slender missile bodies (\(L/D > 15\)).

    The effective natural frequency of a uniform beam (first mode):

    \[\omega_{b1} = \frac{(1.875)^2}{L^2} \sqrt{\frac{EI}{\rho_L}}\tag{24.29}\]

    where \(EI\) is bending stiffness, \(L\) is missile length, and \(\rho_L\) is linear mass density.

    24.10.2 Structural Damping¶

    Structural damping in CFRP airframes is characterised by the loss factor \(\eta = 2\zeta\):

    • CFRP: \(\zeta = 0.01\)–\(0.03\) (loss factor 0.02–0.06).

    • Aluminium: \(\zeta = 0.001\)–\(0.005\).

    • Steel: \(\zeta = 0.001\)–\(0.003\).

    The higher intrinsic damping of CFRP relative to metals is beneficial for reducing structural resonance amplification (\(Q = 1/(2\zeta)\)) and for aeroacoustic fatigue.

    24.10.3 Fin Flutter Speed Requirement¶

    Flutter speed must exceed the maximum structural speed with adequate margin:

    \[V_F \geq 1.20 \times V_{\text{max,struct}}\tag{24.30}\]

    \(V_{\text{max,struct}}\) is the maximum dive speed including a 15% overspeed allowance above the maximum operating airspeed. If \(V_F\) is insufficient, design options include:

    • Increase torsional stiffness (thicker skin, carbon orientation change).

    • Reduce fin span (reduces aerodynamic forces but also reduces stability).

    • Add mass balance weight near leading edge (shifts frequency ratio \(\omega_h/\omega_\theta\)).
