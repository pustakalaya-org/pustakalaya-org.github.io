    Chapter 21: Seeker Systems¶

    21.1 Seeker Systems¶

    Seeker systems constitute the perceptual front-end of a guided weapon. They measure the angular relationship between the missile body axis and the optical, electromagnetic, or acoustic line-of-sight (LOS) to the target, and they generate the angular rate signal that feeds the proportional navigation (PN) guidance law. Modern seekers combine precision stabilised gimbals, sophisticated detector arrays, and embedded signal processors into packages that must survive launch shock, sustain tracking through violent manoeuvring, and resist deliberate countermeasures—all within severe constraints on mass, volume, and cost.

    This chapter treats each seeker modality in systematic engineering depth: infrared (IR), imaging infrared (IIR), semi-active and active radar, laser and electro-optical, and multi-mode architectures. Signal processing, gimbal design, hardware-in-the-loop (HWIL) testing, and future technologies complete the treatment.

    21.2 Seeker Fundamentals¶

    21.2.1 Purpose and Information Flow¶

    The primary function of a seeker is to measure the angular error between the missile longitudinal axis and the target LOS, and to differentiate that angle to produce the LOS rate vector \(\dot{\boldsymbol{\lambda}} = [\dot{\lambda}_{az},\,\dot{\lambda}_{el}]^\top\). This two-component signal is the essential input to the proportional navigation (PN) guidance law:

    \[\mathbf{a}_{c} = N' V_{c}\, \dot{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}\tag{21.1}\]

    where \(N'\) is the effective navigation ratio (typically 3–5), \(V_c\) is the closing velocity, and \(\mathbf{a}_c\) is the commanded lateral acceleration perpendicular to the current LOS. Any error or noise in \(\dot{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}\) propagates directly into miss distance; therefore seeker noise is the dominant performance driver in the terminal phase of flight.

    21.2.2 Geometric Relationships¶

    Let \(\boldsymbol{r}\) be the missile-to-target relative position vector. The LOS angle in the inertial frame is:

    \[\lambda = \arctan\!\left(\frac{r_y}{r_x}\right)\tag{21.2}\]

    and the LOS rate is:

    \[\dot{\lambda} = \frac{r_x \dot{r}_y - r_y \dot{r}_x}{r_x^2 + r_y^2} = \frac{\dot{r}_\perp}{r}\tag{21.3}\]

    where \(\dot{r}_\perp\) is the component of relative velocity perpendicular to the LOS and \(r = |\boldsymbol{r}|\). For an unguided (constant-velocity) collision course \(\dot{\lambda} = 0\); any deviation indicates the geometry is not collision and guidance is required.

    21.2.3 Gimbal Stabilisation¶

    In the inertial frame the seeker must hold its optical axis on the target regardless of missile body motion. This is achieved through a gyroscopically stabilised gimbal. An inner-loop rate gyro (mounted on the gimbal itself) measures gimbal inertial angular rate \(\dot{\theta}_{gimbal}\) and drives a torque motor to null the residual error. The closed-loop transfer function of the stabilisation loop is:

    \[\frac{\dot{\theta}_{gimbal}(s)}{\dot{\theta}_{body}(s)} = \frac{K_s}{s + K_s}\tag{21.4}\]

    where \(K_s\) is the stabilisation loop gain (typically 200–500 rad/s). The residual coupling from body motion to apparent LOS rate is:

    \[\dot{\lambda}_{parasite} = \dot{\theta}_{body}\cdot\frac{s}{s+K_s}\tag{21.5}\]

    For \(K_s = 300\) rad/s, the body coupling at 3 Hz (typical airframe bending mode) is attenuated by 20 dB.

    21.2.4 Tracking Bandwidth and Noise¶

    The seeker tracker must follow target angular motion, which may reach angular rates of 30–40 rad/s in a high-closing-speed engagement. The tracking bandwidth \(\omega_T\) (inner angle-tracking loop) must satisfy:

    \[\omega_T \geq 2\,\dot{\lambda}_{max} / \varepsilon_{max}\tag{21.6}\]

    where \(\varepsilon_{max}\) is the maximum allowable track lag. Typical values are \(\omega_T = 10\)–50 rad/s.

    Seeker noise is characterised by the noise equivalent angle (NEA), \(\sigma_\lambda\), and the resulting LOS-rate noise density \(S_{\dot{\lambda}}(\omega)\). Via adjoint analysis, the contribution to miss distance variance is:

    \[\sigma_{miss}^2 = \int_0^\infty S_{\dot{\lambda}}(\omega)\, \left|H_{miss}(j\omega)\right|^2 d\omega\tag{21.7}\]

    where \(H_{miss}\) is the adjoint transfer function from LOS-rate noise to miss distance. A rule of thumb for PN guidance with \(N'=4\) is:

    \[\sigma_{miss} \approx \frac{V_c\,t_{go}^2}{6}\,\sigma_{\dot{\lambda}}\tag{21.8}\]

    where \(t_{go}\) is the time-to-go and \(\sigma_{\dot{\lambda}}\) is the RMS LOS-rate noise. This highlights why seeker noise is so consequential in the last few seconds of flight.

    21.2.5 Signal-to-Noise Ratio Requirement¶

    Reliable track-lock requires a minimum SNR at the detector or receiver:

    \[\mathrm{SNR}_{min} = 10\;\text{dB} \quad\text{(track maintenance)}\tag{21.9}\]

    Below this threshold, the tracking loop loses coherence and track is broken. Acquisition typically requires SNR \(\geq\) 15 dB to ensure unambiguous lock-on. The seeker designer must verify that SNR exceeds these thresholds across the entire engagement envelope: maximum range, minimum target signature, and worst-case atmospheric conditions.

    21.3 Infrared Seekers¶

    21.3.1 Overview and Detector Bands¶

    Infrared seekers detect the thermal emission and reflected IR radiation from the target. The atmosphere has two primary transmission windows exploited by IR seekers:

    • MWIR (mid-wave IR): 3–5 \(\mu\)m — dominated by hot exhaust gas (CO\(_2\), H\(_2\)O emission bands near 4.3 \(\mu\)m) and hot metal surfaces. Detectors: InSb cooled to 77 K; HgCdTe (MCT).

    • LWIR (long-wave IR): 8–12 \(\mu\)m — aerodynamic heating and body skin emission; ambient background objects; uncooled bolometers are feasible. Detectors: uncooled VOx microbolometer; cooled MCT.

    • SWIR (short-wave IR): 1–2.5 \(\mu\)m — reflected solar illumination; laser spot (1064 nm, 1550 nm); InGaAs photodiode arrays; no cooling required.

    21.3.2 Reticle-Based Point-Source Seekers¶

    Early and low-cost IR seekers use a reticle (chopper disc) to modulate the incoming target spot and derive angle-error information.

    SpinScan (Amplitude Modulation).

    A spinning reticle with alternating transparent and opaque sectors amplitude-modulates the target spot at frequency \(f_r = \Omega/2\pi\). When the target is centred on the optical axis the modulation depth is zero; off-axis, the signal amplitude is proportional to the angular error \(\varepsilon\) and the phase of the modulation relative to a reference encoder gives the error direction:

    \[V_{signal}(t) = A\,\varepsilon\,\cos(\Omega t - \phi_{dir})\tag{21.10}\]

    where \(\phi_{dir}\) is the azimuth direction of the target offset. SpinScan is susceptible to background bias: a uniform hot cloud can produce a false signal.

    ConScan (Conical Scan).

    The optical axis is nutated (precessed) around the seeker axis at rate \(\Omega\). When on-target the signal strength is constant; an off-axis target modulates signal amplitude at \(\Omega\). The error signal is:

    \[\varepsilon_{AZ} = K_{cs}\,A_{ac}\cos\phi; \qquad \varepsilon_{EL} = K_{cs}\,A_{ac}\sin\phi\tag{21.11}\]

    where \(A_{ac}\) is the AC amplitude and \(K_{cs}\) is a scale factor. ConScan is less susceptible to uniform background but vulnerable to amplitude-modulated jamming at frequency \(\Omega\).

    Rosette Scan.

    The spot traces a rosette pattern covering the FOV. Acquisition capability is superior to SpinScan; used in Stinger (POST variant). The scan pattern is generated by superposing two circular motions:

    \[x(t) = A\cos(\omega_1 t) + B\cos(\omega_2 t); \quad y(t) = A\sin(\omega_1 t) + B\sin(\omega_2 t)\tag{21.12}\]

    where \(\omega_1,\,\omega_2\) are incommensurate to ensure dense coverage.

    21.3.3 Imaging IR (IIR) Seekers¶

    IIR seekers replace the single-element detector and reticle with a focal plane array (FPA), producing a full 2D thermal image of the scene at each frame. This transforms the seeker into an imaging sensor and enables:

    • Target shape recognition and discrimination

    • Aimpoint selection at a specific vulnerable zone

    • Robust IRCCM via image-based tracking algorithms

    • Lock-on at longer ranges due to larger detector area

    FPA Specifications.

    Current military FPAs are typically \(320\times256\) or \(640\times512\) elements, with pixel pitch 15–30 \(\mu\)m and readout integrated circuits (ROICs) providing integration time control, non-uniformity correction (NUC), and serial digital output. The NETD (noise equivalent temperature difference) for cooled MCT arrays is 20–50 mK; for uncooled bolometers it is 30–80 mK.

    The number of target pixels at range \(R\) is:

    \[N_{pix} = \frac{A_{target}}{R^2} \cdot \frac{f^2}{p^2}\tag{21.13}\]

    where \(f\) is focal length and \(p\) is pixel pitch. Reliable tracking requires \(N_{pix} \geq 4\); ATR requires \(N_{pix} \geq 25\)–64.

    21.3.4 Stirling Cryocooler¶

    Cooled detectors (InSb, MCT) require operation at 77 K. Linear Stirling cryocoolers are the standard solution:

    • Input power: 1.5–5 W electrical

    • Cool-down time: 3–10 minutes (typically reduced to \(<\)90 s for tactical seekers via pre-cooled dewar strategies)

    • MTBF: 5000–8000 h (military-grade)

    • Mass: 80–300 g depending on cooling capacity

    • Vibration export: \(<\)0.5 g at compressor frequency; requires dual-piston or active cancellation

    The thermodynamic COP (coefficient of performance) of the Stirling cycle:

    \[\mathrm{COP} = \frac{Q_{cold}}{W_{input}} = \eta_{Stirling}\,\frac{T_{cold}}{T_{hot}-T_{cold}}\tag{21.14}\]

    where \(\eta_{Stirling} \approx 0.3\)–0.5 for practical devices.

    21.3.6 Lock-On Range¶

    The IR seeker lock-on range is derived from the radiometric link equation. Irradiance at the detector from a target of intensity \(I_{target}\) (W/sr) at range \(R\) after atmospheric transmission \(\tau_{atm}\):

    \[E_{det} = \frac{I_{target}\,\tau_{atm}\,A_{opt}}{R^2}\tag{21.15}\]

    Setting \(E_{det} \cdot A_{det} = \mathrm{SNR}_{req}\cdot \mathrm{NEP}\) and solving for \(R\):

    \[R_{lock} = \sqrt{\frac{I_{target}\,\tau_{atm}\,A_{opt}\,A_{det}} {\mathrm{SNR}_{req}\cdot\mathrm{NEP}}}\tag{21.16}\]

    Equivalently, in terms of the detector specific detectivity \(D^*\):

    \[R_{lock} = D^*\,\sqrt{\frac{I_{target}\,\tau_{atm}\,A_{opt}} {\mathrm{SNR}_{req}\cdot\sqrt{A_{det}\,\Delta f}}}\tag{21.17}\]

    where \(\Delta f\) is the noise bandwidth of the pre-detection filter.

    21.3.7 IRCCM Techniques¶

    Infrared countercountermeasures (IRCCM) protect the seeker against flares, laser jammers, and directed IR jamming. Major techniques:

    Dual-band discrimination:
    Compare MWIR and LWIR signatures. Targets have characteristic spectral ratios; flares typically emit differently (flares peak in MWIR; plume peak in LWIR varies). A discrimination threshold \(R_{band} = I_{MWIR}/I_{LWIR}\) is compared against the expected target ratio.
    Kinematic rejection:
    Flares decelerate rapidly after ejection. The seeker tracks the rate of change of angular velocity; a genuine target maintains consistent kinematics, while a flare diverges rapidly.
    Spectral discrimination:
    High-resolution spectral analysis identifies CO\(_2\) and H\(_2\)O emission features unique to jet exhaust. Flares (magnesium-based) have different spectra.
    Image processing / shape tracking (IIR):

    With FPA-based seekers, the target shape (fuselage, wing planform, exhaust nozzle) is tracked via template matching. Flares are compact point sources with different morphology. Normalised cross-correlation:

    \[\mathrm{NCC}(\mathbf{u}) = \frac{\sum_{x,y} [I(x,y)-\bar{I}][T(x-u_x,y-u_y)-\bar{T}]} {\sqrt{\sum_{x,y}[I-\bar{I}]^2\sum_{x,y}[T-\bar{T}]^2}}\tag{21.18}\]
    Jam-to-signal ratio monitoring:
    The seeker processor monitors sudden signal-level jumps indicative of DIRCM (directed IR countermeasure) illumination and triggers IRCCM logic.

    21.4 Radar Seekers¶

    21.4.1 Semi-Active Radar Homing (SARH)¶

    In SARH, the launch platform maintains a CW or pulsed-CW radar illumination on the target. The missile receiver tracks the reflected signal. The radar equation for the bistatic SARH link is:

    \[P_r = \frac{P_t\,G_t\,G_r\,\sigma\,\lambda^2} {(4\pi)^3\,R_t^2\,R_m^2\,L}\tag{21.19}\]

    where \(R_t\) is the illuminator-to-target range, \(R_m\) is the missile-to-target range, \(\sigma\) is the target radar cross-section, and \(L\) is the total system loss.

    Doppler Processing.

    The closing rate between missile and target is measured from the Doppler shift:

    \[f_d = \frac{2\,f_0\,V_c}{c} = \frac{2\,V_c}{\lambda}\tag{21.20}\]

    where \(V_c = -\dot{r}\) is the closing velocity and \(f_0\) is the carrier frequency. For \(f_0 = 10\) GHz and \(V_c = 500\) m/s, \(f_d = 33.3\) kHz. The Doppler filter centred on the expected \(f_d\) improves clutter rejection.

    Monopulse Angle Tracking.

    Angle measurement in SARH uses monopulse processing. A four-horn or five-horn cluster forms sum (\(\Sigma\)) and difference (\(\Delta_{AZ}\), \(\Delta_{EL}\)) beams. The angle error in azimuth is:

    \[\varepsilon_{AZ} = k_{mon}\,\frac{\mathrm{Re}(\Delta_{AZ}/\Sigma)} {|\Sigma|^2/|\Sigma|^2} = k_{mon}\,\frac{\Delta_{AZ}}{\Sigma}\tag{21.21}\]

    where \(k_{mon}\) is the monopulse slope (rad\(^{-1}\)). The monopulse technique is instantaneous (single pulse) and is immune to amplitude-modulated jamming that defeats sequential lobing.

    21.4.2 Active Radar Homing (ARH)¶

    Active seekers carry their own transmitter, making the weapon fire-and-forget after launch. The monostatic radar equation is:

    \[P_r = \frac{P_t\,G^2\,\sigma\,\lambda^2} {(4\pi)^3\,R^4\,L}\tag{21.22}\]

    where all quantities now refer to the seeker alone. The required minimum \(P_t\) to achieve lock at range \(R_{lock}\) with \(\mathrm{SNR}_{req}\) is:

    \[P_t = \frac{(4\pi)^3\,R_{lock}^4\,k\,T_s\,B_n\,F\,\mathrm{SNR}_{req}\,L} {G^2\,\sigma\,\lambda^2}\tag{21.23}\]

    and the SNR-limited lock-on range:

    \[R_{lock} = \left[\frac{P_t\,G^2\,\sigma\,\lambda^2\,\tau\,B_n} {(4\pi)^3\,k\,T\,F\,\mathrm{SNR}_{req}\,L}\right]^{1/4}\tag{21.24}\]

    Waveform Design.

    Modern ARH seekers use Linear Frequency Modulated (LFM) pulse compression. A pulse of bandwidth \(B\) yields range resolution:

    \[\delta R = \frac{c}{2B}\tag{21.25}\]

    For \(B = 500\) MHz, \(\delta R = 0.3\) m. The time-bandwidth product (pulse compression ratio) is \(TB\); typical values are 100–1000. Velocity resolution from coherent integration of \(N\) pulses over integration time \(T_c\):

    \[\delta v = \frac{\lambda}{2\,T_c}\tag{21.26}\]

    AESA Seeker.

    Active electronically scanned array (AESA) seekers replace the mechanically gimballed antenna with a phased array. A planar array of \(M\times N\) transmit/receive (T/R) modules provides:

    • Electronic beam steering at \(\pm\)60\(^\circ\) without mechanical motion

    • Simultaneous multiple beams for guidance + ESM functions

    • Bandwidth 1–5 GHz enabling pulse compression

    • Low probability of intercept (LPI) waveforms

    • Monopulse angle accuracy: \(\sigma_\theta \approx \theta_{3dB}/(2\sqrt{2\,SNR})\)

    The beam steering phase shift for element \((m,n)\) to steer to angle \((\theta,\phi)\):

    \[\psi_{mn} = \frac{2\pi}{\lambda}(m\,d_x\sin\theta\cos\phi + n\,d_y\sin\theta\sin\phi)\tag{21.27}\]

    Constant False Alarm Rate (CFAR) Detection.

    CFAR processing adapts the detection threshold to the local clutter level. In cell-averaging CFAR (CA-CFAR) with \(N\) reference cells:

    \[T_{CFAR} = \alpha\,\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N} x_i\tag{21.28}\]

    where \(\alpha\) is the threshold multiplier chosen to achieve the desired false alarm rate \(P_{fa}\):

    \[\alpha = N\left(P_{fa}^{-1/N} - 1\right)\tag{21.29}\]

    21.4.3 Anti-Radiation Seeker (Passive)¶

    Anti-radiation missiles (ARMs) home on the target’s own electromagnetic emissions, requiring no illumination from the launch platform.

    Wide-Band ESM Receiver.

    Coverage: 0.5–18 GHz (extendable to 40 GHz); instantaneous bandwidth typically 500 MHz with channelised or digital receiver scanning.

    Interferometric Direction Finding.

    Phase-difference interferometry using two antennas separated by baseline \(d\):

    \[\Delta\phi = \frac{2\pi\,d\,\sin\theta}{\lambda}\tag{21.30}\]

    Solving for target bearing:

    \[\theta = \arcsin\!\left(\frac{\lambda\,\Delta\phi}{2\pi\,d}\right)\tag{21.31}\]

    Angle accuracy: \(\sigma_\theta = \lambda/(2\pi\,d\,\sqrt{\mathrm{SNR}})\). For \(d = 0.5\) m, \(f = 10\) GHz, SNR \(=20\) dB, \(\sigma_\theta \approx 0.1\) mrad.

    Memory Mode.

    When the target radar is switched off, the ARM’s inertial navigation system maintains the last-known LOS direction and the seeker continues guiding toward the estimated target location. Updated bearing estimates from brief radar re-emissions are fused with the INS propagated position.

    21.4.4 ECCM in Active Radar Seekers¶

    • Frequency agility: Pulse-to-pulse frequency hopping over \(>100\) MHz defeats narrowband noise jammers.

    • PRF agility: Randomised pulse repetition intervals defeat range-gate pull-off (RGPO) jamming.

    • LPI waveforms: Low peak power, wide bandwidth, random modulation reduces probability of intercept by target ESM receivers.

    • Adaptive CFAR: Detects jamming-induced threshold elevation; uses alternative reference cells.

    • Home-on-jam (HOJ): If a jammer is sufficiently powerful, the seeker switches from radar tracking to passive tracking of the jammer beam.

    21.5 Laser Seekers¶

    21.5.1 Semi-Active Laser (SAL) Homing¶

    SAL seekers require a laser designator (airborne, ground-based, or SOF-carried) to illuminate the target. The designator pulses at a coded pulse repetition frequency (PRF) and the seeker’s quadrant photodetector measures the centroid of the returned laser spot.

    Wavelengths and Atmospheric Windows.

    • \(\lambda = 1064\) nm: Nd:YAG; most common; good Si detector response; atmospheric transmission excellent in clear air.

    • \(\lambda = 1550\) nm: Er:glass fibre laser; eye-safe at Class 1 power levels at range; requires InGaAs detector.

    • \(\lambda = 532\) nm: frequency-doubled Nd:YAG; visible; limited tactical use.

    Quadrant Detector Angle Sensing.

    The quadrant (four-element) detector produces four signals \(Q_A\)–\(Q_D\). Angle error signals:

    \[\varepsilon_{AZ} = K_Q\,\frac{(Q_A + Q_D) - (Q_B + Q_C)} {Q_A + Q_B + Q_C + Q_D}\tag{21.32}\]
    \[\varepsilon_{EL} = K_Q\,\frac{(Q_A + Q_B) - (Q_C + Q_D)} {Q_A + Q_B + Q_C + Q_D}\tag{21.33}\]

    The normalisation by total signal makes the angle measurement independent of absolute signal level. \(K_Q\) is the quadrant detector scale factor (rad/\(\Delta\)signal).

    Radiometric Link Equation.

    The received power at the detector from a Lambertian target (reflectivity \(\rho\), area \(A_{spot}\), cosine factor \(\cos\theta\)):

    \[P_r = \frac{E_L\,\rho\,A_{det}\,\tau_{atm}^2\,\cos\theta} {\pi\,R^2\,\Omega_{spot}}\tag{21.34}\]

    where \(E_L\) is the designator pulse energy (J), \(\Omega_{spot}\) is the solid angle of the laser spot, and \(\tau_{atm}^2\) accounts for two-way atmospheric attenuation.

    Atmospheric Attenuation.

    Beer–Lambert law for laser propagation:

    \[I(R) = I_0\,\exp(-\beta R)\tag{21.35}\]

    where the attenuation coefficient \(\beta\) (m\(^{-1}\)) combines molecular absorption \(\beta_{mol}\) and aerosol scattering \(\beta_{aer}\):

    \[\beta = \beta_{mol} + \beta_{aer}\tag{21.36}\]

    For \(\lambda = 1064\) nm in clear air, \(\beta \approx 0.1\)–0.3 km\(^{-1}\); in moderate haze, \(\beta \approx 0.5\)–1 km\(^{-1}\); in thick smoke, \(\beta\) can exceed 5 km\(^{-1}\).

    PRF Coding.

    The designator transmits coded pulse trains (typically 4–8 pulse sequences) to prevent fratricide. The seeker performs temporal pattern matching:

    \[\hat{c} = \arg\max_{c}\sum_{k} y(t_k)\cdot p_c(t_k)\tag{21.37}\]

    where \(y(t_k)\) are the received pulse timestamps and \(p_c\) is the expected pattern for code \(c\).

    Accuracy.

    SAL seekers achieve CEP \(<\) 1–3 m in clear conditions. Accuracy degrades in turbulence (beam wander \(\sigma_{BW} \propto C_n^2 R^{11/6}\)) and smoke obscuration.

    21.5.2 Active Laser (LADAR) Terminal Seeker¶

    Missile-borne pulsed LADAR provides 3D point-cloud imagery of the target in the terminal phase. Range measurement:

    \[R = \frac{c\,\Delta t}{2}\tag{21.38}\]

    with timing accuracy \(\sigma_{\Delta t} \approx 0.1\) ns giving \(\sigma_R \approx 15\) mm. Flash LADAR illuminates the entire scene in one pulse, producing a \(128\times128\) range/intensity image per shot. Applications include:

    • Aimpoint selection on 3D target model (e.g., engine intake vs. cockpit vs. fuel tank)

    • Decoy discrimination by 3D shape comparison

    • Terminal navigation update: compare measured point-cloud to stored 3D target model; CEP \(<\) 0.1 m achievable

    21.6 Electro-Optical Seekers¶

    21.6.1 TV (Visible Band) Seekers¶

    Television seekers use silicon CCD or CMOS arrays in the visible band (0.4–0.9 \(\mu\)m). Key characteristics:

    • Resolution: 640\(\times\)480 to 1920\(\times\)1080 pixels

    • Frame rate: 30–120 Hz

    • Tracking: centroid or correlation algorithm

    • Limitation: passive daylight only; cannot operate at night or in smoke/cloud

    Centroid Tracker.

    The target centroid in the image plane is computed as the intensity-weighted mean of the target-segmented pixels:

    \[\bar{x} = \frac{\sum_{(i,j)\in\mathcal{T}} i\,I(i,j)} {\sum_{(i,j)\in\mathcal{T}} I(i,j)}, \qquad \bar{y} = \frac{\sum_{(i,j)\in\mathcal{T}} j\,I(i,j)} {\sum_{(i,j)\in\mathcal{T}} I(i,j)}\tag{21.39}\]

    The angular error signals are then \(\varepsilon_{AZ} = (\bar{x} - x_c) \cdot \delta_{pix}\) and similarly for elevation, where \(\delta_{pix}\) is the pixel angular subtense (IFOV).

    21.6.2 DSMAC: Digital Scene Matching Area Correlator¶

    The Tomahawk cruise missile employs DSMAC as a terminal navigation update (TNI). A stored reference image of the target area (from satellite reconnaissance) is correlated with the live seeker image:

    \[C(\mathbf{u}) = \sum_{x,y} I(x,y)\cdot T(x-u_x,\, y-u_y)\tag{21.40}\]

    The peak of \(C(\mathbf{u})\) gives the displacement of the missile from the planned aimpoint. This correction is fed to the guidance computer to correct accumulated INS drift. DSMAC achieves CEP \(<\) 3–10 m even after long-range cruise.

    21.6.3 Automatic Target Recognition (ATR)¶

    Modern IIR and TV seekers incorporate CNN-based ATR. A neural network (typically MobileNetV3 or a bespoke lightweight architecture) is trained offline on thermal signature databases. Onboard inference:

    \[\hat{c} = \arg\max_{c}\, P(class_c \mid \mathbf{x}; \boldsymbol{\theta})\tag{21.41}\]

    where \(\mathbf{x}\) is the image patch and \(\boldsymbol{\theta}\) are the network weights stored in flash memory. ATR enables:

    • Classification of tank, APC, truck, helicopter, ship

    • Automatic selection of the high-value target in a scene containing multiple objects

    • Aimpoint offset selection (e.g., turret ring vs. engine deck)

    • Rejection of decoys whose thermal signature doesn’t match the target class

    The ATR probability of correct identification (PID) must exceed 0.95 for autonomous operation; below this threshold, human-in-the-loop confirmation is required (man-on-the-loop ethics requirement).

    21.7 Multi-Mode Seekers¶

    21.7.1 Rationale and Architecture¶

    No single seeker modality excels in all engagement conditions:

    • IR fails in cloud/smoke/flares

    • Radar is susceptible to ECM and has limited aimpoint precision

    • Laser requires target illumination and degrades in obscurants

    • EO is daylight-only

    Multi-mode seekers combine two or more modalities on a shared gimbal to overcome individual limitations. Common pairings:

    • SAL + IIR (e.g., GBU-54 LJDAM concept, Dual Seeker variant): laser provides GPS-denied precision; IIR provides all-weather fallback.

    • ARH + IIR: active radar for acquisition at long range; IIR for precision terminal aimpointing.

    • ARH + SAL: fire-and-forget plus optional designator override; Brimstone dual-mode.

    • MMW + IIR: millimetre-wave radar penetrates smoke and cloud; IIR provides high-resolution aimpoint. Brimstone 2 uses this combination.

    21.7.2 Fusion Algorithms¶

    Weighted Combination.

    The fused LOS rate estimate is a reliability-weighted sum:

    \[\dot{\lambda}_{fused} = \frac{w_{IR}\,\dot{\lambda}_{IR} + w_{RF}\,\dot{\lambda}_{RF}} {w_{IR} + w_{RF}}\tag{21.42}\]

    where \(w_k = 1/\sigma_{\dot{\lambda},k}^2\) are the inverse variance weights.

    Interacting Multiple Model (IMM).

    The IMM estimator maintains \(M\) parallel Kalman filters, each modelling a different target motion (constant velocity, constant acceleration, coordinated turn). The fused state estimate:

    \[\hat{\mathbf{x}} = \sum_{k=1}^{M} \mu_k^{post}\,\hat{\mathbf{x}}_k\tag{21.43}\]

    where the model probabilities \(\mu_k^{post}\) are updated via Bayes’ rule on the likelihood of each model given the measurements. IMM provides smooth transitions between target motion modes and robustness against countermeasures.

    Track-Before-Detect (TBD).

    At low SNR (below the single-scan detection threshold), TBD integrates target energy across multiple scans before declaring detection. The optimal TBD test statistic is:

    \[\Lambda = \sum_{k=1}^{K} \log\frac{p(z_k | target)}{p(z_k | noise)}\tag{21.44}\]

    compared against a threshold \(h\). TBD extends acquisition range by 20–40% compared to single-scan detection.

    21.8 Gimbal and Stabilisation Design¶

    21.8.1 Gimbal Configurations¶

    Two-Axis Gimbal (Pitch/Yaw).

    The most common configuration for tactical missiles. The inner ring carries the detector/antenna and a rate gyro; the outer ring provides a second degree of freedom. The LOS is stabilised in two angular coordinates. Cross-coupling exists between axes; for small angles the coupling torque is:

    \[\tau_{couple} = I_z\,(\omega_{body} \times \boldsymbol{\Omega}_{gyro})\tag{21.45}\]

    Three-Axis Gimbal.

    Full decoupled stabilisation; required for bank-to-turn (BTT) missiles where the body rolls. Adds mass and complexity; slip rings must carry signals through three axes. Total angular momentum of inner assembly must be minimised to reduce torque export from gimbal to airframe.

    Strapdown Seeker.

    Some modern designs (particularly IIR with very wide FPA) use a strapdown (fixed) detector and electronically stabilise the LOS by digitally de-rotating the image using IMU data. The angular compensation applied to pixel \((u,v)\):

    \[\begin{pmatrix}u'\\v'\end{pmatrix} = \mathbf{R}_{stab}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{body}) \begin{pmatrix}u\\v\end{pmatrix}\tag{21.46}\]

    Strapdown eliminates mechanical gimbal but requires very fast (>1000 Hz) IMU and image processing.

    21.8.2 Stabilisation Loop Design¶

    The inner stabilisation loop architecture uses a rate gyro on the gimbal to measure inertial angular rate \(\dot{\theta}_{gim}\) and drives a torque motor to null this rate:

    \[G_{stab}(s) = \frac{K_p(1 + s/\omega_z)}{s(1 + s/\omega_p)}\tag{21.47}\]

    where \(\omega_z\) is the lead compensator zero and \(\omega_p\) is the pole. Stability margins: gain margin \(>\)6 dB, phase margin \(>\)45\(^\circ\). The friction in gimbal bearings is a primary disturbance; flexure bearings (no contact friction) are used in precision seekers but limit angular range to \(\pm\)5–10\(^\circ\) and require an outer coarse gimbal.

    21.8.3 Gimbal Angle Limits and Vignetting¶

    The practical angular range of a gimballed seeker is \(\pm\)35–60\(^\circ\) from the missile boresight. Beyond this limit, vignetting (partial obscuration of the aperture by the dome rim or gimbal structure) reduces sensitivity. The vignetting transmission factor:

    \[\tau_{vig}(\theta) = \cos(\theta/\theta_{max})\qquad \theta \leq \theta_{max}\tag{21.48}\]

    This imposes a practical constraint on launch geometry: the missile must be pointed within \(\pm\)35\(^\circ\) of the target at launch for reliable acquisition.

    21.8.4 Lock-Before-Launch vs Lock-After-Launch¶

    Lock-Before-Launch (LBL):
    The seeker is slaved (via databus) to the parent aircraft’s radar or IRST track. Before release, the seeker acquires the target and confirms lock (crosshair on track, SNR adequate). Only then does the pilot consent to release. Standard for short-range IR missiles (AIM-9, ASRAAM).
    Lock-After-Launch (LAL):
    The missile is released on a predicted bearing; after motor ignition the missile manoeuvres its seeker toward the target, acquires, and locks. Required when the target is beyond launch seeker acquisition range (long-range BVR engagements) or when rapid launch (spray-and-pray) tactics are used. The launch aircraft continues to provide target track updates via datalink until the seeker acquires.

    21.9 Seeker Signal Processing¶

    21.9.1 Angle Tracking Filters¶

    The seeker angle tracker maintains an estimate of target angular position and rate in the seeker frame. The Kalman filter state vector is:

    \[\mathbf{x}_k = [\lambda_{AZ},\,\dot{\lambda}_{AZ},\,\lambda_{EL}, \,\dot{\lambda}_{EL}]^\top\tag{21.49}\]

    with process model \(\mathbf{x}_{k+1} = \mathbf{F}\,\mathbf{x}_k + \mathbf{w}_k\) where:

    \[\mathbf{F} = \begin{pmatrix}1 & T & 0 & 0\\ 0 & 1 & 0 & 0\\ 0 & 0 & 1 & T\\ 0 & 0 & 0 & 1\end{pmatrix}\tag{21.50}\]

    \(T\) is the sample interval (typ. 10–50 ms). Measurement noise covariance \(R = \sigma_{NEA}^2 \mathbf{I}\). Process noise covariance \(Q = q\,\mathrm{diag}(T^3/3,\, T,\, T^3/3,\, T)\) where \(q\) is the manoeuvre noise intensity.

    21.9.2 Glint and Extended Target Effects¶

    For an extended target (a ship, aircraft, or large vehicle), the radar or IR return originates from distributed scattering centres. The apparent centre of radiation wanders randomly—this is called glint. The glint noise spectral density is approximately:

    \[S_{glint}(f) \approx \frac{\sigma_{glint}^2\,\tau_{glint}} {1 + (2\pi f \tau_{glint})^2}\tag{21.51}\]

    where \(\sigma_{glint}\) is the RMS glint displacement (typically 0.1–0.5 of target extent) and \(\tau_{glint}\) is the correlation time (0.01–1 s). Glint is white noise at frequencies below \(1/(2\pi\tau_{glint})\) and is a significant terminal miss distance contributor for radar seekers engaging large targets.

    21.9.3 MTI and Doppler Filtering¶

    Moving Target Indication (MTI) processing rejects stationary clutter. A two-pulse MTI canceller output:

    \[y_k = x_k - x_{k-1}\tag{21.52}\]

    The MTI improvement factor for \(N\)-pulse canceller with target Doppler \(f_d\) and clutter spectral width \(\sigma_v\):

    \[I_{MTI} = \frac{\mathrm{SNR}_{out}}{\mathrm{SNR}_{in}} = \frac{N}{\pi^2\,(2\sigma_v/\lambda)^2\,T_{PRI}^2}\tag{21.53}\]

    For a pulse Doppler (PD) seeker the target return is isolated in the Doppler domain; the Doppler filter bank covers PRF/2 to PRF/2 with \(N_{FFT}\) bins, each of width \(\Delta f_d = PRF/N_{FFT}\).

    21.10 Seeker Testing and Calibration¶

    21.10.1 Boresight Calibration¶

    Boresight error (BSE) is the angular offset between the seeker null (zero-error output direction) and the missile mechanical axis. BSE must be \(<\)0.5 mrad (\(\sim\)0.03\(^\circ\)) to avoid systematic offset at target impact. Calibration procedure:

    1. Mount missile on precision boresight stand aligned to theodolite

    2. Command seeker to null (zero error output)

    3. Measure optical axis direction using retro-reflection collimator

    4. Compute BSE and apply electrical correction bias

    5. Verify residual BSE \(<\)0.5 mrad across temperature range \(-\)40 to \(+\)71\(^\circ\)C

    21.10.3 Hardware-in-the-Loop (HWIL) Testing¶

    HWIL seeker testing places the physical seeker hardware on a multi-axis flight motion simulator (FMS) table with the seeker looking at a simulated target scene:

    • IR scene projector: Resistive array (128\(\times\)128 to 512\(\times\)512 elements) heated to simulate thermal scenes; dynamic update rate 200–1000 Hz.

    • RF scene generator: Signal injection into seeker receiver; simulates target return, clutter, and ECM at correct power levels.

    • Laser spot simulator: Pulsed laser diode with beam steering mirror; coded PRF sequence; dithered position for noise.

    • 6-DOF FMS table: Reproduces missile body angular rates from simulated 6-DOF trajectory; bandwidth 50–200 Hz; angular rate \(\pm\)500 deg/s.

    The HWIL simulation loop processes:

    \[\text{Seeker output } \dot{\lambda} \longrightarrow \text{Guidance computer} \longrightarrow \mathbf{a}_c \longrightarrow \text{6-DOF sim} \longrightarrow \dot{\theta}_{body} \longrightarrow \text{FMS table} \longrightarrow \text{Scene projector update}\tag{21.54}\]

    This closed-loop HWIL evaluates the complete sensor-to-guidance signal chain under realistic dynamic conditions.

    21.10.4 Environmental Testing¶

    All seekers must pass MIL-STD-810H environmental qualification:

    • Temperature-Altitude: Method 520; simulates high-altitude storage and operation; reduced convective cooling.

    • Vibration: Method 514.8; random vibration 0.04 g²/Hz across 20–2000 Hz; swept sine for resonance searches.

    • Shock: Method 516.8; functional shock 40 g, 11 ms; crash hazard 75 g, 11 ms.

    • Sand and Dust: Method 510.7; particulate ingestion.

    • Humidity: Method 507.6; 95% RH at 40\(^\circ\)C cycling.

    • Salt Fog: Method 509.7; maritime environments.

    EMI/EMC compliance is verified to MIL-STD-461G: radiated emissions RE102 (10 kHz–18 GHz); radiated susceptibility RS103 (up to 200 V/m); conducted susceptibility CS114 (bulk current injection).

    21.11 Advanced and Future Seeker Technologies¶

    21.11.1 Wide-Band AESA Radar Seeker with SAR Imaging¶

    Next-generation AESA seekers will achieve sufficiently wide instantaneous bandwidth to generate synthetic aperture radar (SAR) imagery of the target area. SAR cross-range resolution:

    \[\delta_{cr} = \frac{\lambda R}{2\,L_{syn}} = \frac{D_{antenna}}{2}\tag{21.55}\]

    This resolution is independent of range and depends only on the physical aperture size \(D_{antenna}\). For a 150 mm AESA seeker at \(Ka\)-band (\(\lambda = 8\) mm), \(\delta_{cr} = 75\) mm—sufficient to identify vehicle class from aimpoint selection. Inverse SAR (ISAR) uses target motion rather than missile motion to generate the synthetic aperture.

    21.11.2 Quantum Dot IR Detectors¶

    Conventional cooled IR detectors require cryogenic cooling to reduce dark current. Quantum dot (QD) nanocrystal detectors (e.g., PbS, PbSe, InAs QDs in colloidal suspension or epitaxially grown) exploit quantum confinement to tune the absorption cutoff wavelength by varying dot size:

    \[E_{gap}(r) = E_{gap,bulk} + \frac{\hbar^2\pi^2}{2r^2} \left(\frac{1}{m_e^*} + \frac{1}{m_h^*}\right)\tag{21.56}\]

    where \(r\) is the dot radius and \(m_e^*\), \(m_h^*\) are effective masses. MWIR QD detectors operating at room temperature have demonstrated \(D^* > 10^{10}\) Jones, approaching cooled InSb at 77 K. Elimination of the Stirling cryocooler would reduce seeker mass by 0.1–0.3 kg and remove the 90-second cool-down time constraint.

    21.11.3 RF/IR Dual-Mode on Single Gimbal¶

    Integrating a millimetre-wave (MMW) patch array antenna with an IIR detector on the same gimbal stabilisation platform reduces mass and cost compared to separate RF and IR gimbals. The shared gimbal must be transparent (or at least compatible) to both RF and IR; challenges include:

    • Dome material must transmit both 94 GHz MMW and 3–5 \(\mu\)m IR (no single natural material does this; solutions use a composite dome with a FSS element or a window aperture for each band)

    • Electromagnetic compatibility: high-power RF T/R modules must not saturate the sensitive IR detector

    • Gimbal cross-axis torque from the larger MMW antenna must not degrade IR stabilisation

    21.11.4 Cognitive and AI-Enabled Seekers¶

    The cognitive seeker paradigm applies machine learning throughout the signal chain:

    • Scene understanding: CNN detects and classifies all objects in the scene simultaneously, assigns lethality priorities.

    • Countermeasure recognition: Trained on jammer signatures; classifies jammer type and automatically selects the optimal IRCCM or ECCM response.

    • Adaptive waveform: Reinforcement learning agent selects radar waveform (frequency, PRF, bandwidth, power) to maximise SNR given real-time clutter and jamming environment.

    • Aimpoint selection: Identifies the most lethal aimpoint on the target structure (engine, fuel, crew).

    The inference engine runs on a radiation-hardened AI accelerator (e.g., a radiation-tolerant version of a systolic-array chip) consuming \(<\)5 W, delivering \(>\)1 TOPS inference throughput.

    21.11.5 Hyperspectral Seeker¶

    A hyperspectral seeker acquires a full spectral cube (\(x\)-spatial, \(y\)-spatial, \(\lambda\)-wavelength) at each frame. Typical specification:

    • Spectral range: 1–5 \(\mu\)m or 8–12 \(\mu\)m

    • Spectral channels: 64–256

    • Spatial pixels: 128\(\times\)128 per frame

    • Frame rate: 10–30 Hz

    Target discrimination uses the full spectral signature: paint type, metal alloy, exhaust composition, and fuel type leave unique spectral fingerprints. The detection statistic is the spectral matched filter:

    \[d_{SMF} = \frac{(\mathbf{s} - \boldsymbol{\mu})^\top \mathbf{C}^{-1}(\mathbf{t} - \boldsymbol{\mu})} {\sqrt{(\mathbf{s}-\boldsymbol{\mu})^\top \mathbf{C}^{-1}(\mathbf{s}-\boldsymbol{\mu})}}\tag{21.57}\]

    where \(\mathbf{t}\) is the measured pixel spectrum, \(\mathbf{s}\) is the target spectral signature, \(\boldsymbol{\mu}\) is the background mean, and \(\mathbf{C}\) is the background covariance matrix. The hyperspectral seeker provides the ultimate IRCCM capability: flares, decoys, and jammers cannot replicate the full spectral signature of a real target.

    21.11.6 Flash LiDAR Terminal Seeker¶

    Flash LiDAR (Light Detection and Ranging) illuminates the scene with a single pulse and captures a 2D range image simultaneously on an InGaAs Geiger-mode APD array. Each pixel records the photon time-of-arrival:

    \[R_{ij} = \frac{c\,(t_{ij} - t_{tx})}{2}\tag{21.58}\]

    yielding a 3D point cloud. At terminal engagement distances (100–500 m), aimpoint precision is:

    \[\sigma_{aimpoint} = \frac{R\cdot p_{det}}{f} \approx \text{1--10 cm}\tag{21.59}\]

    This exceeds the precision of any RF or single-band IR seeker. Flash LiDAR enables selective aimpointing on specific structural elements (jet intake, cockpit, missile rail) that would maximise damage or kill probability.
