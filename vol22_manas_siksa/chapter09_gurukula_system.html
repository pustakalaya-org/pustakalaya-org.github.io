    Chapter 9: The Teacher’s Household: Brahmanical Education¶

    mātṛdevo bhava | pitṛdevo bhava | ācāryadevo bhava | atithidevo bhava |
    Treat your mother as a god. Treat your father as a god. Treat your teacher as a god. Treat your guest as a god.
    — Taittirīya Upaniṣad 1.11.2

    9.1 Introduction¶

    The word gurukula, “the teacher’s family”, has become in modern India a byword for an idealised traditional education: a boy living in the forest home of a sage, serving him, learning from him by heart and by example. The ideal has a real basis in the Vedic and Dharmaśāstra sources, which describe in detail a period of studentship (brahmacarya) spent living with a teacher. But those sources are normative texts written by and for a Brahmin elite. They describe what their authors thought should happen, and they restricted Vedic education to a minority of the population: boys of the three upper varṇas, and in practice mainly Brahmin boys. This chapter describes the model as the texts present it, its methods and curriculum, and its exclusions. The broader landscape of Indian education — monasteries, colleges, vernacular schools, madrasas — is the subject of Chapter 11. The standard scholarly account is Scharfe (2002); the older surveys by Altekar (1965) and Mookerji (1947) are still useful for their collections of evidence but are more celebratory in tone.

    9.2 Teachers and Students¶

    9.2.1 Terms¶

    The Vedic and Dharmaśāstra texts use several words for teachers and students. The ācārya is the teacher who initiates a student and teaches him the Veda with its ritual and esoteric parts; the upādhyāya teaches a portion of the Veda or the auxiliary sciences for a living (Manu 2.140–141). The guru (“weighty one”) is a general term of respect that includes parents. The student is a brahmacārin, “one who lives the life of the Veda”, or an antevāsin, “one who dwells near” the teacher; the word śiṣya, “one to be taught”, is general.

    The popular etymology of guru as “remover (ru) of darkness (gu)” comes from late texts such as the Advayatāraka Upaniṣad; linguistically, guru simply means “heavy, weighty”. The etymology is nonetheless a fair expression of how the role was understood.

    9.2.2 The Qualified Teacher¶

    The Upaniṣads expect a student who seeks knowledge of Brahman to approach a qualified teacher with humility:

    Muṇḍaka Upaniṣad 1.2.12

    tadvijñānārthaṃ sa gurum evābhigacchet samitpāṇiḥ śrotriyaṃ brahmaniṣṭham
    To know that, he should go, firewood in hand, to a teacher who is learned in the Veda and grounded in Brahman.

    Carrying firewood was the student’s sign of willingness to serve: tending the teacher’s fire was one of his daily duties. The two qualifications — learning in the texts (śrotriya) and personal grounding in what they teach (brahmaniṣṭha) — became the standard description of the ideal teacher.

    9.2.3 The Student’s Conduct¶

    The Dharmasūtras of Āpastamba, Gautama, Baudhāyana and Vasiṣṭha (composed in the last centuries BCE) and the Manusmṛti (c. 2nd century CE) regulate the student’s life minutely (Olivelle 2005). He must rise before the teacher and go to bed after him; beg food from households and hand it over to the teacher; fetch water and firewood; tend the sacred fire; avoid honey, meat, perfumes, ornaments, dancing, gambling and the company of women; speak truthfully; and treat the teacher, the teacher’s wife and the teacher’s family with deference. The teacher in turn must teach the student without withholding anything and treat him like a son. The texts permit physical punishment, though some restrict it to a rope or a thin cane applied to the back, and threaten the teacher with penalties if he goes further.

    9.2.4 Teaching and Payment¶

    The normative texts held that Vedic knowledge should not be sold. A teacher received gifts and the student’s service, and at the end of studentship a final gift (gurudakṣiṇā), but teaching for a stipulated fee was frowned upon: Manu lists the “paid teacher” (bhṛtakādhyāpaka) and the student who pays a fee among those who should not be invited to an ancestral offering. In practice teachers depended on the gifts of students’ families, on royal and temple endowments, and on land grants (Chapter 11), and the epics and Jātakas speak of students who paid fees as a matter of course. The ideal of knowledge as a gift rather than a commodity was real, but it was sustained by an economy of patronage.

    The relationship was thus both affectionate and hierarchical. Its modern image as a model of mentoring should not obscure the degree of control the teacher exercised and the dependence of the student, who might spend many years in another man’s household as an unpaid servant.

    9.3 The Rites of Childhood¶

    Brahmanical education was framed by the life-cycle rites (saṃskāra) prescribed in the Gṛhyasūtras and Dharmaśāstras (Kane 1941). Before initiation a child of the twice-born classes passed through a series of rites: the birth rite (jātakarman), in which the father touches the newborn’s lips with honey and ghee and whispers in its ear; the naming (nāmakaraṇa); the first outing; the first feeding with solid food (annaprāśana); and the tonsure (cūḍākaraṇa), usually in the first or third year. Later texts add a rite for beginning to learn the alphabet (vidyārambha; see below). Initiation followed, and the end of studentship was marked by the first shaving of the beard (keśānta) and the ceremonial bath (samāvartana).

    These rites expressed the idea that a person is formed, not merely born: the child is ritually “made” (saṃskṛta) into a full member of the community in stages, and education is one of those stages. The same idea underlies the use of the word saṃskāra for the impressions that experience leaves in the mind (Chapter 3): in both senses a saṃskāra is a “making” that shapes what a person becomes. Like initiation itself, the full series of rites applied to boys of the twice-born classes; for girls most of the rites were performed without Vedic mantras, and marriage took the place of initiation.

    9.4 Initiation: Upanayana¶

    Vedic study began with the rite of initiation, upanayana, “leading near” — that is, to the teacher. In it the boy was invested with a girdle (mekhalā), a staff and an antelope skin (later, the sacred thread, yajñopavīta), was taught the sāvitrī verse (Ṛgveda 3.62.10, the “Gāyatrī”), and was formally accepted by the teacher. Initiation was regarded as a second birth; hence the three upper classes are called “twice-born” (dvija).

    The texts prescribe initiation in the eighth year (counted from conception) for a Brahmin, the eleventh for a Kṣatriya and the twelfth for a Vaiśya, with outside limits of sixteen, twenty-two and twenty-four years (Manu 2.36–38); the Gṛhyasūtras assign the three classes to spring, summer and autumn respectively. A twice-born man who was not initiated in time became an outcaste (vrātya) unless he performed expiation. Śūdras were not initiated, and by the time of Manu girls were not initiated either: for a woman, Manu says, the marriage ceremony takes the place of initiation, service to her husband takes the place of residence with the teacher, and household work takes the place of tending the sacred fire (Manu 2.67).

    The duration of study was long. The Chāndogya Upaniṣad (6.1.2) tells of Śvetaketu, who went to his teacher at twelve and returned at twenty-four, “having learned all the Vedas”; Manu (3.1) prescribes thirty-six years for the three Vedas, or half or a quarter of that, “or until he has learned them”. These are ideal figures; in practice many students learned only part of the Veda of their own school.

    9.5 What Was Taught¶

    9.5.1 The Veda and Its Auxiliaries¶

    The core of Brahmanical education was the memorisation of the Veda of the student’s own branch (svādhyāya), in the manner described in Chapter 10. Alongside it stood the six “limbs of the Veda” (vedāṅga): phonetics (śikṣā), metre (chandas), grammar (vyākaraṇa), etymology (nirukta), astronomy and calendrics (jyotiṣa) and ritual (kalpa).

    A verse found in the Pāṇinīya Śikṣā pictures the auxiliaries as the limbs of the Veda’s body: metre its feet, ritual its hands, astronomy its eye, etymology its ear, phonetics its nose — and grammar its mouth, the most important of all. The image explains why grammar came to stand at the head of the traditional curriculum.

    9.5.2 Why Study Grammar?¶

    Patañjali opens his “Great Commentary” (Mahābhāṣya, 2nd century BCE) on Pāṇini’s grammar by asking what the purpose of grammar is. He gives five principal reasons, summed up in a mnemonic compound (rakṣohāgamalaghvasandehāḥ): the preservation (rakṣā) of the Veda; the ability to adapt (ūha) Vedic formulas correctly to new ritual contexts; the injunction of scripture (āgama) that a Brahmin should study the Veda with its auxiliaries; economy (laghu), since grammar teaches the language through general rules rather than by listing every word; and the removal of doubt (asandeha) about the correct form of words. The passage includes the warning about the wrongly accented word quoted in Chapter 10, and the remark, attributed to an earlier teacher, that one should not teach the language word by word: even Bṛhaspati, teaching Indra word by word for a thousand divine years, did not reach the end. Grammar’s claim to be the “mouth” of the Veda rested on its power to generalise.

    The study of Pāṇini became the foundation of all advanced Sanskrit learning, Brahmanical, Buddhist and Jain alike; Yijing found it at the start of the Buddhist curriculum at Nālandā (Chapter 11). Its method — a compact rule-system learned by heart and then applied through commentary and practice — set the pattern for much of Indian technical education. On Pāṇini and the grammatical tradition see Volume 10 of this series.

    9.5.3 Lists of Sciences¶

    Later texts list the branches of learning in different ways.

    • The Yājñavalkyasmṛti (1.3) names fourteen “sources of knowledge and law” (vidyāsthāna): the four Vedas, the six Vedāṅgas, and Purāṇa, Nyāya (logic), Mīmāṃsā (Vedic exegesis) and Dharmaśāstra.

    • Later authorities, such as Madhusūdana Sarasvatī in his Prasthānabheda (16th century), add four “secondary Vedas” (upaveda) to make eighteen: Āyurveda (medicine), Dhanurveda (archery and warfare), Gāndharvaveda (music) and Arthaśāstra (polity).

    • The Arthaśāstra (a composite text, c. 50 BCE–300 CE in its present form; (Olivelle 2013)) prescribes for a prince four sciences: the Veda, investigative reasoning (ānvīkṣikī), economics (vārttā) and government (daṇḍanīti).

    An earlier and more varied picture is given in the Chāndogya Upaniṣad (7.1), where Nārada comes to Sanatkumāra for instruction and lists what he has already studied: the four Vedas, the epics and ancient lore as a “fifth Veda”, grammar, the rites for the ancestors, mathematics (rāśi), the science of portents, chronology, logic, ethics, etymology, the sciences of spirits, of weapons, of the stars, and of serpents and celestial beings. All this, Nārada confesses, has made him merely “a knower of texts, not a knower of the self” — a reminder that the Upaniṣads valued one kind of knowledge above all the rest.

    The Kāmasūtra (1.3) lists sixty-four arts (kalā) — music, dance, painting, garland-making, cookery, carpentry, word games, and many more — that the cultured townsman and the accomplished courtesan should know. The list shows the range of refined accomplishments valued in urban society; it does not describe the curriculum of any school.

    9.5.4 Professional Learning¶

    Most practical skills — crafts, trade, agriculture, accountancy, the arts — were learned in families and guilds by apprenticeship, not in the teacher’s household. Medicine is an instructive intermediate case. The Carakasaṃhitā (Vimānasthāna 8) describes how a student should choose a teacher and a teacher a student, and records the address with which the teacher initiates a medical student, enjoining celibacy, truthfulness, obedience and care for patients. The Suśrutasaṃhitā (Sūtrasthāna 9) prescribes practical training for surgeons: incision on gourds, cucumbers and other vegetables; excision on leather bags filled with water or mud; scraping on hides with hair; puncturing veins on the vessels of dead animals and on lotus stalks; and so on. It insists that a student who has learned the texts without practice is like a coward on the battlefield. These passages are among the earliest descriptions of skills training in Indian literature.

    9.6 How It Was Taught¶

    The sources point to a number of characteristic methods.

    • Oral repetition. The teacher recited and the students repeated, phrase by phrase, until the text was fixed in memory (Chapter 10).

    • Exposition and commentary. In the śāstric disciplines, the teacher explained a root text through a commentary, and the student learned to explain it in turn.

    • Question and answer. The Upaniṣads are full of dialogues in which students question teachers — and teachers question students. Uddālaka’s demonstrations to Śvetaketu (Chapter 2) and Prajāpati’s staged instruction of Indra (Chapter 7) are teaching by inquiry.

    • Debate. Formal debate (vāda), with rules of argument codified by the Nyāya school, was central to advanced study and is discussed in Chapter 11.

    • Guided discovery. Varuṇa’s teaching of Bhṛgu in the Taittirīya Upaniṣad (3.1–6), in which the student is given only a definition and sent away to find the answer by his own effort, is the classic example.

    • Example. The student lived in the teacher’s household and learned conduct as much as texts.

    Two Upaniṣadic stories show how varied the teacher–student relationship could be. In the Praśna Upaniṣad, six students come to the sage Pippalāda with questions; he tells them to live with him for another year in austerity, celibacy and faith, and only then to ask — a lesson that readiness must precede instruction. In the Chāndogya Upaniṣad (4.10–15), Upakosala serves his teacher Satyakāma Jābāla for twelve years, but the teacher goes on a journey without having instructed him. Distressed, Upakosala falls ill; the sacred fires he has tended then teach him themselves, and when the teacher returns he notices that the student’s face “shines like that of one who knows Brahman” and completes the teaching. The story suggests both the devotion expected of a student and the possibility of learning from one’s own practice when the teacher is absent.

    No fixed daily timetable survives from antiquity; the timetables printed in some modern books are reconstructions. What the texts prescribe is a day framed by the morning and evening twilight rites (sandhyā), the tending of the fire and the begging round, with study fitted around them, and a calendar with an annual opening (upākaraṇa) and closing (utsarjana) of Vedic study and many days on which recitation was suspended (anadhyāya).

    9.7 Learning a Śāstra¶

    Beyond the Veda, advanced study meant mastering a śāstra, a systematic discipline such as grammar, logic, Mīmāṃsā, Vedānta, medicine, astronomy or poetics. The structure of śāstric literature shaped the way it was taught (Scharfe 2002).

    • The root text. Most disciplines had a root text (mūla), often in the extremely concise form of aphorisms (sūtra) or memorial verses (kārikā). The student learned it by heart. Pāṇini’s grammar, with nearly four thousand sūtras, is the most famous example; its rules are formulated with technical devices — abbreviations, markers, conventions of ordering — that make it compact enough to memorise but impossible to understand without instruction.

    • The commentaries. The root text was explained through layers of commentary: a bhāṣya expounding it, sub-commentaries (vārttika, ṭīkā) explaining the bhāṣya, and so on, sometimes over many centuries. Learning a śāstra meant learning to read this layered literature and, eventually, to write in it.

    • Method of exposition. A traditional analysis lists the elements of explaining a text: dividing it into words, giving their meanings, analysing compounds, construing the sentence, raising objections and answering them. The last two — objection (ākṣepa, pūrvapakṣa) and reply (samādhāna, siddhānta) — made dialectic part of every lesson.

    • Manuals and primers. Introductory works (prakaraṇa) such as the Tarkasaṅgraha in logic or the Laghusiddhāntakaumudī in grammar were written to lead beginners into a discipline, and are still used.

    This pedagogy produced scholars of great precision and the habit of arguing every point from both sides. It also had characteristic limitations: the authority of the root text and its commentators could inhibit innovation, and learning was organised by school and lineage rather than by subject.

    9.7.1 Learning Mathematics¶

    Mathematical astronomy (jyotiṣa) and arithmetic were taught within families of astronomers and in the schools of particular masters, and some mathematical works were written with teaching in mind. Bhāskara II’s Līlāvatī (1150), an arithmetic in verse, addresses many of its problems to a girl — “O Līlāvatī” — in the playful manner of a teacher setting exercises, and was used as a textbook across India for centuries; it was later translated into Persian at Akbar’s court. In sixteenth-century Kerala, Jyeṣṭhadeva’s Yuktibhāṣā, written in Malayalam rather than Sanskrit, set out the reasoning (yukti) behind the results of the Kerala school of mathematics and astronomy, including its infinite series, in a form designed for students. These works show that Indian mathematical pedagogy valued both memorable formulation and explanation of reasons. The history of Indian mathematics is the subject of Volume 11 of this series.

    9.7.2 Learning Medicine¶

    Medical education combined study of the texts with practical training. Besides the exercises on fruits and leather described above, the Suśrutasaṃhitā (Śārīrasthāna 5) describes how a student should learn anatomy by examining a human corpse that has been left to decompose in a cage in a river for seven days and then gradually scraped with brushes of grass and bark, so that the layers of the body can be observed — one of the very few descriptions of systematic dissection in the ancient world. The text insists that a surgeon must know the body both from the texts and by direct observation. Caraka’s instructions on the choice of a teacher and the conduct of discussions (Chapter 11) complete a picture of a learned profession with a deliberate theory of training.

    9.7.3 Learning Music¶

    The classical musical traditions have been transmitted orally from teacher to student within lineages for centuries, and in Hindustani music the lineages crystallised in the nineteenth century into gharānās associated with particular families and styles. A student would live with or near the teacher, serve him, and learn through years of imitation and practice; in some lineages the relationship is sealed by a ceremony of tying a thread on the student’s wrist. The modern institutionalisation of music teaching in colleges and universities, from the early twentieth century, was in part a response to the restrictiveness of the hereditary system. The history of music and its teaching is treated in Volume 9.

    9.8 Other Kinds of Education¶

    9.8.1 The Education of Princes¶

    The Arthaśāstra (1.5) prescribes the education of a prince: after the tonsure ceremony he should learn writing and arithmetic; after initiation, the Veda and investigative reasoning from learned teachers, economics from officials, and government from theoreticians and practitioners. He should spend part of the day on training in elephants, horses, chariots and weapons, and part on hearing the epics and histories, and should keep company with elders to develop discipline. The epics describe the training of princes in archery and the use of weapons by teachers such as Droṇa. These texts show that the education of the ruling class combined literacy, practical administration, military skills and a moral formation through narrative.

    The Arthaśāstra (1.5) also offers a short theory of education. Discipline (vinaya), it says, is of two kinds, innate and acquired; training can discipline only a suitable person, not an unsuitable one, just as work can shape only suitable material. The capacities of a good learner are listed as the desire to hear, hearing, grasping, retaining, understanding, reasoning for and against, and attachment to the truth. The same list of intellectual qualities — wishing to learn, listening, grasping, retaining, reasoning — recurs in many Indian texts as an account of the stages of learning.

    9.8.2 Teaching through Stories: The Pañcatantra¶

    One of the most influential Indian works on education is a collection of animal fables. The Pañcatantra (perhaps 3rd–5th century CE) opens with a frame story: a king despairs of his three sons, who are dull and uninterested in learning, and the Brahmin Viṣṇuśarman undertakes to make them expert in the conduct of affairs (nīti) within six months. He does so by telling them stories, in five books, in which animals act out the dilemmas of friendship, alliance, war and statecraft, and in which maxims in verse comment on the action. The later Hitopadeśa reworks the same material for the same purpose. The frame embodies a pedagogical idea — that practical wisdom is best taught through narrative, to learners who would not respond to abstract instruction — and the collection became one of the most widely translated books of the pre-modern world, passing through Middle Persian and Arabic (as Kalīla wa Dimna) into the languages of Europe. Its view of the world is pragmatic, often cynical, and frankly hierarchical; it teaches prudence and self-interest as much as virtue. On the Pañcatantra as literature see Volume 8 of this series.

    9.8.3 Craft and Apprenticeship¶

    The great majority of skills — weaving, metalwork, carpentry, pottery, sculpture, architecture, trade, agriculture — were learned by apprenticeship, usually within the family or the occupational caste, and sometimes in guilds (śreṇī). The law book of Nārada (perhaps 5th–6th century CE) regulates the relationship between a master and an apprentice (antevāsin) who lives with him to learn a craft: the master must teach him and feed him, may not make him do other work, and may discipline him; the apprentice must stay for the agreed term even if he learns the craft sooner. This model of hereditary and apprenticed learning, rather than schooling, was the normal form of education for most Indians until modern times; its knowledge was largely unwritten, and is treated in Volume 17 of this series.

    9.8.4 Beginning to Read¶

    By the medieval period, a ceremony for beginning to learn the alphabet (vidyārambha or akṣarārambha), in which a small child traces letters in rice or sand, had become widespread, especially in the south, where it is still widely performed. It was not restricted to the twice-born in the way that upanayana was, and it marks the existence of a broader culture of elementary literacy alongside the Vedic schools (Chapter 11).

    9.9 Testing and Recognition¶

    Traditional Indian education had no written examinations, grades or diplomas of the modern kind. Mastery was tested in other ways. A Vedic student’s recitation was checked by his teacher and, in public recitations, by other reciters; a student of a śāstra demonstrated his learning by expounding texts and by debate, first with fellow students and then before assemblies of scholars (pariṣad, sabhā). Reputation was established by success in such assemblies and by the judgement of recognised teachers. In the later period, communities of scholars conferred honorific titles — in Bengal, for example, titles such as Tarkālaṅkāra (“ornament of logic”) or Vidyāsāgara (“ocean of learning”) — and royal courts honoured scholars with gifts and appointments. Tradition reports distinctive local practices, such as an examination in Mithilā in which a needle was pushed at random through a manuscript and the candidate had to explain the passage on the page where it stopped; such reports are difficult to verify but convey the expectation that a scholar must command the whole of a text, not selected parts.

    Monastic institutions had their own procedures. Admission to Nālandā, according to Xuanzang, required success in a debate with the gatekeeper scholars (Chapter 11); Tibetan monastic colleges later developed formal examinations by debate leading to degrees such as geshe. The absence of written examinations should not be taken to mean the absence of rigorous assessment; it meant that assessment was oral, public and dependent on the judgement of masters.

    9.10 Completion: Samāvartana¶

    At the end of studentship the student performed the “return” (samāvartana), marked by a ceremonial bath, after which he was called a snātaka, “one who has bathed”. He offered his teacher a gift (gurudakṣiṇā) and was then expected to marry and become a householder. The Taittirīya Upaniṣad preserves the teacher’s final instruction:

    Taittirīya Upaniṣad 1.11.1

    satyaṃ vada | dharmaṃ cara | svādhyāyān mā pramadaḥ | ācāryāya priyaṃ dhanam āhṛtya prajātantuṃ mā vyavacchetsīḥ |
    Speak the truth. Practise dharma. Do not neglect your recitation. Having brought your teacher a gift that pleases him, do not cut off the line of offspring.

    The passage continues with injunctions not to neglect truth, dharma, welfare, prosperity, recitation and the rites for gods and ancestors, and to treat parents, teacher and guest as gods. It ends with practical advice: when in doubt about conduct, follow the example of Brahmins who are thoughtful, devoted and gentle. It is often called the first “convocation address”; it is better understood as a summary of the duties of a Vedic householder.

    9.11 Life-Stages¶

    The period of studentship was later integrated into the scheme of four life-stages (āśrama): student, householder, forest-dweller and renouncer. Olivelle (1993) has shown that the āśramas were originally alternative ways of life between which an adult could choose; only later were they arranged as successive stages of a single life, and the age-ranges often quoted (“8 to 25”, “25 to 50” and so on) are modern conventions, not classical rules. The scheme applied, in any case, only to twice-born men.

    The Atharvaveda devotes a whole hymn (11.5) to the Vedic student, whom it glorifies as sustaining the world by his discipline:

    Atharvaveda (Śaunaka) 11.5.17

    brahmacaryeṇa tapasā rājā rāṣṭraṃ vi rakṣati |
    ācāryo brahmacaryeṇa brahmacāriṇam icchate ||
    By studentship and austerity the king protects the realm; by studentship the teacher seeks a student.

    9.12 Who Could Learn? Caste, Gender and Exclusion¶

    Any account of the teacher’s household must face the fact that it was closed to most people. The restriction was not incidental; the normative texts state it repeatedly and sometimes violently.

    9.12.1 Śūdras¶

    The Dharmasūtras and Manu define the Śūdra as “once-born” (ekajāti), without initiation (Manu 10.4), and exclude him from Vedic study. The Gautama Dharmasūtra prescribes that if a Śūdra listens to a recitation of the Veda his ears are to be filled with molten tin or lac; if he recites it, his tongue is to be cut out; if he commits it to memory, his body is to be split apart (Gautama 12.4–6). Manu forbids giving a Śūdra advice or teaching him the law (Manu 4.80). Śaṅkara, as noted in Chapter 4, cites the Gautama rule in his commentary on Brahmasūtra 1.3.38. Whether such punishments were ever carried out is unknown; their importance lies in what they reveal about the ideology of knowledge as a hereditary possession of the twice-born.

    The epics dramatise the exclusion. In the Mahābhārata (1.123 in the critical edition), the Niṣāda prince Ekalavya, refused as a pupil by the archery teacher Droṇa, teaches himself before a clay image of Droṇa and becomes the finest archer in the land; Droṇa, to protect the pre-eminence of his Kṣatriya pupil Arjuna, demands as his fee Ekalavya’s right thumb, which Ekalavya cuts off and gives him. In the Uttarakāṇḍa of the Rāmāyaṇa (7.64–67 in the critical edition), Rāma beheads the Śūdra Śambūka for practising austerities, an act forbidden to his class, and a Brahmin boy who had died is restored to life. Both stories have been retold, criticised and reinterpreted for centuries; in modern times they became central texts for anti-caste thinkers.

    The tradition also preserves counter-currents. The Chāndogya Upaniṣad (4.4) tells of Satyakāma Jābāla, who does not know his father’s lineage and is accepted as a student by Hāridrumata Gautama because he tells the truth about it — though the teacher concludes that only a Brahmin could speak so honestly. The Aitareya Brāhmaṇa (2.19) tells of Kavaṣa Ailūṣa, the son of a slave woman, driven from a sacrifice but then recognised as a seer. The Mahābhārata gives important teaching roles to Vidura, born of a servant woman, and to the meat-seller of Mithilā who instructs a Brahmin in dharma. The Buddhist and Jain monastic orders admitted members of all castes (Chapter 11), and the Buddhist Vajrasūcī attacked the idea of birth-based superiority with arguments drawn from the Brahmanical texts themselves. The devotional poets of the second millennium — among them Nandanār, Cokhāmeḷā, Ravidās and Kabīr — were themselves from excluded or low-ranked communities and composed in the regional languages, outside the Sanskrit curriculum.

    9.12.2 Women¶

    The evidence on women’s education is mixed and changes over time. Several hymns of the Ṛgveda are attributed by the later index (Anukramaṇī) to women seers such as Ghoṣā, Apālā and Lopāmudrā. In the Bṛhadāraṇyaka Upaniṣad Gārgī Vācaknavī twice questions Yājñavalkya in a public debate (3.6, 3.8), and Maitreyī receives his teaching on the self (2.4, 4.5). In the Mahābhārata (12.308 in the critical edition) the female ascetic Sulabhā debates King Janaka and exposes his pretensions to liberation. The grammatical tradition, commenting on Pāṇini 4.1.49, distinguishes ācāryā, a woman who is herself a teacher, from ācāryāṇī, the wife of a teacher. A passage attributed to the lawgiver Hārīta and quoted in medieval digests distinguishes “women who speak of Brahman” (brahmavādinī), who were initiated and studied, from women who married directly.

    But the normative texts from about the turn of the common era exclude women from initiation (Manu 2.67) and declare that rites for women are performed without Vedic mantras (Manu 9.18). Women’s access to Sanskrit learning thereafter depended on family circumstances, and was most often found among royal and courtly women, in families of scholars, and among nuns in the Buddhist and Jain orders. Feminist historians have analysed how the control of women’s education was bound up with the control of their sexuality and with the maintenance of caste boundaries (Chakravarti 2003).

    9.12.3 Modern Critiques¶

    From the nineteenth century the exclusion of Śūdras, Dalits and women from education became a central issue of Indian social reform. Jotirao Phule (1827–1890) and Savitribai Phule (1831–1897) opened a school for girls in Pune in 1848 and later schools for children of the “untouchable” castes; Jotirao’s Gulāmgirī (1873) attacked Brahmin control of knowledge, and in 1882 he submitted a memorial to the Education Commission demanding universal primary education (O’Hanlon 1985). B. R. Ambedkar (1891–1956) analysed the Dharmaśāstra rules on Śūdras and argued that the denial of education had been one of the principal instruments of caste domination; his Annihilation of Caste (1936) remains the classic statement (Ambedkar 1936). These critiques are part of the history of Indian education and must be read alongside the texts that praise the teacher’s household.

    9.13 The Legacy¶

    The teacher’s household as an ideal has had a long afterlife. Traditional Vedic schools (pāṭhaśālās) continue to train students in recitation; Sanskrit śāstras are still taught by traditional teachers in some institutions. In the colonial period the ideal was revived in new forms: the Arya Samaj founded the Gurukul Kangri near Haridwar in 1902 as a residential school combining Vedic study with modern subjects; Rabindranath Tagore founded a school at Santiniketan in 1901, which grew into Visva-Bharati (1921), drawing on the image of the forest hermitage while rejecting its caste restrictions. The National Education Policy of 2020 invokes the tradition in general terms (Chapter 13).

    What can fairly be claimed for the model is an emphasis on close, long-term relationships between teacher and student, on learning by heart and by example, and on education as the formation of character as well as the transmission of knowledge. What must also be acknowledged is that, as described in the normative texts, it was available to few, hierarchical in its relations, and bound up with an ideology of birth.
