    Preface¶

    This is Volume 22 of the series Bhāratīya Jñāna Paramparā: Manas and Śikṣā: Psychology and Education. It brings together two subjects that the Indian sources often treat together: the analysis of the mind — consciousness, perception, memory, emotion, attention, dreams and sleep — in the Upaniṣads, Sāṃkhya and Yoga, Nyāya, the Jain and Buddhist traditions, Vedānta, the medical texts, the theory of rasa, the Sufi masters and the Sikh Gurus; and the history of teaching and learning in India, from the Vedic teacher’s household and the oral transmission of the Veda to the Buddhist monasteries, the Brahmanical and Jain colleges and libraries, the madrasas, the village schools, the first schools for girls and the colonial and modern periods.

    Dates are given as the range accepted in current scholarship, with traditional dates labelled as such. The Indian accounts of the mind are presented in their own terms, not as “anticipations” or “proofs” of modern neuroscience and psychology; where modern research on practices such as meditation and yoga is discussed, its results are reported with their limitations and with attention to possible harms. Popular myths about Indian education — Takṣaśilā as “the world’s first university”, the “nine million manuscripts” of Nālandā — are corrected. The volume also tries to give an honest account of who was excluded from learning, and of the women, Buddhist, Jain, Islamic and anti-caste traditions that are often left out of celebratory histories.

    The book has no formal parts. Chapters 2 to 8 treat the mind, including a section on the Tamil accounts of inner life and one on what the classical theories said about women’s minds; Chapters 9 to 12 follow the history of learning from the Vedic teacher’s household and the oral tradition to the monasteries, colleges, madrasas and village schools and the manuscript tradition; and Chapter 13 carries the story through the colonial period to modern psychology and education.

    References to standard editions, translations and studies are given in the text and collected in the select bibliography; a glossary, a chronology, a list of the editions of the principal texts and an index of quoted passages are provided at the end. No survey of this size can be exhaustive, and this one is weighted towards the classical Sanskrit and Pali sources. Readers should treat it as an introduction, turn to the works cited for the texts themselves, and check every quotation against a critical edition before scholarly use.

    Nikhil Kumar Rajput
    Ramanujan College, University of Delhi
    Guru Pūrṇimā, 2026
