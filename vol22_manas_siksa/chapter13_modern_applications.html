    Chapter 13: Colonial and Modern India: Education and Psychology¶

    13.1 Introduction¶

    The traditions described in this volume met, from the late eighteenth century, a colonial state, Christian missions, the printing press and a new kind of school. They did not end with that encounter. Some continue in their traditional forms — Vedic schools, Buddhist and Jain monastic education, Sanskrit and Islamic scholarship. Others have been transformed and transplanted: meditation practices derived from Buddhism and Yoga are now used in hospitals, schools and workplaces across the world; Indian philosophical ideas about the mind have entered academic psychology; and Indian educational ideals have been invoked by reformers from Tagore and Gandhi to the authors of the National Education Policy of 2020.

    This chapter first completes the history of learning begun in Chapters 9–12, with the colonial surveys, the first modern colleges and the schooling of girls between 1820 and 1930, and then surveys the modern developments. It has two aims: to describe what has been done with the traditions, and to say clearly what the evidence does and does not support. Enthusiasm for “ancient wisdom” has often outrun the evidence, and some claims made in its name are untrue. A tradition is better served by accurate claims than by inflated ones.

    13.2 Colonial Encounters, 1780–1930¶

    The institutions described in Chapter 11 were still the main providers of learning in India at the end of the eighteenth century. The century and a half that followed brought missionary schools, colonial colleges and universities, the first statistical surveys of indigenous schooling, and the first schools for girls. This section and the next follow those changes; the rest of the chapter considers the responses of Indian educators and the modern afterlife of the traditions described in this volume.

    13.2.1 Missionaries and the First Modern Colleges¶

    From the late eighteenth century, Christian missionaries founded schools that taught in the regional languages and in English, printed textbooks and grammars, and opened schools for girls and for low-caste children, groups that most indigenous schools excluded. The Baptist missionaries of Serampore near Calcutta founded Serampore College in 1818. In Calcutta, Indian reformers and Europeans together founded the Hindu College in 1817 to teach Western learning in English; the colonial government established Sanskrit colleges in Vārāṇasī (1791) and Calcutta (1824) and a madrasa in Calcutta (1781). These institutions mark the beginning of the transition from the older forms of learning to the colonial and modern systems discussed in the rest of this chapter.

    13.2.2 The Colonial Surveys and Their Interpretation¶

    In the 1820s and 1830s the East India Company’s administrators carried out the first systematic surveys of indigenous education.

    • In the Madras Presidency, Governor Thomas Munro ordered district collectors in 1822 to report on the schools in their districts; the returns, compiled by 1825, list schools and scholars by district and record the castes of the pupils. In his minute of 1826 Munro drew from them the conclusion that a substantial minority of boys, though far from all, received some schooling.

    • In the Bombay Presidency, similar enquiries were made in the 1820s.

    • In Bengal and Bihar, the missionary William Adam produced three reports (1835, 1836 and 1838). His first report, based on earlier estimates, suggested that there might be about 100,000 village schools in Bengal and Bihar; his detailed district surveys in the later reports gave a more varied and generally less favourable picture, and recorded very few girls in any school (Adam 1941).

    • In the Punjab, G. W. Leitner’s History of Indigenous Education in the Panjab (1882) argued that indigenous schooling had declined sharply under British rule (Leitner 1882).

    These materials were brought to wide attention by Dharampal’s The Beautiful Tree (1983), whose title comes from Gandhi’s remark at Chatham House in London in 1931 that the British had uprooted the “beautiful tree” of Indian education. Dharampal argued, using especially the Madras returns, that schooling in early nineteenth-century India was widespread and that Śūdra and other non-Brahmin castes made up a large share of the pupils in some districts — a picture that challenges both the colonial claim of an illiterate India and the assumption that Brahmanical exclusion governed all education (Dharampal 1983).

    Dharampal’s work is valuable and its documents are genuine, but its conclusions have limits that should be stated.

    1. The returns were compiled by different officials with different methods; their figures for “schools” and “scholars” are not always comparable, and some are estimates.

    2. The schools concerned were overwhelmingly elementary schools teaching reading, writing and accounts. The presence of Śūdra pupils in them does not show that Vedic or śāstric learning was open to them; it was not.

    3. Girls are almost absent from the returns, except for a few, mostly from particular communities, and Dalit (“untouchable”) castes are scarcely represented.

    4. The thesis that British rule destroyed a flourishing system is contested. Some decline in indigenous schools in the nineteenth century is well attested, but its causes included economic change and the withdrawal of patronage as well as government policy; and the overall literacy rate recorded by the first census in 1881 was low. The educationist Philip Hartog disputed Gandhi’s claim in 1939 (Hartog 1939), and the debate continues.

    The colonial schools did not automatically open education to those whom the indigenous system had excluded. Government schools were in principle open to all, but upper-caste parents and teachers often resisted the admission of “untouchable” children, and officials frequently gave way. A well-known case arose in the Bombay Presidency in 1856, when a boy of the Mahar caste was refused admission to a government school in Dharwar; such cases led the authorities to affirm that schools maintained from public funds were in principle open to all castes, even as they acknowledged the practical difficulties. In practice, much of the early education of Dalit children was provided by missionaries and, later, by Dalit and anti-caste organisations themselves. The Education Commission of 1882 recorded the continuing exclusion of low-caste children and recommended measures to include them. The history of access to education in colonial India is thus not simply one of “Western” opening against “traditional” closure; caste exclusion was reproduced within the new institutions and had to be fought there as well.

    Colonial policy turned decisively towards English-medium higher education in the 1830s. Thomas Babington Macaulay’s Minute on Indian Education (2 February 1835) asserted that “a single shelf of a good European library was worth the whole native literature of India and Arabia” — a genuine and notorious sentence. (A different “speech” attributed to Macaulay, describing an India without beggars or thieves, circulates widely but is a modern fabrication.) Wood’s Despatch of 1854 led to the founding of the universities of Calcutta, Bombay and Madras in 1857, and the Education Commission chaired by W. W. Hunter (1882) reviewed primary education. The history of modern Indian education, and of the nationalist and anti-caste responses to it, is outlined in Section 13.4 (Nurullah and Naik 1951; Kumar 1991).

    13.3 Schooling Girls, 1820–1930¶

    The learned women of earlier centuries (Section 11.14) were exceptional, and most of them learned at home or in religious communities. The great expansion of women’s education in the nineteenth and twentieth centuries depended on new institutions: schools for girls, women teachers, journals and books written for women readers. Between about 1820 and 1930 these institutions were created against strong resistance, by missionaries, colonial officials, Indian reformers of several religions and, increasingly, by women themselves. Geraldine Forbes’s Women in Modern India is the standard survey (Forbes 1996).

    13.3.1 Missions, Reformers and the Home¶

    The first schools for girls in the modern sense were opened by Protestant missionaries in Calcutta, Bombay and the Madras Presidency from the late 1810s. Mary Ann Cooke, who began work in Calcutta in 1821, is the best-known of the women teachers. The mission schools drew most of their pupils from poor and low-caste families; respectable Hindu and Muslim households kept their daughters away, fearing conversion and loss of status. A belief, widely reported in the period, that a girl who learned to read would become a widow expressed the deeper fear that literacy would make wives independent (Borthwick 1984). Some Indian reformers argued from the other side. A Bengali tract published in 1822 with the support of the Calcutta Hindu leader Radhakanta Deb cited learned women of the past to show that women’s education was in keeping with Hindu tradition.

    A secular school for the daughters of respectable Hindu families was opened in Calcutta in 1849 by J. E. D. Bethune, then law member of the governor-general’s council, with the support of Bengali reformers. Ishvarchandra Vidyasagar, who served as its secretary, went on to found several dozen girls’ schools in the districts of Bengal in the late 1850s. Where seclusion kept girls and women at home, teaching came to them. From the 1850s women missionaries and, later, Indian women teachers visited the women’s quarters (zenana, antaḥpur) of middle-class homes to teach reading, needlework and, in the missionaries’ case, scripture. Bengali reformers published journals such as the Bāmābodhinī Patrikā (from 1863), which set lessons and examinations for women studying at home.

    The autobiography of Rassundari Devi, Āmār Jīban (“My Life”), published in 1876, shows what learning could mean for a woman whom no school reached. Married as a child into a village household, she taught herself to read in secret, as an adult, by matching the letters of a page of a devotional book she had hidden in the kitchen with the sounds she heard in her son’s lessons. Her wish was to read the religious books for herself. Tanika Sarkar has shown how her account makes reading an act of devotion and, at the same time, of self-assertion against the rules of the household (Sarkar 1999). Malavika Karlekar’s study of early Bengali women’s narratives shows how often the desire to read appears in them, and how often it was resisted (Karlekar 1991).

    13.3.2 Schools Founded by Reformers¶

    In western India the Phules’ school of 1848 (Section 9.12) was followed by further schools for girls and, from the early 1850s, for children of the Mahar and Māṅg castes. They were supported by a committee of local sympathisers and taught by Savitribai Phule and a small number of colleagues, in the face of open hostility. Their curriculum followed that of the government vernacular schools; their significance lay in whom they taught (O’Hanlon 1985). Pandita Ramabai’s evidence to the Education Commission of 1882, urging the training of women as teachers and doctors, and her schools for widows, carried the argument further. In 1896 D. K. Karve opened a home for widows near Pune, out of which grew the Indian Women’s University (1916), later the SNDT Women’s University: an institution whose founder held that women should be taught in their own languages and in subjects suited, as he saw it, to their lives.

    In the Panjab the Arya Samaj founded the Kanya Mahavidyalaya at Jalandhar, which by the 1890s had become a pioneering institution for girls’ secondary education. Madhu Kishwar’s study of it shows the tensions within the reform: the Samaj wanted educated women who would be learned in Sanskrit and Vedic religion, but also obedient wives and mothers, and its leaders disagreed over how far girls’ education should go (Kishwar 1986). At Ferozepur Bhāī Takht Singh opened a small school for Sikh girls in the 1890s; with the help of his wife Harnām Kaur it grew, after a boarding house was added in 1904, into the Sikh Kanya Mahavidyalaya, which served Sikh girls within the Singh Sabha movement, teaching Gurmukhī, the scripture and household skills, and training women teachers (Jakobsh 2003).

    13.3.3 Muslim Women’s Education¶

    Gail Minault’s Secluded Scholars traces the parallel history among north Indian Muslims (Minault 1998). The starting point was education at home. Daughters of the elite (sharīf) families were taught to read the Qurʾān, and sometimes Persian and Urdu, by women teachers (ustānī) or by relatives. Reformers wanted to extend and redirect this education. Nazir Ahmad’s Urdu novel Mirʾāt al-ʿArūs (“The Bride’s Mirror”, 1869) contrasted an educated, capable housewife with her spoilt and ignorant sister, and became the model for a whole literature of instruction for women. Maulānā Ashraf ʿAlī Thānawī’s Bihishtī Zewar (“Ornaments of Paradise”, early twentieth century) was written as a complete curriculum for women at home. It covers letter-writing, accounts, household management, medicine, the religious law of prayer and ritual purity, and in its later parts the discipline of the self in the tradition of Sufi ethics. Barbara Metcalf, its translator, points out that it assumed women capable of the same religious and moral education as men (Metcalf 1990).

    Schools followed. In Lahore Sayyid Mumtāz ʿAlī and his wife Muḥammadī Begum founded the women’s journal Tahzīb-i Niswān in 1898, and in Aligarh Shaikh ʿAbdullāh and his wife Wahīd Jahān Begum opened a girls’ school in 1906, from which the Aligarh women’s college grew. The rulers of Bhopal, a state governed by women for much of the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, were patrons of girls’ schools. In Bengal Rokeya Sakhawat Hossain opened a school for Muslim girls at Bhagalpur in 1909 and moved it to Calcutta in 1911. She went from house to house to persuade parents to send their daughters, and accommodated the demands of seclusion while opposing its extremes. Her ideas are treated in Volume 24.

    13.3.4 What Kind of Education?¶

    Across these communities the same question recurred: should girls learn what boys learned, or an education designed for wives and mothers? Most reformers, Hindu, Muslim and Sikh, chose the second. They justified women’s education as the making of better companions for educated husbands and better first teachers for sons, and they put religion, domestic skills and the vernacular at the centre. Historians have shown how this ideal both opened education to women and confined it (Forbes 1996; Chakravarti 2003). Women who wanted more had to argue for it: for English, for the sciences, for medicine and for university degrees. Some won it against strong opposition. Pandita Ramabai was awarded the title “Paṇḍitā” by scholars in Calcutta in 1878 for her Sanskrit learning; in 1883 Kadambini Ganguly and Chandramukhi Basu became the first women graduates of the University of Calcutta; and in 1886 Anandibai Joshi completed a medical degree in Philadelphia. By 1930 girls’ schooling had become respectable among the educated classes of the towns, but the great majority of Indian women still could not read, and the expansion of women’s education belongs mostly to the decades after independence (Section 13.5).

    13.4 Modern Indian Education: Colonial Rule and Its Critics¶

    The colonial reorganisation of education, described in Section 13.2, created a system of English-medium higher education, government and missionary schools, and examinations, which displaced or marginalised the indigenous institutions. Indian responses took several forms (Nurullah and Naik 1951; Kumar 1991).

    • Reformist and religious foundations. The Arya Samaj founded Dayanand Anglo-Vedic schools (from 1886) and the Gurukul Kangri (1902); the Aligarh movement founded the Muhammadan Anglo-Oriental College (1875–77); the Deoband seminary (1866) reorganised Islamic education; Christian missions ran many of the earliest schools for girls and for lower castes.

    • National education. During the Swadeshi movement a National Council of Education was established in Bengal (1906). During the non-cooperation movement, institutions such as the Gujarat Vidyapith (1920), Jamia Millia Islamia (1920) and the Kashi Vidyapith (1921) were founded as alternatives to government colleges.

    • Educational philosophies. Tagore, Vivekananda, Aurobindo, Gandhi and Ambedkar each proposed an alternative to colonial schooling; their ideas are outlined below.

    • Education against caste and patriarchy. The schools of the Phules, Pandita Ramabai’s Sharada Sadan for widows (1889) and Rokeya Sakhawat Hossain’s school for Muslim girls (Section 13.3) opened education to those whom both the traditional and the colonial systems had largely excluded.

    13.4.1 Five Educational Thinkers¶

    The educational ideas of five modern thinkers have been especially influential in debates about an “Indian” education.

    Rabindranath Tagore (1861–1941), who had hated his own schooling, founded his school at Santiniketan in 1901 and Visva-Bharati in 1921. He wanted children to learn in the open air, in close contact with nature, through music, art, drama and crafts as well as books, and in their mother tongue; he wanted the university to be a meeting place of cultures, “where the world makes a home in a single nest”. His model drew on the image of the ancient forest hermitage but rejected its restrictions of caste and gender: girls and boys of all backgrounds studied at Santiniketan.

    Swami Vivekananda (1863–1902), though not an educational theorist in the strict sense, gave the modern Indian discussion one of its most quoted definitions: “Education is the manifestation of the perfection already in man.” He criticised colonial schooling as a mere cramming of information, called for an education that would build character and self-reliance, and insisted on the education of women and of the poor and low-caste masses, whom he saw as having been neglected by both the traditional and the colonial systems. The Ramakrishna Mission, which he founded, became a major provider of schools and colleges.

    Sri Aurobindo (1872–1950) set out, in his early essays on national education, three principles of teaching. The first, he wrote, is that “nothing can be taught”: the teacher is not an instructor or taskmaster but a helper and guide, who suggests rather than imposes. The second is that the mind should be consulted in its own growth; the third, that education should work from the near to the far, from what is to what shall be. His ideas, developed with Mirra Alfassa (“the Mother”), shaped the school of his ashram in Pondicherry.

    Mohandas Gandhi (1869–1948) proposed in 1937 a scheme of “Basic Education” (Nai Talīm) in which all learning would be organised around a productive craft, such as spinning and weaving, taught in the mother tongue, and the school would be largely self-supporting. The scheme, adopted with modifications by several provincial governments after the Wardha conference of 1937, aimed to unite manual and intellectual work and to dissolve the division between those who labour and those who learn — a division which, in India, coincided with caste.

    B. R. Ambedkar (1891–1956) saw education as the indispensable means of emancipation for the Dalits and other oppressed groups. His own education — obtained against great obstacles, and culminating in doctorates from Columbia University and the University of London — made him the most highly educated political leader of his generation. He founded the People’s Education Society (1945), whose colleges in Bombay and Aurangabad served Dalit students, campaigned for scholarships and hostels for Dalit students, and, as chairman of the drafting committee of the Constitution, helped to make equality of opportunity a constitutional principle.

    These five thinkers disagreed sharply on many questions — Ambedkar was a severe critic of Gandhi, and Tagore of Gandhi’s emphasis on spinning — but together they show that modern Indian educational thought drew on traditional ideals in very different ways, and that the critique of tradition was as important as its revival.

    Independent India committed itself in its Constitution to free and compulsory education for children, a goal reaffirmed by the Education Commission of 1964–66 and made a legal right by the Right of Children to Free and Compulsory Education Act of 2009. The expansion of education since 1947, and the reservation of places for Scheduled Castes, Scheduled Tribes and other disadvantaged groups, have transformed access to learning on a scale without precedent in Indian history, though large inequalities remain.

    13.5 Access to Education Today¶

    The scale of change since independence is best seen in literacy figures. The census of 1951 recorded a literacy rate of about 18 per cent; the census of 2011 recorded about 74 per cent, with a substantial gap between men (about 82 per cent) and women (about 65 per cent), and further gaps between regions, between town and country, and between social groups. School enrolment is now close to universal at the primary level, and India’s higher education system is among the largest in the world. These achievements are recent: for most of Indian history, as the preceding chapters have shown, literacy and advanced learning were the possession of a small minority. The persistence of inequalities of caste, gender, region and class in access to good education is the modern form of an old problem.

    Language is a recurrent question. The Education Commission of 1964–66 recommended a “three-language formula” — the regional language, Hindi and English — and later policies have repeatedly endorsed instruction in the mother tongue in the early years. In practice, English-medium education has expanded rapidly because of its perceived economic value, raising difficult questions about how to combine access to English with education in the languages that children speak at home.

    13.6 The National Education Policy 2020¶

    The National Education Policy (NEP) of 2020 invokes India’s educational heritage — it mentions Takṣaśilā, Nālandā, Vikramaśilā and Valabhī and the ideals of holistic and multidisciplinary learning — and proposes, among other measures, instruction in the home language or mother tongue in the early years wherever possible, a more flexible and multidisciplinary curriculum, the integration of vocational education, and the inclusion of “Indian Knowledge Systems” in school and university curricula. A division for Indian Knowledge Systems was set up under the Ministry of Education in 2020 to promote research and teaching in the field.

    These initiatives have been welcomed by many as a long-overdue recognition of India’s intellectual traditions, and criticised by others for the risk that heritage will be presented uncritically, that myths will enter textbooks, and that the exclusions of the past will be passed over. The present volume is offered in the spirit of the first view and with the cautions of the second. Teaching about Indian knowledge systems is most valuable when it presents the sources accurately, distinguishes evidence from legend, and includes the voices of those who were excluded.

    13.7 Psychology in India¶

    Academic psychology in India began in the colonial universities. A department of experimental psychology was established at the University of Calcutta in 1915–16 under N. N. Sengupta, and Girindrasekhar Bose founded the Indian Psychoanalytical Society in 1922, corresponding with Freud about his own theory of opposite wishes. Christiane Hartnack’s history of psychoanalysis in colonial India shows how differently the new discipline was taken up by Bose and his Bengali colleagues, who read it alongside Indian philosophical and medical ideas, and by British psychoanalysts working in India, some of whom used it to describe the colonised as immature (Hartnack 2001). For most of the twentieth century Indian psychology largely followed Western models. From the 1970s onwards, psychologists such as Durganand Sinha called for “indigenous” psychologies grounded in Indian social realities, and others — including Anand Paranjpe, K. Ramakrishna Rao and the contributors to the Handbook of Indian Psychology (Rao et al. 2008) — have tried to develop psychological theory from Indian philosophical sources. The psychoanalyst Sudhir Kakar has explored Indian childhood, healing and religious experience in psychoanalytic terms (Kakar 1982).

    Indian psychologists and social scientists have also used psychological concepts to analyse Indian society and history. Ashis Nandy’s The Intimate Enemy (1983), for example, studies the psychological effects of colonialism on both colonised and colonisers, and Kakar’s The Inner World (1978) offers a psychoanalytic account of Indian childhood and family life (Nandy 1983; Kakar 1978). These works use Western theories reflexively, to understand Indian experience, rather than claiming that Indian traditions anticipated them.

    A related line of research has asked whether Indians understand the self differently from people in the West. The psychoanalyst Alan Roland, working with patients in India and Japan, described a “familial self” in which identity is experienced largely through relationships, alongside a highly private inner “spiritual self” (Roland 1988); cross-cultural psychologists have contrasted “interdependent” and “independent” self-construals. Such generalisations are useful as starting points but are easily overstated: Indian society includes enormous variation by region, class, caste, religion, gender and generation, and the classical Indian philosophies of the self, discussed in Chapters 2–8, are not descriptions of how ordinary Indians experience themselves.

    This “Indian psychology” movement has produced valuable work, especially on the self, on contemplative practice and on culturally specific forms of distress and healing. It has also been criticised for treating diverse traditions as a single “Indian” view, for relying on the prestige of ancient texts rather than on evidence, and for paying little attention to caste and gender. The most persuasive work in the field combines careful reading of the sources with ordinary standards of empirical and philosophical argument (Paranjpe 1998).

    13.8 Indian Ideas in Western Psychology¶

    Western psychologists encountered Indian ideas from the late nineteenth century. William James, in The Varieties of Religious Experience (1902), discussed yoga and quoted Swami Vivekananda on Vedānta as examples of mystical experience. Carl Jung was deeply interested in Indian thought; in 1932 he gave a seminar in Zurich on the psychology of kuṇḍalinī yoga, interpreting the cakras as symbols of stages in the development of the psyche — an interpretation that tells us more about Jung’s psychology than about the Tantric texts. In the 1960s and 1970s the humanistic and “transpersonal” psychologies drew on Indian and Buddhist ideas of higher states of consciousness, often in highly selective ways; and from the late 1970s the clinical adaptation of mindfulness began.

    Two features of this reception should be noted. First, it was largely mediated by modern Indian teachers — Vivekananda, Paramahansa Yogananda, Maharishi Mahesh Yogi, the Tibetan and Burmese meditation masters — who themselves presented the traditions in modernised, universalised forms. Secondly, Western interest tended to focus on meditation, altered states and the self, and to ignore the rest of the Indian analyses of the mind: logic and epistemology, the Abhidharma’s ethics, the medical and aesthetic theories. The picture of “Eastern psychology” that resulted is narrower than the traditions themselves. Evan Thompson has criticised one of its consequences, the claim that Buddhism alone among religions is a “science of the mind” (Thompson 2020).

    13.9 Meditation and Mindfulness in Clinical Practice¶

    13.9.1 From Monastery to Clinic¶

    In 1979 Jon Kabat-Zinn founded a programme of Mindfulness-Based Stress Reduction (MBSR) at the University of Massachusetts Medical School, adapting meditation practices from Buddhist traditions into an eight-week secular course for patients with chronic pain and stress-related conditions (Kabat-Zinn 1990). In the 1990s Zindel Segal, Mark Williams and John Teasdale combined MBSR with elements of cognitive therapy in Mindfulness-Based Cognitive Therapy (MBCT), designed to prevent relapse in people who had recovered from recurrent depression. Other therapies, such as Dialectical Behaviour Therapy and Acceptance and Commitment Therapy, incorporated mindfulness exercises. In the process, sati (Chapter 5) was redefined as “paying attention in a particular way: on purpose, in the present moment, and non-judgementally” — a definition that is useful clinically but differs from the meaning of the term in the Pali texts, where mindfulness is inseparable from ethical discrimination.

    13.9.2 What the Evidence Shows¶

    Research on these programmes has grown enormously. A balanced summary is as follows.

    • A systematic review and meta-analysis commissioned by the US Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality found moderate evidence that meditation programmes improve anxiety, depression and pain, compared with non-specific control conditions, and low or insufficient evidence for many other outcomes; it found no evidence that meditation was better than active treatments such as medication or exercise (Goyal et al. 2014).

    • An individual-patient meta-analysis of randomised trials found that MBCT reduced the risk of depressive relapse over sixty weeks compared with usual care, and was at least comparable with maintenance antidepressant medication (Kuyken et al. 2016). MBCT is recommended in some national clinical guidelines for people with recurrent depression.

    • Meta-analyses of mindfulness-based therapies report moderate effects on anxiety and mood symptoms (Hofmann et al. 2010); effects are smaller when the comparison is with an active treatment rather than with a waiting list or usual care (Goyal et al. 2014).

    • A review of forty-four meta-analyses of randomised trials of mindfulness-based interventions found them consistently more effective than passive controls and often comparable to established active treatments, while noting the modest quality of much of the underlying evidence (Goldberg et al. 2022).

    • Leading researchers have warned that the field has suffered from exaggerated claims, inconsistent definitions of “mindfulness”, small samples, inadequate control groups and over-interpretation of brain-imaging results (Van Dam et al. 2018).

    13.9.3 Meditation and the Brain¶

    Neuroscientific studies have reported that long-term practitioners show patterns of brain activity and structure that differ from those of non-practitioners: for example, high-amplitude gamma-band synchrony during compassion meditation in a small group of experienced Tibetan Buddhist practitioners (Lutz et al. 2004), and greater cortical thickness in some brain regions in a group of experienced Western meditators (Lazar et al. 2005). Such findings are interesting, but most early studies were small and cross-sectional, and some have not been consistently replicated. They show that meditation, like other intensive practices, is associated with measurable changes; they do not show that the doctrines of the traditions from which the practices come — about the self, consciousness or liberation — are true, and they should not be described as “proving” ancient teachings.

    13.9.4 Adverse Effects¶

    Meditation is not risk-free. A qualitative study of Western Buddhist meditators documented a range of challenging experiences, some severe and long-lasting, including anxiety, panic, depersonalisation and re-experiencing of trauma (Lindahl et al. 2017), and a systematic review of meditation studies found that adverse events were reported in a non-trivial proportion of participants, most often anxiety and depressive symptoms (Farias et al. 2020). Traditional teachers were aware that intensive practice could disturb the mind — the literature on “meditation sickness” in several traditions shows this — and generally taught meditation within a framework of ethical preparation and close supervision.

    Health and Safety Note

    Mindfulness and meditation programmes can be helpful for some people, but they are not a cure for mental illness and are not suitable for everyone. People with a history of psychosis, severe trauma, or current severe depression should practise intensive meditation only under the guidance of a qualified clinician. Anyone who experiences distressing symptoms during or after meditation should stop and seek professional advice. Meditation should not replace evidence-based treatment.

    13.10 Yoga and Mental Health¶

    Modern postural yoga, with breathing exercises and relaxation, is widely practised for health. Systematic reviews have found some evidence that yoga reduces symptoms of depression in the short term, but the trials are often small and at high risk of bias (Cramer et al. 2013). Indian research centres, notably the National Institute of Mental Health and Neurosciences (NIMHANS) in Bengaluru, have conducted trials of yoga as an addition to standard treatment for conditions such as depression and schizophrenia, with encouraging but not conclusive results. Breathing practices such as those taught in Sudarshan Kriya Yoga have been studied in small trials. On present evidence, yoga can reasonably be offered as a complement to, not a substitute for, standard care. The history of yoga and its modern transformations is treated in Volume 5 of this series.

    The modern relaxation practice called “Yoga Nidra” (Chapter 7) and its clinical adaptation iRest have been used with military veterans and in pain and palliative care. Pilot studies have reported improvements in sleep, anxiety and post-traumatic symptoms, but these studies are small and mostly uncontrolled; the practice cannot yet be described as a validated treatment for post-traumatic stress disorder or any other condition.

    13.10.1 Modern Postural Yoga¶

    The “yoga” now practised worldwide is largely a twentieth-century creation, though it draws on older Haṭha Yoga techniques. Teachers such as T. Krishnamacharya (1888–1989) at the Mysore palace and his students B. K. S. Iyengar and K. Pattabhi Jois developed sequences of postures influenced by Indian wrestling and gymnastics and by contemporary physical culture, while Swami Kuvalayananda (1883–1966) at Lonavala began to study yogic practices with the methods of physiology from the 1920s. Elizabeth De Michelis, Joseph Alter and Mark Singleton have shown how modern yoga emerged from this blend of traditional practice, nationalist physical culture and international fitness movements (De Michelis 2004; Alter 2004; Singleton 2010). Recognising this history does not diminish modern yoga; it explains why its health claims must be assessed by modern methods rather than by appeal to ancient authority.

    13.11 Traditional Medicine and Mental Health¶

    Since independence, the Indian state has supported the traditional systems of medicine — Āyurveda, Yoga, Unani, Siddha, Sowa-Rigpa and Homoeopathy — through a separate department, which became the Ministry of AYUSH in 2014, with its own research councils, colleges and hospitals. The systems are thus institutionally established in India, and their practitioners treat many patients with mental as well as physical complaints. Institutional recognition is not the same as evidence of effectiveness, and the research councils’ own trials have often been small or of limited quality.

    The Āyurvedic concepts of mental constitution (mānasa prakṛti) and of the three guṇas (Chapter 3) have inspired questionnaires designed to measure “sattva”, “rajas” and “tamas” as personality dimensions. These instruments are research tools whose validity is still being assessed; they should not be used for diagnosis. Āyurvedic remedies for mental conditions are sold widely in India and abroad. Some herbs traditionally classed as medhya have been studied in small trials for memory or anxiety, with mixed results. The safety concerns noted in Chapter 3, including heavy-metal contamination of some products (Saper et al. 2004, 2008), apply here with particular force, since people with mental illness may stop effective treatment in favour of unproven remedies.

    13.12 Mental Health Care in Modern India¶

    Modern psychiatric institutions came to India with colonial rule. The East India Company opened “lunatic asylums” from the late eighteenth century, at first mainly for Europeans. Waltraud Ernst’s study of the European insane in British India in the first half of the nineteenth century shows how these institutions served to protect the image of the ruling race as well as to treat patients, and how asylums for Indians developed on different and usually poorer terms (Ernst 1991). The Indian Lunacy Act of 1912 regulated asylums across British India; and in the twentieth century they were gradually reformed into mental hospitals. The hospital at Bangalore developed into the National Institute of Mental Health and Neurosciences (NIMHANS, 1974), which became the leading centre of psychiatric research and training in the country. Independent India launched a National Mental Health Programme in 1982, and the Mental Healthcare Act of 2017 replaced earlier legislation with a rights-based framework.

    The gap between need and services remains very large, and most people with mental illness in India receive no specialist care. In this situation the appeal of traditional and religious forms of healing, and of yoga and meditation as low-cost self-help, is easy to understand. It makes it all the more important that such practices be described accurately, that their benefits not be exaggerated, and that they not be used to delay effective treatment.

    13.13 Traditional Healing and Its Dangers¶

    In India, as elsewhere, people with mental illness have long sought help from many sources at once: physicians of various systems, temples and shrines, exorcists, astrologers and families. Studies of healing at temples and Sufi shrines, such as Sudhir Kakar’s account of the Balaji temple at Mehandipur in Rajasthan (Kakar 1982), have shown that such places can offer meaning, community and relief to some sufferers, and that their rituals often work with culturally shared understandings of distress (for example, as possession by spirits). They have also documented serious abuses: people chained, beaten or confined, sometimes for years. In 2001 a fire at a shrine at Erwadi in Tamil Nadu killed twenty-eight mentally ill people who had been chained there, an event that led to inquiries and reforms. India’s Mental Healthcare Act of 2017 recognises the rights of persons with mental illness, including the right to be protected from cruel and degrading treatment.

    The lesson for a history of Indian psychology is that “tradition” in mental health is not uniformly benign. The classical texts contain humane insights — Caraka’s “conquest of the mind”, the Buddhist cultivation of compassion — but the practices that have claimed traditional authority include some that are harmful. A responsible use of the traditions distinguishes between them.

    13.14 Comparisons with Modern Psychotherapy¶

    Several modern psychotherapies have explicit or implicit links with Indian traditions. Mindfulness-based therapies drew directly on Buddhist (and Zen) practice; Acceptance and Commitment Therapy’s notion of an “observing self” resembles the Vedāntic witness and the Buddhist practice of observing mental events; cognitive therapy’s insistence that distress depends on interpretation recalls the Buddhist and Yoga analyses of craving and affliction. These parallels are worth exploring, and some Indian psychologists have developed therapies that draw deliberately on the Bhagavad Gītā or on Buddhist practice. Two points should be kept in mind. First, the modern therapies were developed and tested within a scientific framework, and their effectiveness is a matter of evidence, not of their resemblance to older teachings. Secondly, the traditions themselves were not therapies in the modern sense: their aims were ethical and soteriological, and stripping practices of that context changes them.

    13.15 Contemplative Practices in Schools¶

    Practices derived from Indian traditions have entered schools in India and abroad. Yoga has been part of physical education in many Indian schools for decades, and the International Day of Yoga (21 June), established by the United Nations General Assembly in 2014, has encouraged its promotion. Mindfulness programmes have been introduced in schools in several countries. In 2018 the government of Delhi introduced a “Happiness Curriculum” in its schools, with daily periods of mindfulness, stories and activities intended to support children’s emotional well-being.

    Research on school-based mindfulness and yoga programmes has produced mixed results. Some studies report improvements in attention, stress and classroom behaviour; others, including the MYRIAD trial, a large cluster-randomised trial of mindfulness training in British secondary schools published in 2022, found no clear benefit over the usual curriculum (Kuyken et al. 2022). Such programmes may be worthwhile as part of a broader approach to well-being, but they should be evaluated carefully rather than adopted on the strength of their traditional origins.

    13.16 The Revival of Nālandā¶

    The idea of reviving Nālandā as an international university was taken up by the Government of India and partner countries of the East Asia Summit in the 2000s. The Nalanda University Act was passed by Parliament in 2010; teaching began in temporary premises at Rajgir in 2014, with the economist Amartya Sen as the university’s first chancellor, and a new campus near the ancient site was inaugurated in 2024. The university is a modern postgraduate institution with schools of historical studies, ecology and environment, Buddhist studies and other subjects. It is a symbol of the continuing appeal of the ancient monastery; it is not, and does not claim to be, a continuation of it.

    13.17 Memory and Pedagogy¶

    It is sometimes proposed that the Vedic recitation techniques (Chapter 10) should be revived as a general method of learning. The techniques were designed for one purpose — the verbatim preservation of sacred sound — and achieved it superbly. Some of the principles they embody, such as distributed practice and repeated retrieval, are supported by modern research on learning and can be applied in many contexts. But modern education also requires understanding, application and critical thinking, which rote recitation alone does not develop — a limitation recognised by Yāska more than two thousand years ago. The lesson of the oral tradition for modern pedagogy is not that memorisation should replace understanding, but that disciplined practice over long periods can achieve remarkable results.

    13.18 Open Questions¶

    Research on practices derived from Indian traditions has made progress, but many basic questions remain open.

    • Active ingredients. Mindfulness and yoga programmes combine many elements — attention training, breathing, physical exercise, relaxation, group support, ethical reflection — and it is often unclear which of them produce the effects observed.

    • Comparison with alternatives. Many studies compare an intervention with no treatment or a waiting list; fewer compare it with equally credible active treatments, and these comparisons tend to show smaller differences.

    • Long-term effects. Most trials are short; little is known about effects over years, or about what happens when people stop practising.

    • Harms. Adverse effects have been systematically studied only recently, and their frequency and risk factors are not well established.

    • Context. Practices removed from their traditional settings may work differently from those embedded in them, and results obtained with Western, educated participants may not transfer to other populations, including Indian ones.

    • Traditional claims. Many claims made in the classical texts — about supernormal powers, specific effects of particular breathing techniques or mantras, or the effects of dietary and herbal regimens on the mind — have not been tested at all, or have been tested only in small studies of low quality.

    Acknowledging these open questions is not a dismissal of the traditions. It is the normal condition of a field of research, and the traditions themselves, at their best, valued the careful testing of claims (parīkṣā) over credulity.

    13.19 Teaching Indian Knowledge Systems¶

    The introduction of “Indian Knowledge Systems” into curricula raises practical questions for teachers. The material surveyed in this volume suggests some guidelines.

    1. Quote real texts, with references. Many quotations that circulate in popular books and on the internet as “Vedic” or “from the Upaniṣads” are invented or garbled. A teacher should be able to say where a verse comes from and should check it against an edition or a reliable translation.

    2. Give dates as scholars give them. Traditional dates (for example, for the Buddha or Śaṅkara) can be mentioned, but they should be labelled as traditional and distinguished from the dates supported by historical evidence.

    3. Avoid claims of priority. The value of an Indian theory does not depend on its having “anticipated” a modern discovery. Claims that ancient texts contain modern science usually misread both.

    4. Present debate, not doctrine. Indian philosophy is a history of argument between schools. Students learn more from the arguments of Buddhists, Naiyāyikas, Vedāntins and Jains against each other than from a single “Indian view”.

    5. Include the excluded. The history of Indian learning includes those who were denied it — Śūdras and Dalits, women, tribal communities — and those who fought for access to it. Their voices belong in the curriculum.

    6. Distinguish history from health advice. Classical accounts of the mind and of healing are part of intellectual history; recommendations about health must rest on modern evidence.

    Applied in this way, the study of Indian traditions of mind and education can serve the aims that the NEP sets for it — a better understanding of India’s intellectual heritage — without repeating the myths that have too often been taught in its name.

    13.20 Conclusion¶

    The Indian traditions of thought about the mind and of education are among the richest in the world. They include sophisticated analyses of perception, error, memory, emotion, attention and the self; detailed methods of mental training; an oral tradition of unmatched precision; and institutions of learning that drew students from across Asia. They also include an ideology that reserved the highest learning for a few and excluded many, and a body of legends that has grown up around the real history.

    Three principles follow for anyone who wishes to draw on these traditions today. First, read the sources accurately, in their own terms and in their historical context. Secondly, test claims about effects on health and well-being by the same standards as any other claims, and report the results honestly, including harms. Thirdly, remember who was excluded, and value the traditions in a way that includes them. On these conditions, the inheritance described in this volume can be what the Viṣṇu Purāṇa hoped knowledge would be: not merely a skill, but a means of freedom.
