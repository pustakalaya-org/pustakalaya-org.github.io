    Chapter 10: The Oral Tradition: Knowledge in the Living Voice¶

    yad eṣām anyo anyasya vācaṃ śāktasyeva vadati śikṣamāṇaḥ
    When one of them repeats the speech of another, as a learner repeats his teacher’s …
    — Ṛgveda 7.103.5 (the hymn to the frogs)

    10.1 Introduction¶

    The Vedic hymns were composed, collected and transmitted for many centuries without writing. The Ṛgveda, a collection of 1,028 hymns comprising some 10,500 verses whose core is dated c. 1500–1200 BCE, was handed down from teacher to student by word of mouth, together with the other Vedic collections, their ritual prose (the Brāhmaṇas) and their speculative appendices (the Āraṇyakas and Upaniṣads). Apart from the undeciphered Indus script of the third millennium BCE, which had long disappeared by the Vedic period, writing is first securely attested in India in the inscriptions of Aśoka in the third century BCE, and the Vedic texts seem to have been written down only much later; no surviving Vedic manuscript is older than the early second millennium CE, and most are far more recent. Yet the oral transmission was so precise that versions recited today in widely separated regions agree almost syllable for syllable, including the pitch accents that had long since disappeared from spoken Sanskrit.

    This achievement rested on a specialised educational technique: the training of reciters through years of repetition, supported by elaborate “modifications” of the text and by a body of phonetic science. UNESCO proclaimed the tradition of Vedic chanting a Masterpiece of the Oral and Intangible Heritage of Humanity in 2003. This chapter describes how the system worked, what it achieved and what is claimed for it (Staal 1961; Staal 1986).

    10.2 Why Oral?¶

    Scholars have offered several explanations for the long preference for oral transmission of the Veda, which are compatible with each other.

    1. The power of exact sound. The Veda was śruti, “what is heard”. Its efficacy in ritual was believed to depend on exact pronunciation, including accent, and a written text could not guarantee this.

    2. Control of transmission. Oral teaching kept the Veda within the families and schools entitled to it. The prohibitions on Śūdras hearing or learning the Veda (Chapter 9) presuppose oral transmission, and the reluctance to write the Veda down was partly a reluctance to make it accessible.

    3. Prestige of memorised knowledge. A well-known saying holds that “knowledge that is in a book, and wealth that is in another’s hand” are useless when needed. Learning that existed only in a book was regarded as second-rate.

    4. Chronology. The Vedic corpus was largely composed before writing came into use; by the time writing was available, the oral system was well established and highly prestigious.

    Writing, when it came, was used for many other purposes — administration, commerce, literature, the sciences — and the śāstric literature was transmitted both orally and in manuscripts (Chapter 12). But the most sacred texts remained, in principle, oral.

    10.3 Branches of Transmission¶

    Each Veda was transmitted in several “branches” (śākhā), recensions preserved by particular lineages of teachers and students, each with its own text, recitation style and ritual manuals. Patañjali’s Mahābhāṣya (2nd century BCE) gives the traditional numbers: twenty-one branches of the Ṛgveda, a hundred and one of the Yajurveda, a thousand “paths” of the Sāmaveda and nine of the Atharvaveda. Only a few survive (Table 10.1).

    Table 10.1. Surviving branches (śākhā) of the Vedas.
    Veda Traditional number Surviving branches (text and/or recitation)
    Ṛgveda 21 Śākala (the extant saṃhitā); traces of others
    Yajurveda 101 White: Mādhyandina, Kāṇva. Black: Taittirīya, Maitrāyaṇī, Kaṭha (and fragments of Kapiṣṭhala)
    Sāmaveda 1,000 Kauthuma, Rāṇāyanīya, Jaiminīya
    Atharvaveda 9 Śaunaka, Paippalāda (the latter recited by a small community in Odisha)

    The loss of most branches shows the vulnerability of oral transmission: when a lineage died out, its text disappeared with it. The survival of the others shows its robustness: where lineages continued, texts were preserved with extraordinary fidelity.

    10.4 The Recitation Patterns¶

    10.4.1 The Basic Texts¶

    A Vedic text was learned in several forms. The continuous text (saṃhitāpāṭha) presents the words as they are recited in ritual, joined by the rules of euphonic combination (sandhi), which can obscure word boundaries. The word-by-word text (padapāṭha) separates every word and gives it in its independent form, splitting compounds and noting suffixes; the padapāṭha of the Ṛgveda is attributed to Śākalya and is already an analysis of the text, making explicit its grammar. The step text (kramapāṭha) recites the words in overlapping pairs: 1–2, 2–3, 3–4, and so on.

    10.4.2 The Eight Modifications¶

    On these foundations the tradition built eight more complex “modifications” (vikṛti): jaṭā (“braid”), mālā (“garland”), śikhā (“crest”), rekhā (“line”), dhvaja (“flag”), daṇḍa (“staff”), ratha (“chariot”) and ghana (“dense”). Each permutes the words according to a fixed pattern. Two are commonly performed today. Table 10.2 shows how the basic forms and two of the modifications treat a sequence of four words.

    Table 10.2. Recitation patterns for a sequence of four words A B C D.
    Pattern Recitation
    saṃhitā A B C D (with sandhi)
    pada A  |  B  |  C  |  D
    krama AB  |  BC  |  CD
    jaṭā AB BA AB  |  BC CB BC  |  CD DC CD
    ghana AB BA ABC CBA ABC  |  BC CB BCD DCB BCD  |  …

    In the patterns that recite words backwards, sandhi must be applied anew to each reversed pair, and the accents must be adjusted according to strict rules. A reciter who can perform the ghana of a long text — a ghanapāṭhin — has therefore mastered not only the sequence of words but their grammatical form and accentuation. The permutations multiply the length of the text many times over.

    10.4.3 How the System Checks Itself¶

    The patterns provide multiple, interlocking representations of the same text. An error in the continuous text would be exposed by the word-by-word text, and an error in the order of words by the step and braided patterns, since each word appears in several combinations with its neighbours. The redundancy is often explained by analogy with error-detecting codes (Section 1.10), but the system’s reliability depended as much on the discipline of teachers and the social organisation of the schools as on the patterns themselves.

    10.5 Accent and Phonetics¶

    Vedic Sanskrit had a pitch accent, which is marked in the saṃhitā and pada texts and preserved in recitation. The grammarians distinguish the raised (udātta), the not-raised (anudātta) and the “sounded” or falling (svarita) tones, the last typically following an udātta. In recitation the accents are often accompanied by movements of the head or hand, which differ from school to school; Sāmaveda singers use finger positions to mark the notes of the chants.

    Every student was taught that accent could change meaning, and that an error could be dangerous. Patañjali, justifying the study of grammar at the opening of his Mahābhāṣya, quotes the verse that became the standard warning (the Pāṇinīya Śikṣā has a variant beginning mantro hīnaḥ):

    Patañjali, Mahābhāṣya, introduction (Paspaśāhnika)

    duṣṭaḥ śabdaḥ svarato varṇato vā mithyāprayukto na tam artham āha |
    sa vāgvajro yajamānaṃ hinasti yathendraśatruḥ svarato ’parādhāt ||
    A word spoiled in accent or in sound and misused fails to say what is meant; it becomes a thunderbolt of speech that strikes the patron of the rite, like indraśatru with its misplaced accent.

    The example, a compound whose meaning flips between “slayer of Indra” and “having Indra as slayer” with the position of the accent, comes from a Brāhmaṇa story retold in Volume 10, Śabda Vidyā. For the history of education its interest lies in the attitude it expresses: recitation was drilled not as an aesthetic accomplishment but under a sense of ritual danger, which helps explain the severity and length of the training described below.

    To secure correct pronunciation, each Vedic school developed phonetic manuals. The prātiśākhyas state the rules by which the pada text is converted into the saṃhitā text for a particular branch, and the śikṣā texts describe the articulation of sounds by place and manner, the length of vowels and the qualities of good and bad recitation. This phonetic science, which classifies sounds by their point of articulation and the effort involved, is among the most precise in the ancient world and laid the foundation for Pāṇini’s grammar (Scharfe 2002). Its achievements are discussed further in Volume 10 of this series.

    10.6 Chant: The Sāmaveda¶

    The Sāmaveda poses special problems of transmission, because its texts are melodies (sāman) sung to verses drawn mostly from the Ṛgveda. In singing, the words of the verse are transformed: syllables are lengthened, repeated and replaced by meaningless “stobha” syllables, and the melody is divided into sections sung by different priests. The songs are collected in “song-books” (gāna), and their notes were indicated in later manuscripts by numerals or syllables written above the text, and in performance by finger and hand positions. The three surviving branches — Kauthuma, Rāṇāyanīya and Jaiminīya — differ in their melodies and their styles of singing; the Jaiminīya tradition survives mainly among the Nambūdiris of Kerala and in parts of Tamil Nadu. Recordings and studies of these traditions since the mid-twentieth century have shown how much regional variation exists within a framework of strict fidelity to the inherited melodies.

    10.7 A Rediscovered Branch¶

    The fate of the Paippalāda branch of the Atharvaveda illustrates both the fragility and the persistence of the oral tradition. For a long time the Paippalāda text was known in the West only from a single, damaged birch-bark manuscript from Kashmir, discovered in the nineteenth century. In the 1950s the Bengali scholar Durgamohan Bhattacharyya found that Brahmin families in Odisha still possessed palm-leaf manuscripts of the Paippalāda saṃhitā and preserved a tradition of reciting it. The Odisha manuscripts have since become the basis of new critical editions. The episode shows that branches of Vedic learning considered lost may survive in small communities, and that the oral and manuscript traditions supported each other.

    10.8 How Students Learned¶

    The texts and modern observation of traditional schools allow a fairly clear picture of the method.

    • The teacher recites a short segment — often half a verse — and the student repeats it, usually several times, until it is exact. The Ṛgvedic “frog hymn” (7.103) already compares the croaking of frogs to students repeating the words of their teacher.

    • New material is added daily while earlier material is repeated, so that the whole is revised at regular intervals.

    • The padapāṭha and kramapāṭha are learned after the saṃhitā, and the advanced modifications only by specialists.

    • Recitation is accompanied by hand and head movements marking the accents, which serve as a physical aid to memory.

    • Students recite together and correct one another, and large recitations in assemblies provide public checks.

    • Learning begins young, often at seven or eight, and continues for many years; full mastery of a Veda with its modifications may take a decade or more.

    Psychologists who have looked at these methods note that they combine several techniques known to support retention: distributed practice over long periods, active recall, multiple overlapping encodings of the same material, rhythmic and melodic structure, and the use of movement. These observations help explain why the method works; its purpose, however — verbatim preservation of sound, often without attention to meaning — is not the purpose of most modern education (Section 13.17).

    10.8.1 The Rhythm of the Year¶

    Vedic study was regulated by a calendar. It began each year with a ceremony of opening (upākaraṇa), usually in the rainy season, and was formally closed some months later (utsarjana). The Dharmasūtras list a great many occasions on which recitation must be suspended (anadhyāya): on certain lunar days, during storms, thunder and earthquakes, at the death of certain persons, in the presence of particular sounds or people, in a village where there is a corpse, and so on. Some of these rules served practical purposes — recitation in a thunderstorm would be inaudible — while others expressed ideas of ritual purity. Together they show that Vedic learning was embedded in ritual time, not organised as a continuous “course”.

    10.8.2 Meaning and Sound¶

    A striking feature of the Vedic schools is that the preservation of sound was often separated from the understanding of meaning. Many reciters, past and present, learned the texts without studying their sense, which was the business of exegetes and ritualists. The Nirukta of Yāska (mid-first millennium BCE) complains of those who learn the Veda without understanding it, comparing such a person to a pillar or a bearer of burdens. The tradition thus contained an internal critique of rote learning even while it depended on it.

    10.9 Feats of Memory: Avadhāna¶

    The culture of trained memory found other expressions beyond the Veda. In the literary tradition of the Telugu and Kannada regions, and in Sanskrit, a performance called avadhāna (“attention”) tests a poet’s memory and concentration in public. In the “eightfold” form (aṣṭāvadhāna) the performer deals simultaneously with eight questioners: composing verses on set themes and in set metres, under constraints such as the exclusion of certain letters; completing a verse of which one line is given; conversing on unrelated subjects; keeping count of bells rung at random intervals; and finally reciting all the compositions in order. Larger forms with a hundred (śatāvadhāna) or more participants are recorded; in the Jain tradition, Śrīmad Rājacandra (1867–1901), the spiritual guide of the young Gandhi, was famous in his youth for such performances. Avadhāna is a living art and a striking demonstration of the capacities that long training in memory and verse composition can develop.

    10.10 Memorising the Śāstras¶

    Memorisation was not confined to the Veda. The root texts of the śāstras — Pāṇini’s nearly four thousand grammatical rules, the Amarakośa (the standard Sanskrit thesaurus, in verse), the sūtras of the philosophical schools, the verse summaries of medicine and astronomy — were composed in forms designed to be memorised, and students learned them by heart before studying their meaning. The extreme concision of the sūtra style, in which a grammarian was said to rejoice over saving half a short vowel as much as over the birth of a son, made texts easy to memorise but impossible to understand without a teacher’s explanation; memory and commentary were thus two halves of a single method. Versification served the same purpose in the sciences: mathematical rules, medical prescriptions and astronomical procedures were cast in metrical form so that they could be carried in the mind and recalled when needed.

    Scholarly Note: “Vedic Mathematics”

    A set of rapid calculation techniques published in 1965 under the title Vedic Mathematics, by Bhāratī Kṛṣṇa Tīrtha, Śaṅkarācārya of Puri, is widely taught in Indian schools and coaching classes as an ancient system. The sixteen “sūtras” on which it is based do not occur in any Vedic text or Vedic appendix; the author stated that he had reconstructed them, and the methods are modern arithmetical shortcuts. They may be useful for mental arithmetic, but they have no historical connection with the Veda, and they should not be presented as part of India’s mathematical heritage. The genuine history of Indian mathematics is treated in Volume 11 of this series.

    10.11 Ritual Performance¶

    Vedic recitation was originally inseparable from ritual. In the great solemn (śrauta) sacrifices, priests of the four Vedas each recited or sang their own texts in a fixed sequence, and the correctness of the ritual depended on the correctness of the recitation. Some of these rituals survive in a few families. In 1975 a twelve-day performance of the Agnicayana, the ritual of building the fire altar, by Nambūdiri priests at Panjal in Kerala was documented on film and in a two-volume study by Frits Staal and his collaborators (Staal 1983), preserving a detailed record of a ritual tradition of great antiquity. Such documentation is one way in which a tradition that was designed to be transmitted only from person to person is now also preserved in other media.

    10.12 Research on Vedic Reciters¶

    A neuroimaging study published in 2016 compared the brains of a group of professional Vedic pandits, trained from childhood in intensive memorisation and recitation, with those of matched controls (Hartzell et al. 2016). The authors reported differences in grey-matter density and cortical thickness in several regions, including areas associated with language, memory (among them parts of the hippocampus) and vision. The study is of genuine interest as evidence that long training in a specialised skill is associated with differences in brain structure, as has been found for musicians, taxi drivers and other experts. Its limitations should also be noted: it was cross-sectional and relatively small, so it cannot show that recitation training caused the differences, and its findings concern a highly selected group. It does not show that Vedic recitation improves general intelligence or that it should be adopted as an educational method.

    10.13 Other Oral Traditions¶

    The Vedic schools were not the only oral traditions of India.

    • Buddhist recitation. According to tradition, the Buddha’s teachings were recited and fixed at a council soon after his death, and the canon was transmitted orally by specialised reciters (bhāṇaka) for several centuries; the Sri Lankan chronicles say the Pali canon was written down in the first century BCE. Communal recitation remained central to monastic life.

    • The Pali word for these councils, saṅgīti, means literally “chanting together”: a council was an assembly at which the community recited the teachings in unison, establishing an agreed text by collective performance. The Theravāda chronicles describe three or four such councils before the canon was committed to writing, and modern Buddhist countries have held further councils on the same model, the most recent in Burma in 1954–56.

    • Jain transmission. The Jain scriptures were likewise transmitted orally, with periodic councils to reconcile versions, until the council at Valabhī (fifth century CE) had them written down (Chapter 11).

    • Epic and Purāṇic narration. The epics and Purāṇas present themselves as narrated by bards (sūta) and storytellers. Unlike the Vedic texts, they were transmitted with much variation; the critical edition of the Mahābhārata prepared at Pune shows how freely the text grew in different regions. Public recitation and exposition (kathā, pravacana) of the epics and Purāṇas, and musical narration such as Harikathā, remain living traditions.

    • Regional and folk traditions. Oral epics in the regional languages — the Rajasthani epic of Pābūjī, the Ālhā of north India, the epics of the Tamil and Telugu countries — are performed by hereditary bards, often with painted scrolls or cloths, and vary with each performance. Tribal communities transmit genealogies, myths and knowledge of the environment orally (Volume 23).

    • Music. Classical music was taught orally from teacher to student within lineages (gharānā, paramparā) until the twentieth century and is still largely so taught (Volume 9).

    Comparison with oral traditions elsewhere is illuminating. In the 1930s Milman Parry and Albert Lord studied the oral epic singers of Yugoslavia and showed that they composed their long poems anew in each performance, using a stock of traditional formulas and themes; they argued that the Homeric epics had been composed in the same way (Parry 1971; Lord 1960). The Indian epics and Purāṇas, and many regional oral epics, show features of this kind of composition-in-performance. The Vedic tradition is quite different: it aimed at, and achieved, verbatim reproduction of a fixed text, with techniques designed to eliminate variation. The contrast shows that “oral tradition” covers very different practices, and that the Vedic achievement is unusual among the world’s oral literatures.

    Women’s oral traditions deserve special mention, because they are rarely represented in the textual record. Across India women have composed and transmitted songs for work, ritual and the life cycle — songs for grinding grain, for weddings, for the birth of children, for laments — in which they express their experiences, griefs and judgements of family and society. In Maharashtra, for example, the “grinding songs” (ovī) sung by women at the hand-mill have been collected in their tens of thousands by researchers since the 1980s, revealing a large body of women’s poetry about work, kinship, devotion and suffering. Such songs are part of the history of Indian knowledge and education: they are the means by which much of what women knew was taught to the next generation.

    These traditions differ from the Vedic one in a fundamental way: they valued faithful transmission of a story, a style or a repertoire, not verbatim fixity of every syllable. Together they show that Indian oral culture was not one system but many.

    10.14 The Living Tradition¶

    Vedic recitation continues in traditional schools in many parts of India — among the Nambūdiri Brahmins of Kerala, whose Ṛgvedic and Sāmavedic traditions were documented by Frits Staal (1961); in Tamil Nadu, Karnataka, Andhra Pradesh and Maharashtra; in Vārāṇasī; and elsewhere. Some branches survive in only a handful of families. Government and private bodies, including the Maharshi Sandipani Rashtriya Veda Vidya Pratishthan at Ujjain, support Vedic schools, and recordings have been made of many branches. Changes in the economy and in family occupations have reduced the number of full-time reciters, and the long-term survival of several branches is uncertain.

    The social basis of the tradition has also begun to change. Vedic recitation was for most of its history restricted to males of particular Brahmin families. In the twentieth century reform movements such as the Arya Samaj taught the Veda to women and to members of other castes, and today a number of institutions train women in Vedic recitation and as ritual officiants, though this remains contested in some traditional circles. The Veda itself, once guarded from those not entitled to hear it, has been printed — the first complete edition of the Ṛgveda with Sāyaṇa’s commentary was published by Friedrich Max Müller in 1849–1874 — and is now freely available in print, in recordings and online.

    The oral tradition is an achievement that historians of every persuasion admire. Admiration need not mean uncritical celebration: the same system that preserved the Veda so faithfully also kept it within a narrow circle, and its methods were designed to preserve sound rather than to cultivate understanding. Both facts are part of its history.
