    Chapter 7: Dreams, Sleep and the States Between¶

    na tatra rathā na rathayogā na panthāno bhavanti | atha rathān rathayogān pathaḥ sṛjate
    There are no chariots there, no harnessings, no roads; but he creates chariots, harnessings and roads.
    — Bṛhadāraṇyaka Upaniṣad 4.3.10

    7.1 Introduction¶

    Few ancient traditions took dreams and sleep as seriously as the Indian. For the Upaniṣadic teachers the dream state showed that the self can create a world from its own resources; for Gauḍapāda and the Buddhist idealists it was the decisive argument about the status of the waking world; for the physicians it was a source of diagnostic and prognostic signs and sleep was one of the three supports of life; for the dream-interpreters it was a field of omens; and for the yogins it was a state to be traversed with awareness. This chapter surveys these approaches and reports the claims of each tradition as claims; modern sleep science, whose methods and questions are different, neither inherits nor confirms them (Section 1.10).

    7.2 Dreams in the Upaniṣads¶

    7.2.1 Yājñavalkya on the Dreaming Self¶

    The fullest Upaniṣadic treatment of dreams is Yājñavalkya’s instruction of King Janaka in the Bṛhadāraṇyaka Upaniṣad (4.3.9–18). The person has two places, this world and the other world, and a third, the place of dream, which is the “junction” (sandhya) between them. When he dreams, he takes material from this world, “himself breaks it down and himself builds it up”, and dreams by his own light; there the person becomes self-illuminated. The dreamer creates chariots and roads, joys and delights, ponds and rivers — “for he is a creator” (BU 4.3.10, quoted at the head of this chapter).

    Yājñavalkya illustrates the movement between states with an image:

    Bṛhadāraṇyaka Upaniṣad 4.3.18

    tad yathā mahāmatsya ubhe kūle anusaṃcarati pūrvaṃ cāparaṃ ca | evam evāyaṃ puruṣa etāv ubhāv antāv anusaṃcarati svapnāntaṃ ca buddhāntaṃ ca ||
    As a great fish moves along both banks of a river, the near and the far, so this person moves along both these realms, the realm of dream and the realm of waking.

    Beyond dream lies the state “where, asleep, he desires no desire and sees no dream”, to which the self hastens like a falcon that, tired of flying, folds its wings and settles in its nest (BU 4.3.19); there, embraced by the self of knowledge, it knows nothing within or without (BU 4.3.21).

    7.2.2 Indra and Virocana¶

    The Chāndogya Upaniṣad (8.7–12) tells how the god Indra and the demon Virocana came to the creator Prajāpati to learn about the self. Prajāpati first identifies the self with the body seen in a reflection; Virocana is satisfied and leaves, but Indra returns, troubled. Prajāpati then identifies the self with the one who moves about happily in dreams; Indra objects that the dream self, though not blinded when the body is blind, still seems to be struck, chased and to weep. The self in dreamless sleep is offered next; Indra objects that in that state one does not know oneself at all and seems to be annihilated. Only then does Prajāpati teach the self beyond all three. The story is an early example of teaching by stages, with the student’s own objections driving the inquiry forward (see Chapter 9).

    7.2.3 The Māṇḍūkya¶

    As described in Chapter 2, the Māṇḍūkya Upaniṣad assigns the dream state to the “luminous” self (taijasa), whose cognition is turned inward and which enjoys subtle objects, and correlates it with the sound u of oṃ.

    7.3 Dreams as Philosophical Evidence¶

    7.3.1 Gauḍapāda¶

    Gauḍapāda’s Māṇḍūkyakārikā (chapter 2; see Chapter 2) uses dreams to argue that the waking world is unreal. Dream objects are experienced as external and spatially located, yet they are within the body and are contradicted on waking; and the features that seem to make waking objects real — their location, their apparent usefulness — are present in dreams too, judged from within the dream. Waking objects are equally “seen”, and therefore equally unreal.

    Māṇḍūkyakārikā 2.31

    svapnamāye yathā dṛṣṭaṃ gandharvanagaraṃ yathā |
    tathā viśvam idaṃ dṛṣṭaṃ vedānteṣu vicakṣaṇaiḥ ||
    As a dream and a magic show are seen, and as a city of the Gandharvas in the clouds, so this whole world is seen by those versed in the Vedānta.

    7.3.2 The Buddhist Idealists¶

    The Yogācāra philosopher Vasubandhu, in his Viṃśatikā (“Twenty Verses”), used dreams to answer objections to the doctrine that there are no external objects. If there are no external objects, the objector asks, why are experiences restricted to particular places and times, why do many people perceive the same things, and why do objects have real effects? Vasubandhu replies that in dreams, too, things appear in particular places and times, and dream experiences can have physical effects; and that in the hells described by Buddhist cosmology many beings perceive the same infernal guardians without these being external to their minds. Dreams thus show that the marks of reality do not require external objects.

    7.3.3 The Realists on Dreams¶

    The realist schools explained dreams without making them a problem for the reality of the world. The Vaiśeṣika philosopher Praśastapāda (6th century) defined the dream as a cognition that arises, through the contact of the self with the mind, when the senses have ceased to function. He attributed dreams to three causes: the strength of impressions left by previous experience (as when a person who has been thinking intensely of something dreams of it), disorders of the bodily humours (as when one affected by wind dreams of flying), and unseen merit and demerit, which produce auspicious and inauspicious dreams. Dreams are thus a species of memory-based cognition, erroneous because what is remembered is experienced as present. The Naiyāyikas argued, against the idealists, that dream objects are themselves fragments of things experienced in waking life; dreams therefore presuppose a real external world rather than casting doubt on it.

    7.3.4 Śaṅkara’s Reply¶

    Śaṅkara, although a non-dualist, rejected the Buddhist argument. Commenting on Brahmasūtra 2.2.29 (“and because of difference in nature, [waking cognition] is not like dream”), he argued that dream cognitions are contradicted on waking whereas waking cognitions are not contradicted in the same way; that dreams are a form of memory, while waking experience is direct perception; and that the Buddhist cannot deny the external world while relying on its regularities. The debate shows how dreams were used on both sides of a central metaphysical controversy, and why Śaṅkara distinguished the “empirical” reality of the waking world from the “apparent” reality of dreams (Chapter 4).

    7.3.5 The Yogavāsiṣṭha¶

    The Yogavāsiṣṭha turns the philosophical argument into narrative. In the story of Līlā, a queen grieving for her dead husband, King Padma, is granted by the goddess Sarasvatī the power to travel in consciousness. She discovers that his mind contains a whole world in which he has been reborn as another king, with a long history of his own — and that the world in which she herself lives is in turn contained in another’s mind. The work’s stories of nested worlds, in which a moment in one corresponds to years in another, are literary explorations of the claim that the world is a projection of mind, works of imaginative philosophy in their own right (Venkatesananda 1984; O’Flaherty 1984).

    7.4 The Physicians on Dreams¶

    The medical saṃhitās treat dreams as signs. The Carakasaṃhitā (Indriyasthāna 5.43) distinguishes seven kinds of dream (Table 7.1): what has been seen (dṛṣṭa), heard (śruta) or experienced (anubhūta) in waking life; what has been wished for (prārthita); what is imagined (kalpita); what is prophetic (bhāvika); and what arises from the disordered doṣas (doṣaja). The first five, Caraka says, are without significance; only the prophetic dream and the dream caused by disease are meaningful. A dream seen in the first part of the night bears little fruit; one after which the dreamer does not fall asleep again bears great fruit at once (5.44–45).

    Table 7.1. The seven kinds of dream in the Carakasaṃhitā (Indriyasthāna 5).
    Kind Meaning Significance according to Caraka
    dṛṣṭa Of what has been seen Without consequence
    śruta Of what has been heard Without consequence
    anubhūta Of what has been experienced Without consequence
    prārthita Of what has been wished for Without consequence
    kalpita Of what has been imagined Without consequence
    bhāvika Prophetic Indicates what is to come
    doṣaja Arising from the doṣas Indicates disease

    Caraka’s recognition that most dreams simply rework waking experience, wishes and imagination is striking; his interest, however, lies in the two kinds he considers meaningful. The prophetic dreams that most concern the physician are omens of death (ariṣṭa), listed at length in the same chapter — travelling south with dogs, camels or donkeys, being carried off by a woman while garlanded and dressed in red, and many others. The Indriyasthāna as a whole is a manual of fatal prognosis, and these dream images belong to a wider culture of omens shared with the dream-interpretation manuals. Later Āyurvedic writers associated dream content with the predominant doṣa: dreams of flying and wandering with vāta, of fire and gold with pitta, of water and lotuses with kapha.

    These classifications are historical evidence of how physicians thought; they are not a validated diagnostic method. Modern research has examined whether dream content changes with illness, but the claim that dreams can be used to diagnose disease or predict death has no reliable support.

    7.5 Dreams in Buddhist and Jain Literature¶

    • The Milindapañha. In this dialogue between the Greek king Menander (Milinda) and the monk Nāgasena, Nāgasena distinguishes six kinds of dreamer: those affected by wind, by bile or by phlegm; those influenced by a deity; those who dream of what they are used to; and those who receive a prognostic dream. Only the last kind, he says, is true; and the dream occurs neither in deep sleep nor in waking but in the light sleep between them.

    • Queen Māyā’s dream. According to the Buddhist biographical tradition, the Buddha’s mother dreamed at his conception that a white elephant entered her side; Brahmin interpreters at the court explained that she would bear a son who would become either a universal ruler or a Buddha. The scene became one of the most frequently depicted in Buddhist art, from the early stūpa railings onwards.

    • The Buddha’s dreams. The Aṅguttara Nikāya (5.196) records five great dreams that the Bodhisatta had before his awakening, each interpreted as a sign of it.

    • The mothers of the Jinas. Jain tradition holds that the mother of every Tīrthaṅkara sees a series of auspicious dreams at his conception — fourteen according to the Śvetāmbaras, sixteen according to the Digambaras — including an elephant, a bull, a lion, the goddess Śrī, the moon and the sun. The dreams are a favourite subject of Jain manuscript painting (Chapter 12).

    7.5.1 Dreams in Epic and Drama¶

    Dreams play important parts in Indian narrative literature. In the Rāmāyaṇa’s Sundarakāṇḍa, the demoness Trijaṭā, one of Sītā’s guards in Laṅkā, tells the others of a dream in which she saw Rāma in white garments on a celestial chariot and Rāvaṇa, shaven and smeared with oil, dragged southwards on a donkey — images that match the omens of death listed by the physicians — and warns them to treat Sītā kindly. In the play Svapnavāsavadatta (“Vāsavadattā in a Dream”), attributed to Bhāsa, King Udayana, half asleep, speaks to his queen, whom he believes dead, and the play turns on the ambiguity between dream and waking. Dreams in such works serve as omens, as devices of plot, and as explorations of longing and memory — uses that show how familiar the theories of the physicians and dream-interpreters were to poets and their audiences.

    Dream interpretation was also a learned specialism. The Atharvaveda contains hymns against bad dreams, the Purāṇas and astrological works contain chapters on dream omens, and Jagaddeva’s Svapnacintāmaṇi (c. 12th century) is a manual devoted to the subject, classifying auspicious and inauspicious dreams and prescribing rites to avert the bad ones. Dreams also had a place in religious foundation stories: many temple traditions tell how a deity appeared to a king, a priest or a devotee in a dream and commanded that an image be found or a shrine built, and Sufi hagiography is full of dreams in which a saint appears to his disciples. Islamic dream interpretation (taʿbīr), with its own manuals in Arabic and Persian, circulated in India alongside the Sanskrit tradition (Section 7.6). These texts belong to the history of divination; they are not psychological theories of dreams in the modern sense, though they reveal what images were charged with meaning in the culture.

    7.6 Dreams in the Islamic and Sufi Traditions¶

    From the eleventh century India also had a second learned tradition of dream interpretation, in Arabic and Persian. Its starting point was a ḥadīth, transmitted in the collections of al-Bukhārī and Muslim, that divides dreams into three kinds. A good dream is glad tidings from God; a distressing dream comes from Satan; and a third kind arises from what a person has been “talking to himself” about, his own preoccupations. Another ḥadīth calls the true dream of a believer one of the forty-six parts of prophecy. Dreams were therefore taken seriously as a possible channel of knowledge, but most dreams were not thought to be of this kind. The threefold division is close to the classifications of the Milindapañha and of Caraka, which also set aside dreams arising from habit and bodily disturbance before admitting a small class of meaningful ones.

    The philosophers gave the true dream a psychological explanation. In Ibn Sīnā’s account the imagination (Chapter 8) is busy in sleep with the residues of the day and the effects of the body’s humours, which produce most dreams. When it is quiet, the rational soul can receive knowledge from the higher intellects, and the imagination then translates this knowledge into images. The images of a meaningful dream are thus symbols that need interpretation (taʿbīr, literally “carrying across”), and a large Arabic and Persian literature of dream books, many ascribed to the eighth-century scholar Ibn Sīrīn, catalogued them. Unani physicians, like Caraka, also read dreams as signs of the body’s state: a person dominated by black bile was expected to have dark and fearful dreams.

    The Sufis made dreams part of the spiritual path. Disciples reported their dreams to the master, who interpreted them as signs of the disciple’s inner state and progress. A seeker could be initiated in a dream by a dead master, the so-called uwaysī initiation, and dreams of the Prophet were valued as a confirmation of a teaching or a vocation. The practice of istikhāra, a prayer for guidance after which the answer is sought in a dream, was and is widespread among Indian Muslims. The malfūẓāt of the Chishtī masters record dreams as part of their teaching, and the Mughal emperors noted their dreams and those of their courtiers in their memoirs. In the Indian setting this tradition met the Sanskrit and vernacular dream lore at many points. Mughal and Rajput painting depicts dreams in both traditions, and the dream of the beloved that sets a hero on his quest is a stock motif of the Sufi romances in Avadhi (Chapter 8).

    7.7 Sleep in Āyurveda¶

    The Carakasaṃhitā names three “supports” (upastambha) of life: food, sleep and regulated sexual conduct (Sūtrasthāna 11.35). Its chapter on sleep (Sūtrasthāna 21) begins from a strong claim:

    Carakasaṃhitā, Sūtrasthāna 21.36

    nidrāyattaṃ sukhaṃ duḥkhaṃ puṣṭiḥ kārśyaṃ balābalam |
    vṛṣatā klībatā jñānam ajñānaṃ jīvitaṃ na ca ||
    Happiness and unhappiness, plumpness and leanness, strength and weakness, virility and impotence, knowledge and ignorance, life and its absence depend on sleep.

    Caraka explains sleep as occurring when the mind, together with the senses, becomes exhausted and withdraws from its objects. He distinguishes sleep born of tamas, of kapha, of mental and of bodily exhaustion, sleep that is adventitious (for example from injury), sleep that accompanies disease, and sleep that arises naturally at night (rātrisvabhāvaprabhavā), which he calls the “nurse of all beings” (Sūtrasthāna 21.58–59). Sleeping by day is generally discouraged, except in the hot season and for the weak, the elderly and those exhausted by labour or travel; staying awake at night is said to cause dryness and sleeping by day unctuousness. For sleeplessness Caraka recommends measures such as massage, bathing, nourishing foods with milk, a comfortable bed and pleasant surroundings; for excessive sleep, fasting, purification and activity.

    Caraka’s explanations are framed in terms of the doṣas and guṇas, and his remedies have not been tested as he describes them. Some of his advice — regular hours, a calm environment — overlaps with the commonsense elements of cognitive-behavioural therapy for insomnia, the treatment best supported by modern evidence; others of its elements, such as deliberately restricting time in bed, have no classical counterpart. Traditional advice of this kind needs testing rather than assuming.

    Health and Safety Note

    Persistent insomnia, excessive daytime sleepiness, loud snoring with breathing pauses and similar problems can be signs of medical or psychiatric conditions that need professional assessment. Herbal sedatives sold as traditional remedies can interact with medicines and may be contaminated; see Chapter 3.

    7.8 Sleep in Monastic Discipline¶

    The Buddhist and Jain monastic codes regulated sleep as part of the discipline of the mind. Buddhist monks were taught to sleep in the “lion’s posture” (sīhaseyyā), lying on the right side with one foot on the other, “mindful and clearly comprehending”, having noted the time to rise; the Buddha is described as dividing the night into three watches and sleeping only in the middle one. Over-sleeping and attachment to the pleasure of sleep are listed among the things that hinder a monk’s progress, and “wakefulness” (jāgariyā) is praised as one of the disciplines of the path. The Visuddhimagga recommends particular meditations for those troubled by drowsiness. These rules show that sleep, like eating, was treated as an occasion for mindfulness and restraint rather than as a private matter outside the religious life.

    7.9 Sleep and Dream in Yoga¶

    The Yogasūtra, as noted in Chapter 3, counts dreamless sleep among the fluctuations of the mind: it is “the fluctuation that rests on the cognition of absence” (YS 1.10). Vyāsa’s commentary argues that sleep must be a cognition, since on waking one remembers it: “I slept well; my mind is clear”, or “I slept badly; my mind is dull and wandering”. Sleep is thus a fluctuation of the mind to be stilled like any other. The Advaitins, too, held that deep sleep is an experience later remembered, but for them it is an experience of the witness, of bliss and of ignorance, in which the mind itself is in abeyance (Section 4.4); the contrast lies in where the experience is located and how it is valued, not in whether sleep is experienced at all. Patañjali also lists among the means of steadying the mind “taking as support the knowledge gained in dream and sleep” (svapnanidrājñānālambanaṃ vā, YS 1.38). The commentators understood this in different ways: as meditation on an auspicious image seen in a dream, or on the tranquillity experienced in sleep. In either case, the states of sleep were seen as material for contemplative practice.

    7.10 Yogic Sleep¶

    The term yoganidrā, “yogic sleep”, has several meanings. In the Purāṇas it is the cosmic sleep of Viṣṇu between creations, personified in the Devīmāhātmya as the Goddess. In some medieval Haṭha and Rāja Yoga texts it denotes a state of meditative absorption. The modern practice called “Yoga Nidra” is different again: a guided relaxation technique developed by Swami Satyananda Saraswati of the Bihar School of Yoga and published in 1976 (Satyananda Saraswati, Swami 1976), which combines a resolution (saṅkalpa), the rotation of attention through the body (adapted, according to its creator, from the Tantric rite of nyāsa), awareness of the breath, the evocation of opposite sensations and guided visualisation. The practice is widely taught and is used in some clinical settings; the evidence for its effects is discussed in Chapter 13. It should be understood as a modern synthesis drawing on traditional elements, not as an ancient technique preserved unchanged.

    7.11 Dream Yoga¶

    The most systematic Indian-derived practice of awareness in dreams is the “dream yoga” (Tibetan rmi lam) taught among the Six Doctrines attributed to the Indian adept Nāropa (11th century) and transmitted in Tibetan Buddhism. The practitioner learns to recognise dreams as dreams while dreaming, to transform their contents, and to understand all appearances, waking and dreaming, as mind-made; the practice is also regarded as preparation for the intermediate state (bar do) after death. Lucid dreaming — awareness that one is dreaming while the dream continues — was long regarded by Western psychologists as doubtful, until experiments in the late 1970s and early 1980s showed that dreamers in REM sleep could signal, by pre-arranged eye movements, that they had become lucid (LaBerge et al. 1981). It has since been studied in sleep laboratories, and some researchers have drawn on Tibetan practices for techniques of inducing it. The laboratory findings establish that lucid dreaming occurs; they say nothing about the doctrinal claims — about the illusory nature of all appearances or the preparation for death — that give the Tibetan practice its meaning. Here again, the tradition and the laboratory research have different aims, and the one does not validate the other.

    7.12 Between Death and Birth¶

    Several Indian traditions extended their analysis of the states of consciousness beyond sleep to death and rebirth. The Upaniṣads describe the departure of the self at death, gathering the senses into itself “as a caterpillar, reaching the end of a blade of grass, draws itself together” to step onto the next (BU 4.4.3). Buddhist schools disagreed about whether there is an “intermediate existence” (antarābhava) between death and rebirth. The Sarvāstivādins, as set out in the Abhidharmakośa (chapter 3), held that there is: a subtle being, nourished by smells, that seeks its next birth. Vasubandhu records several opinions on how long it lasts — some masters held seven days, others seven weeks, others that there is no fixed limit and it lasts until the conditions for rebirth come together (Vasubandhu 1988–1990) — and the figure of forty-nine days later became standard in Tibetan teaching. The Theravādins denied the intermediate existence, holding that the last moment of consciousness in one life is followed immediately by the first moment of the next. The Tibetan teachings on the intermediate states (bar do), which include the states of dream, meditation and dying, developed the Indian Buddhist idea in elaborate detail. These doctrines belong to the religious cosmology of their traditions; they show how far the analysis of states of consciousness was extended, and why dream and sleep could be seen as rehearsals for death.

    7.13 Dreams and the Modern Reader¶

    Modern dream research offers no single theory to set against the Indian ones. Freud’s view of dreams as disguised wish-fulfilment, Jung’s view of dreams as compensating messages from the unconscious, and later neuroscientific theories that relate dreaming to memory consolidation, emotional processing or the simulation of threats have all had their advocates, and none commands universal assent. Against this background, some of the Indian classifications look remarkably sensible — Caraka’s recognition that most dreams rework the day’s experiences, wishes and imaginings, and the Milindapañha’s list of bodily, habitual and other sources of dreams — while others, such as the prognostic interpretation of dream images, belong to a world of omens that modern science does not share. Reading the texts well means recognising both.

    7.14 Comparing the Traditions¶

    Table 7.2. Approaches to dreams in Indian traditions.
    Tradition Key texts View of dreams
    Upaniṣads Bṛhadāraṇyaka 4.3; Chāndogya 8.7–12; Māṇḍūkya Evidence of the self’s creative, self-luminous nature; a stage on the way to the self
    Advaita Gauḍapāda; Śaṅkara on BS 2.2.29 For Gauḍapāda, proof of the world’s unreality; for Śaṅkara, a lower order of reality than waking
    Yogācāra Vasubandhu, Viṃśatikā Proof that experience does not require external objects
    Āyurveda Carakasaṃhitā, Indriyasthāna 5 Seven kinds; dreams of disease and omens of death
    Buddhist narrative Milindapañha; Aṅguttara 5.196 Six kinds of dreamer; prophetic dreams
    Jain Lives of the Jinas Auspicious dreams of the Tīrthaṅkaras’ mothers
    Omen literature Svapnacintāmaṇi Classification of auspicious and inauspicious dreams
    Islamic and Sufi Ḥadīth collections; Ibn Sīnā; dream books Three kinds of dream; the true dream as symbolic knowledge; dreams in initiation and guidance
    Tibetan Buddhism Six Doctrines of Nāropa Recognition and transformation of dreams in meditation

    What unites the approaches compared in Table 7.2 is the conviction that dreams matter — as evidence about the self, as signs, or as an opportunity for practice. On what dreams are, where they come from and what they show, the traditions part company. A careful history of Indian thought about dreams should resist both the temptation to read modern dream science into the texts and the temptation to dismiss the texts as superstition. They record serious attempts, within their own frameworks, to make sense of a universal and puzzling experience.
