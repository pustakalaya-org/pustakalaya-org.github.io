    Preface¶

    This is Volume 10 of the series Bhāratīya Jñāna Paramparā: Śabda Vidyā: Language, Grammar and Scripts. It surveys the sciences of language developed in India, as far as possible in the order in which they arose: the phonetics and recitation techniques that preserved the Vedas, the theory of metre and its mathematics, Yāska’s explanation of words and the lexicographical tradition that followed it; Pāṇini’s grammar, its commentaries, its rivals and the language it describes; the argument of Patañjali and the philosophy of Bhartṛhari; the grammars of Pali, the Prakrits, Apabhraṃśa and Sinhala, and the first descriptions of the modern Indo-Aryan languages; the Tamil Tolkāppiyam and the later grammars of Tamil, Kannada, Telugu and Malayalam; and the history of writing from the undeciphered Indus script to Brāhmī, its many descendants and the Perso-Arabic scripts. Two final chapters follow these traditions into Indo-Persian scholarship, colonial and comparative philology, modern linguistics and computational work on Sanskrit.

    Quotations from primary texts are given in standard transliteration with their sources. Dates are given as the ranges accepted in current scholarship (Pāṇini, for instance, is placed in the 4th century BCE, with some scholars preferring the 5th), and traditional dates are labelled as such. Popular myths, such as the claim that Sanskrit is the “mother of all languages” or that it has been found ideal for computers, are corrected briefly where they arise and examined together in the last chapter. References to standard editions, translations and studies are given in the text and collected in the select bibliography. A glossary and a chronology follow.

    The emphasis falls on Sanskrit, because that is where the richest pre-modern linguistic literature survives; but the volume tries to give the Middle Indo-Aryan, Dravidian, Persian and other traditions their due, to recognise the many language families of South Asia, and to describe honestly the social setting in which learning about language was pursued, including the restrictions of caste and gender that shaped it. Every quotation should, before scholarly use, be checked against a critical edition.

    Nikhil Kumar Rajput, Ramanujan College, University of Delhi, Guru Pūrṇimā, 2026
