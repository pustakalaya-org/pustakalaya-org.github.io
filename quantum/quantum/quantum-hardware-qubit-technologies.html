    Chapter 4: Quantum Hardware & Qubit Technologies¶

    This chapter opens the box: what physically counts as a qubit, and how companies build machines from superconducting circuits, trapped ions, neutral atoms, photons, silicon spins, topological wires and more. It covers the refrigerators, vacuum systems, lasers and control electronics that make qubits work, the numbers used to judge hardware, and the 2026-2030 roadmaps — with a sceptic's toolkit for reading them.

    4.1 What Makes a Good Qubit: DiVincenzo Criteria and Modalities¶

    In 2000 David DiVincenzo listed five requirements any quantum computer must meet, plus two more for quantum communication. Every hardware platform — superconducting circuits, trapped ions, neutral atoms, photons, spins in silicon — is a different trade-off against these criteria, and as of 2026 no single platform has won.

    Three leading modalities at a glance (2026)

    Superconducting Trapped ion Neutral atom
    Qubit Josephson-junction circuit Internal levels of an ion Internal levels of an atom
    Environment ~10 mK dilution fridge Ultra-high vacuum, lasers Ultra-high vacuum, laser tweezers
    Two-qubit gate time ~20-300 ns ~10-500 µs ~0.2-1 µs (plus slow moves)
    Best two-qubit fidelity ~99.7-99.9% ~99.9-99.99% ~99.5%+
    Connectivity Nearest neighbour on chip All-to-all in a module Reconfigurable by moving atoms
    Scale in 2026 ~100-160 per chip ~100 per system 1,000-6,000 trapped atoms

    Each platform wins on different rows; typical values, not records.

    Think of it like…

    Choosing a qubit technology is like choosing a material for a bridge: steel, concrete and timber all carry loads, but each trades strength, cost, speed of construction and maintenance differently.

    Real-world example

    Lina, founder of the fictional start-up Qubitline, compares platforms for a chemistry product. Superconducting qubits run two-qubit gates in tens of nanoseconds but need 10 mK refrigerators; trapped ions reach 99.9%+ fidelity with all-to-all links but gates take tens to hundreds of microseconds; neutral atoms scale to thousands of qubits but cycle more slowly. She picks partners for each stage of her roadmap.

    How it works¶

    1. A scalable physical system with well-characterised qubits.
    2. The ability to initialise qubits to a simple state such as |00…0⟩.
    3. Coherence times much longer than gate times.
    4. A universal set of quantum gates.
    5. Qubit-specific measurement.
    6. For networking: convert between stationary and flying (photonic) qubits.
    7. For networking: transmit flying qubits faithfully between locations.

    Who uses it

    Hardware engineers, physicists, investors and product managers comparing technologies; it is a standard interview and exam question.

    Key takeaway

    Memorise '5 + 2' criteria and the key figure of merit: coherence time divided by gate time — e.g. 100 µs / 50 ns ≈ 2,000 operations before the qubit forgets.

    Going deeper

    • DiVincenzo's criteria appeared in 'The Physical Implementation of Quantum Computation' (Fortschritte der Physik, 2000); the two networking criteria anticipate today's modular and distributed architectures.
    • Modern additions to the list include connectivity, crosstalk, leakage out of the qubit subspace, fabrication yield, control-wiring scalability, speed of the error-correction cycle and compatibility with a practical code.
    • Natural qubits (ions, atoms) are identical by physics, so no two differ; fabricated qubits (superconducting, spins) vary from device to device and need individual calibration and frequency allocation.
    • Speed matters for useful runtime: an error-correction cycle of about 1 µs (superconducting) versus about 1 ms (ions, atoms) means a thousand-fold difference in wall-clock time unless slow platforms parallelise more.
    • As of 2026 the DARPA Quantum Benchmarking Initiative's Stage B spans superconducting (IBM, Nord Quantique), ions (IonQ, Quantinuum), atoms (Atom Computing, QuEra), spins (Diraq, Quantum Motion, SQC) and photonics (Xanadu, Photonic Inc.).
    • Stationary-to-flying conversion is hardest for superconducting qubits, whose microwave photons cannot travel through room-temperature fibre; microwave-to-optical transducers remain inefficient research devices.

    Common mistakes & misconceptions

    • Comparing raw qubit counts across modalities: 1,000 trapped atoms and 156 superconducting qubits differ hugely in speed, fidelity and connectivity.
    • Judging a platform by one number such as coherence time; what matters is coherence relative to gate time, plus fidelity and scalability.
    • Assuming the 'winner' is already decided; companies and DARPA are still funding five or more modalities in parallel.

    Try it: Rough 'operations per coherence time' for typical 2025-26 devices¶

    devices = {  # (typical T2 in seconds, typical two-qubit gate time in seconds) - rough orders of magnitude
    'transmon':     (150e-6, 60e-9),
    'trapped ion':  (1.0,    200e-6),
    'neutral atom': (1.0,    0.5e-6),   # but atom moves and readout take ~0.1-1 ms
}
for name, (t2, tg) in devices.items():
    print(f'{name:13s} ~{t2 / tg:,.0f} gate times per T2')

    Crosstalk
    Error type · Concept · Current · Intermediate    		 Unwanted interaction where driving or measuring one qubit disturbs another, often worse when many operate simultaneously. Benchmarks report 'simultaneous' gate errors because isolated-pair numbers hide crosstalk.
    Modular architecture
    Scaling strategy · Concept · Emerging · Intermediate    		 Building large machines from many smaller modules linked by couplers or photonic interconnects. IBM's roadmap links Kookaburra modules with on-chip and inter-chip couplers; IonQ and Quantinuum plan photonic links.

    4.2 Superconducting Qubits: Transmons, Fluxonium and Couplers¶

    Superconducting qubits are tiny electrical circuits on a chip, made from metals such as aluminium, niobium or tantalum and cooled to about 10 millikelvin so current flows without resistance. A Josephson junction makes the circuit's energy levels unevenly spaced, so its two lowest levels can be used as a qubit and controlled with microwave pulses.

    Think of it like…

    A transmon is like a guitar string tuned so the overtones are slightly out of step: you can pluck the first note cleanly without accidentally sounding the next one. The Josephson junction is the clever fret that makes the spacing uneven.

    Real-world example

    Carla, a hardware engineer at the fictional Northlight Quantum, characterises a new transmon: frequency 4.8 GHz, anharmonicity −220 MHz, T1 = 120 µs and echo T2 = 150 µs. A 25 ns single-qubit pulse shaped with DRAG reaches 99.95% fidelity, and a tunable coupler lets her switch the interaction with its neighbour on and off.

    How it works¶

    1. A Josephson junction (Al / aluminium oxide / Al) plus a capacitor forms a nonlinear oscillator.
    2. The two lowest levels, typically 4-6 GHz apart for a transmon, become |0⟩ and |1⟩.
    3. Shaped microwave pulses on a drive line rotate the qubit; DRAG shaping avoids leaking into |2⟩.
    4. Two-qubit gates use couplers: tunable-coupler CZ, cross-resonance or parametric iSWAP-type gates.
    5. Dispersive readout: a resonator's frequency shifts with the qubit state; the weak signal is boosted by a quantum-limited amplifier and a HEMT.

    Who uses it

    Device physicists, microwave engineers, fabrication and materials scientists at IBM, Google, Rigetti, IQM, AWS and many start-ups and universities.

    Key takeaway

    Transmon = charge-insensitive (E_J/E_C ≈ 50 or more) at the cost of small anharmonicity (about −200 to −300 MHz); fluxonium = lower frequency and larger anharmonicity, holding the superconducting single-qubit fidelity record of 99.998% (MIT, 2025).

    Going deeper

    • The transmon (Koch et al., Yale, 2007) shunts the junction with a large capacitor so E_J/E_C ≳ 50, making charge noise exponentially weak; at about 5 GHz the thermal excited population at 10-20 mK is negligible.
    • Fluxonium (Manucharyan et al., 2009) adds a 'superinductor' of many junctions, giving qubit frequencies of hundreds of MHz, large anharmonicity and long T1; MIT reported 99.92% two-qubit and 99.998% single-qubit fidelities.
    • Coherence is limited mainly by two-level-system defects in surface oxides and interfaces; tantalum films on sapphire pushed T1 to about 0.3 ms (2021), and Aalto reported T1 up to about 0.67 ms and echo T2 above 1 ms (2025).
    • Two-qubit gate families: cross-resonance or echoed cross-resonance (older IBM Falcon and Eagle), tunable-coupler CZ (IBM Heron, Google), iSWAP-like fSim gates (Google RCS) and parametric gates (Rigetti); durations of roughly 20-300 ns.
    • Readout takes a few hundred nanoseconds to about 1 µs using a dispersively coupled resonator, a Josephson parametric or travelling-wave amplifier, and a HEMT amplifier at 4 K.
    • Correlated errors from cosmic rays and natural radioactivity create bursts of quasiparticles affecting many qubits at once (McEwen et al., Nature Physics 2022); gap engineering and shielding mitigate them.

    Common mistakes & misconceptions

    • Confusing these with room-temperature superconductors: the metals superconduct only near absolute zero, and qubits additionally need millikelvin temperatures.
    • Judging qubit quality by T1 alone; two-qubit gate fidelity, readout error, leakage and crosstalk matter just as much.
    • Forgetting frequency crowding: on large chips neighbouring qubits must avoid collisions, which complicates fabrication and calibration.

    Try it: Estimate a transmon's frequency and anharmonicity from E_J and E_C¶

    import numpy as np
EJ, EC = 15.0, 0.25                 # GHz; EJ/EC = 60 is typical for a transmon
f01 = np.sqrt(8 * EJ * EC) - EC     # qubit transition frequency
alpha = -EC                         # anharmonicity f12 - f01
print(f'f01 = {f01:.2f} GHz, f12 = {f01 + alpha:.2f} GHz, anharmonicity = {alpha*1000:.0f} MHz')
# f01 = 5.23 GHz, f12 = 4.98 GHz, anharmonicity = -250 MHz

    Transmon
    Koch et al., Yale (2007) · Qubit type · Current · Beginner    		 A Josephson junction shunted by a large capacitor; the dominant superconducting qubit, insensitive to charge noise. IBM Heron and Google Willow both use transmon qubits at about 4-6 GHz.
    Fluxonium

    Anharmonicity
    Transmon physics · Concept · Current · Intermediate    		 The difference between the 1→2 and 0→1 transition frequencies; it lets pulses address only the qubit levels. With −250 MHz anharmonicity, pulses shorter than about 10 ns start exciting |2⟩ unless shaped.
    Tunable coupler
    Google Sycamore; IBM Heron; IQM; Rigetti · Hardware · Current · Intermediate    		 An extra circuit element whose frequency is adjusted to switch qubit-qubit interaction on for gates and off otherwise. IBM's Nighthawk links 120 qubits with 218 tunable couplers in a square lattice.
    Cross-resonance gate
    Rigetti & Devoret (2010); Chow et al., IBM (2011) · Gate · Legacy · Advanced    		 Driving one fixed-frequency qubit at its neighbour's frequency to create a ZX interaction, the basis of IBM's ECR gate. IBM Eagle processors used echoed cross-resonance (ECR) as their native two-qubit gate.
    Dispersive readout
    Circuit quantum electrodynamics (Blais et al., 2004) · Concept · Current · Intermediate    		 A resonator coupled to the qubit shifts frequency depending on the qubit state; probing it reveals 0 or 1. A 300 ns readout pulse at 7 GHz distinguishes the two states with about 99% assignment fidelity.
    Josephson parametric amplifier / TWPA
    Superconducting quantum-limited amplifiers · Hardware · Current · Advanced    		 Amplifiers adding close to the minimum noise allowed by quantum mechanics, the first stage of qubit readout. A travelling-wave parametric amplifier boosts readout signals of a whole chip over several GHz of bandwidth.
    DRAG pulse shaping
    Motzoi, Gambetta, Rebentrost & Wilhelm (2009) · Concept · Current · Advanced    		 Adds a derivative-shaped quadrature component to pulses to suppress leakage and phase errors in weakly anharmonic qubits. Calibrating the DRAG coefficient sharply reduces a 20 ns X gate's leakage into |2⟩ and its phase error.
    Two-level-system (TLS) defects
    Materials science · Concept · Current · Advanced    		 Microscopic defects in oxides and interfaces that absorb qubit energy and fluctuate over time, limiting T1. A qubit's T1 drops from 200 µs to 40 µs overnight when a TLS drifts into resonance with it.
    Tantalum-based qubits
    Place et al., Nature Communications (2021) · Hardware · Emerging · Advanced    		 Transmons with tantalum films on sapphire, whose cleaner oxides cut TLS losses and raised T1 toward 0.3-0.5 ms. Several labs switched from niobium to tantalum capacitor pads to roughly double coherence.
    Quasiparticle and cosmic-ray bursts
    McEwen et al., Nature Physics (2022) · Concept · Current · Advanced    		 High-energy particle impacts break Cooper pairs across the chip, causing correlated errors that error correction struggles with. Google observed chip-wide error bursts about every 10 seconds before redesigning chips to resist them.

    4.3 Superconducting Processors in 2026: IBM, Google, Rigetti, IQM and More¶

    Superconducting qubits are the most widely deployed gate-model technology, used by IBM, Google, Rigetti, IQM, China's USTC, Fujitsu and RIKEN, AWS and others. This section maps the actual chips available or announced by 2026 and what each one demonstrates.

    Think of it like…

    It is like a smartphone chip line-up: every vendor releases generations that trade core count (qubits), wiring (connectivity) and error rates, and the newest is not always best for every job.

    Real-world example

    Tomás, a graduate student, compares IBM's Heron r3 device ibm_boston (156 qubits, best 100-qubit EPLG about 0.2%) with the newer Nighthawk ibm_miami (120 qubits on a square lattice, early access in 2026). He then reads Google's Willow spec sheet: 105 qubits, mean CZ error 0.33% on the error-correction chip, 1.1 µs surface-code cycle.

    How it works¶

    1. Design the qubit lattice: IBM heavy-hex (degree 2-3), square lattices (degree 4) at Google, IBM Nighthawk and IQM.
    2. Fabricate multi-layer chips: qubit layer, wiring layers and bump bonds or through-silicon vias for signals.
    3. Package the chip and mount it on the mixing-chamber plate of a dilution refrigerator.
    4. Calibrate frequencies, pulses and couplers, typically at least daily.
    5. Benchmark with randomised benchmarking, layer fidelity or XEB, and publish the numbers.
    6. Expose the device through a cloud platform with a transpiler that knows its native gates and connectivity.

    Who uses it

    Quantum software developers choosing backends, hardware analysts, and engineers at IBM, Google Quantum AI, Rigetti, IQM, Fujitsu, AWS and national labs.

    Key takeaway

    Memorise: IBM Heron (133/156 qubits, heavy-hex, tunable couplers), IBM Nighthawk (120 qubits, 218 couplers, square lattice, 2025), Google Willow (105 qubits, December 2024), Rigetti Cepheus-1-108Q (generally available April 2026), Zuchongzhi 3.0 (105 qubits, 2025).

    Going deeper

    • IBM's line: Eagle (127, 2021), Osprey (433, 2022), Condor (1,121, 2023) and Heron r1 (133, 2023), r2 (156, 2024) and r3 (2025, e.g. ibm_pittsburgh and ibm_boston, best 100-qubit EPLG about 2.2×10⁻³).
    • IBM Nighthawk (announced November 2025, cloud early access from early 2026 as ibm_miami): 120 qubits, 218 tunable couplers on a square lattice, about 30% more complex circuits than Heron; IBM targets 7,500 two-qubit gates by end-2026 and 10,000 in 2027.
    • IBM Loon (2025) is an experimental processor testing qLDPC error-correction ingredients: long-range on-chip 'c-couplers', multi-layer routing and fast qubit reset.
    • Google Willow (December 2024): 105 qubits, average connectivity 3.47; the error-correction chip has mean CZ error 0.33%, readout error 0.77%, T1 about 68 µs and Λ = 2.14; the RCS chip has T1 about 98 µs and 0.14% iSWAP-like error.
    • Rigetti uses chiplets: Ankaa-3 (84 qubits, December 2024), Cepheus-1-36Q (four 9-qubit chiplets, 2025) and Cepheus-1-108Q (twelve chiplets, April 2026, 99.1% median two-qubit fidelity at launch, 99.5% targeted).
    • Others: IQM Radiance on-premises systems of 20, 54 or 150 qubits (150-qubit deliveries planned for 2026), Fujitsu and RIKEN's 256-qubit machine (2025), USTC's Zuchongzhi 3.0 (99.62% two-qubit fidelity), and D-Wave's 2026 move into gate models via Quantum Circuits.

    Common mistakes & misconceptions

    • Choosing a backend by qubit count: a 156-qubit device with lower errors beats a larger noisy one for almost every algorithm.
    • Trusting spec sheets for today's run: calibrations drift daily, so check the live properties of the device before submitting jobs.
    • Treating 'best pair' or 'best layer' numbers as typical; medians and full distributions describe what your circuit will meet.

    4.4 Trapped-Ion Quantum Computers¶

    Trapped-ion computers hold individual charged atoms (ions) in a vacuum using electric fields from a chip-shaped trap. Each qubit is stored in two very stable internal energy levels and driven by lasers or microwaves. Because every ion of a species is identical and ions can interact with any other through shared motion, ion machines offer the highest fidelities and all-to-all connectivity — but their gates are slower.

    Think of it like…

    Trapped ions are like beads on a string of invisible springs: pluck one bead and the shared vibration lets any bead talk to any other.

    Real-world example

    Jonas, a quant at the fictional Fenwick Capital, runs a 98-qubit, all-to-all circuit on Quantinuum's Helios (launched November 2025: 99.921% two-qubit and 99.9975% single-qubit fidelity). No SWAP gates are needed, so his compiled circuit uses far fewer two-qubit gates than on a nearest-neighbour chip — but each layer takes much longer to execute.

    How it works¶

    1. Ionise atoms (ytterbium, barium or calcium) and trap them with RF and DC voltages on a surface-electrode chip in ultra-high vacuum.
    2. Laser-cool the ions to near their motional ground state.
    3. Initialise each qubit by optical pumping.
    4. Single-qubit gates use laser Raman beams or microwaves; two-qubit gates (Mølmer-Sørensen or ZZ) use the ions' shared motion.
    5. In QCCD designs, shuttle ions between memory, gate and readout zones; recool with a second 'coolant' ion species.
    6. Read out by shining a laser and imaging state-dependent fluorescence.

    Who uses it

    Atomic physicists, laser and optics engineers, trap designers and software teams at Quantinuum, IonQ, AQT, Quantum Art and university labs.

    Key takeaway

    Ions' selling points: all-to-all connectivity and ≥ 99.9% two-qubit fidelity. Weak point: speed — gates take tens to hundreds of microseconds, and shuttling and recooling take longer still.

    Going deeper

    • Common species: ytterbium-171 (IonQ Aria and Forte, earlier Quantinuum H-series) and barium-137 (Quantinuum Helios, IonQ Tempo), using hyperfine 'clock' states whose coherence lasts seconds or longer; AQT uses calcium.
    • The quantum charge-coupled device (QCCD) architecture (Kielpinski, Monroe & Wineland, 2002) moves ions between zones. Quantinuum's H2 uses a 56-qubit racetrack, Helios a junction and ring design with 98 qubits, and Sol (2027) a 2D grid.
    • Records: Quantinuum H2 reached quantum volume 2^25 (September 2025); Helios reports 99.921% two-qubit fidelity across all pairs; IonQ reported 99.99% two-qubit fidelity in October 2025 on a prototype using electronic qubit control.
    • Electronic qubit control (from Oxford Ionics, acquired by IonQ in 2025) drives gates with microwave and oscillating fields from trap electrodes instead of gate lasers, so traps can be made in standard semiconductor fabs.
    • IonQ's line: Aria (25 qubits), Forte (36 qubits, #AQ 36) and Tempo (barium; #AQ 64 reached in 2025; 100-qubit systems ordered by KISTI in Korea), with a 256-qubit chip-based system targeted for late 2026.
    • Scaling challenges: transport and recooling consume most of the runtime, long ion chains suffer crowded motional modes, and going beyond a few hundred qubits per module needs photonic links between traps.

    Common mistakes & misconceptions

    • Assuming ions need no cryogenics: many systems cool the trap to cryogenic temperatures to improve vacuum and reduce heating.
    • Treating 'all-to-all' as free: connecting distant ions costs transport time in QCCD machines.
    • Comparing IonQ's #AQ directly with quantum volume or EPLG; they are different benchmarks, and IonQ itself is moving to physical and logical qubit counts.

    4.5 Neutral-Atom Quantum Computers¶

    Neutral-atom computers hold uncharged atoms — rubidium, caesium, ytterbium or strontium — in tightly focused laser beams called optical tweezers. Hundreds to thousands of atoms are arranged in arrays, entangled by briefly exciting them to giant 'Rydberg' states, and even moved around mid-computation to change which qubits can interact.

    Think of it like…

    It is like a chessboard where laser 'fingers' pick up pieces and place them anywhere; when two pieces sit side by side, they can be briefly 'inflated' so they feel each other and become entangled.

    Real-world example

    Ana, a physicist at the fictional Lakeside Labs, fires 1,000 tweezers into a cloud of cold rubidium atoms; about half catch an atom. A camera finds the gaps and moving tweezers rearrange the atoms into a defect-free 400-site grid in tens of milliseconds. She then runs parallel Rydberg CZ gates on 200 pairs at once with a single global laser pulse.

    How it works¶

    1. Laser-cool a cloud of atoms in a magneto-optical trap inside a vacuum cell.
    2. Load an array of optical tweezers made by spatial light modulators or acousto-optic deflectors; each site catches an atom about half the time.
    3. Image the array and rearrange atoms with moving tweezers into a defect-free pattern.
    4. Single-qubit gates use Raman lasers or microwaves; two-qubit CZ gates use the Rydberg blockade.
    5. Move atoms between zones to change connectivity, running many gates in parallel.
    6. Read out by fluorescence imaging, then replace lost atoms (continuous reloading).

    Who uses it

    Atomic, molecular and optical physicists, laser engineers and software teams at QuEra, Pasqal, Atom Computing, Infleqtion, Google Quantum AI and academic groups at Harvard, MIT, Caltech and elsewhere.

    Key takeaway

    Strengths: scale (thousands of atoms) and reconfigurable connectivity. Weaknesses: slow cycles (moves and readout take hundreds of microseconds to milliseconds) and atom loss. Rydberg CZ fidelity reached about 99.5% in 2023.

    Going deeper

    • Rydberg blockade (Jaksch et al., 2000; Lukin et al., 2001): an atom excited to a high-n Rydberg state shifts neighbours' levels within several micrometres, so only one can be excited — the basis of CZ gates.
    • In 2023 Harvard and QuEra reported 99.5% fidelity for parallel CZ gates (Evered et al., Nature), and in December 2023 ran algorithms on up to 48 logical qubits (Bluvstein et al., Nature 2024).
    • Scale records (Nature, 2025): Caltech trapped 6,100 caesium qubits with about 12.6 s coherence and 99.98% single-qubit fidelity; Harvard and MIT ran 3,000+ qubits continuously for two hours, reloading up to 300,000 atoms per second.
    • Commercial players: QuEra (analogue Aquila on Amazon Braket; Gemini-class digital system installed at Japan's AIST in 2025), Pasqal (roadmap toward a 250-qubit advantage demonstrator and 10,000 atoms by 2030), Atom Computing (with Microsoft) and Infleqtion.
    • Infleqtion became the first public neutral-atom company on the NYSE (ticker INFQ, February 2026) and reported 30 entangled logical qubits on 80 atoms in 2026; Google Quantum AI launched its own neutral-atom team in Boulder in March 2026.
    • Erasure conversion with ytterbium or strontium turns many errors into detectable atom losses, which error-correcting codes handle more efficiently than unknown errors.

    Common mistakes & misconceptions

    • Counting trapped atoms as working qubits: an array of 6,100 atoms is not a 6,100-qubit processor with full gate control.
    • Confusing analogue and digital modes: Aquila emulates one Hamiltonian family and is not a universal gate-model computer.
    • Reading logical-qubit headlines without checking whether errors were corrected or merely detected and discarded (post-selection).

    4.6 Photonic Quantum Computers¶

    Photonic quantum computers encode qubits in particles of light — in their path, polarisation or arrival time, or in 'squeezed' light. Photons barely interact with their surroundings, so they travel well and keep coherence, but they also barely interact with each other: entangling gates rely on clever measurements ('fusion'), and losing photons is the main enemy.

    Think of it like…

    It is like sending messages by paper aeroplanes: they fly well and do not get damaged by weather, but they cannot interact mid-air, so you catch pairs at a checkpoint and compare them (fusion) — and every plane that is lost takes its information with it.

    Real-world example

    Leah, an engineer at the fictional Prism Networks, studies Xanadu's Aurora (Nature, January 2025): 12 qubits built from 35 photonic chips linked by 13 km of optical fibre across four server racks. She notes that the impressive modularity is offset by a hard requirement — fault tolerance needs optical losses far below today's levels.

    How it works¶

    1. Generate photons: single photons from quantum dots, or squeezed light from on-chip resonators.
    2. Route them through integrated waveguides with beam splitters and phase shifters (programmable interferometers).
    3. Entangle photons by fusion measurements, which join small resource states into large cluster states.
    4. Detect photons with superconducting nanowire or transition-edge detectors and switch later paths based on the results (feed-forward).
    5. Encode error-correctable states (fusion-based codes or GKP states) and network modules together by fibre.

    Who uses it

    Integrated-photonics engineers, quantum-optics physicists, detector specialists and foundry partners such as GlobalFoundries, plus teams at PsiQuantum, Xanadu, Quandela, ORCA and QuiX.

    Key takeaway

    Remember three approaches: PsiQuantum — fusion-based quantum computing on GlobalFoundries silicon photonics (Omega chipset, 2025); Xanadu — squeezed light and GKP qubits; Quandela — quantum-dot single photons.

    Going deeper

    • Loss is the defining metric: fault-tolerant photonic architectures need very low loss per photon across sources, chips, switches, fibre and detectors, and every lost photon is an error the code must catch.
    • PsiQuantum unveiled its Omega chipset in February 2025, made at GlobalFoundries; it raised $1 billion in September 2025, is building sites in Brisbane and Chicago, and is one of two companies (with Microsoft) in DARPA QBI Stage C.
    • Xanadu: Borealis (2022) showed Gaussian boson sampling with 216 squeezed modes; Aurora (2025) networked 35 chips; the company also generated GKP states on chip and listed on Nasdaq and the TSX via a SPAC in 2026.
    • Quandela (France) builds quantum-dot single-photon sources; its 12-qubit Belenos launched in 2025 and the 24-qubit Canopus was planned for 2026; a system is destined for France's TGCC computing centre under EuroHPC.
    • Superconducting nanowire single-photon detectors (SNSPDs) exceed 90% efficiency but operate at about 1-3 K, so 'room-temperature photonics' still needs cryostats for detection.
    • Photonic computing follows the measurement-based model: build a large entangled cluster state, then compute purely by measuring photons, with outcomes steering later measurements.

    Common mistakes & misconceptions

    • Saying photonic computers need no cryogenics: high-efficiency single-photon detectors and some sources must be cooled.
    • Equating boson-sampling advantage with universal computing: Borealis and Jiuzhang cannot run Shor or general algorithms.
    • Ignoring how loss compounds: a 1% loss per component across 100 components leaves only about 37% of photons.

def perm(M):                                   # matrix permanent (tiny matrices only)
    n = len(M)
    return sum(np.prod([M[i, p[i]] for i in range(n)]) for p in itertools.permutations(range(n)))
BS = np.array([[1, 1j], [1j, 1]]) / np.sqrt(2)   # 50:50 beam splitter
print('P(one photon in each output) =', round(abs(perm(BS))**2, 6))   # 0.0 -> photons bunch
# Boson sampling scales this up: output probabilities are permanents of large matrices

    4.7 Semiconductor Spin Qubits¶

    Spin qubits use the spin of single electrons (or holes) trapped in tiny silicon or germanium 'quantum dots', or bound to donor atoms such as phosphorus. They are made with the same tools and 300 mm wafers as computer chips and are about 100 nanometres across, so in principle millions could fit on one chip. The challenges are device-to-device variation, wiring density and keeping fidelity high at scale.

    Think of it like…

    Picture tiny compass needles locked in silicon parking spaces: a magnet sets which way is north, microwave nudges flip them, and two neighbouring needles can talk when the wall between their parking spaces is lowered.

    Real-world example

    Kwame, a device engineer at the fictional Siltech Quantum, tests a wafer of Si-MOS quantum dots. He loads one electron per dot, applies a 1 tesla field to split spin up and down by about 28 GHz, flips spins with microwave pulses, and pulses the gate between two dots to switch on their exchange coupling for a two-qubit gate.

    How it works¶

    1. Define quantum dots with gate electrodes on silicon/silicon-germanium or silicon-MOS, ideally using isotopically purified silicon-28.
    2. Load exactly one electron into each dot and apply a magnetic field to split the spin states (Zeeman splitting).
    3. Drive single-qubit gates with microwaves: ESR from an antenna, or EDSR using a micromagnet.
    4. Make two-qubit gates by pulsing the exchange coupling J between neighbouring dots.
    5. Read out by spin-to-charge conversion (Pauli spin blockade) detected with a nearby charge sensor and RF reflectometry.
    6. Operate at about 0.1-1 K; some 'hot' qubits work above 1 K, where cooling power is far greater.

    Who uses it

    Semiconductor device engineers, nanofabrication specialists and cryo-CMOS designers at Intel, Diraq, Quantum Motion, Silicon Quantum Computing, Quobly, HRL, QuTech and imec.

    Key takeaway

    Key ingredients to memorise: silicon-28 enrichment for long coherence, exchange gates for two qubits, Pauli spin blockade for readout, and 300 mm manufacturing as the big selling point.

    Going deeper

    • Variants: Si/SiGe electron dots (Intel, QuTech), Si-MOS dots (Diraq, Quantum Motion, Quobly), phosphorus donors placed with atomic precision (Silicon Quantum Computing), germanium hole spins (QuTech) and exchange-only qubits (HRL).
    • Intel's Tunnel Falls (2023) is a 12-qubit chip fabricated on 300 mm wafers; a 2024 Nature paper reported wafer-scale cryogenic testing of single-electron devices with 99.9% single-qubit fidelity.
    • Diraq and imec (Nature, 2025) measured randomly selected Si-MOS devices from imec's 300 mm line with above 99% single- and two-qubit fidelity and above 99.9% state preparation and measurement.
    • Quantum Motion installed a full-stack silicon CMOS quantum computer in three server racks at the UK's National Quantum Computing Centre in 2025, then raised about $160 million in 2026; it is in DARPA QBI Stage B with Diraq and SQC.
    • Silicon-28 enrichment removes the nuclear spins of silicon-29 that otherwise cause dephasing, pushing coherence from microseconds to milliseconds or longer.
    • Open problems include valley splitting in Si/SiGe, charge noise from interface defects, qubit-to-qubit variability, and fanning out wires to qubits spaced only about 100 nm apart.

    Common mistakes & misconceptions

    • Assuming CMOS compatibility makes scaling easy: variability and wiring density are the real bottlenecks.
    • Confusing silicon spin qubits with NV centres in diamond, a different defect-based technology mainly used for sensing.
    • Comparing today's spin-qubit chips (about 12 qubits) with atom or ion systems on qubit count rather than on manufacturing potential.

    4.8 Topological Qubits and the Majorana Debate¶

    A topological qubit would store information non-locally, shared between exotic quasiparticles called Majorana zero modes, so that local noise cannot easily damage it. Microsoft has pursued this for about 20 years and announced Majorana 1 (February 2025) and Majorana 2 (June 2026), but many physicists say a working topological qubit has not yet been demonstrated.

    Think of it like…

    It is like protecting a secret by tearing the note in half and hiding the halves in two distant houses: a burglar in one house learns nothing. The catch is that you must first prove the two halves really exist.

    Real-world example

    Sofia, a physics student, reads both sides. Microsoft says Majorana 2 uses lead instead of aluminium and shows parity lifetimes of about 22 seconds — over 1,000 times longer than Majorana 1. Critics note the data come from one wire of one device, only one measurement basis (Z) is shown, and the paper was not yet peer reviewed — so 'long-lived parity' is not yet a demonstrated qubit.

    How it works¶

    1. Build a nanowire from a semiconductor (indium arsenide) coated with a superconductor and apply a magnetic field.
    2. Tune voltages into the predicted topological phase, checked with a 'topological gap protocol'.
    3. Majorana zero modes appear at the wire ends; together a pair shares one fermion parity (even or odd).
    4. A 'tetron' joins two wires so four Majoranas encode one qubit.
    5. Measure parities with quantum dots and fast RF reflectometry; sequences of parity measurements replace braiding.
    6. Add magic states for non-Clifford gates, because braiding alone is not universal.

    Who uses it

    Condensed-matter physicists, materials scientists, Microsoft Quantum and its critics, DARPA programme managers and analysts weighing long-term roadmaps.

    Key takeaway

    Distinguish a long parity lifetime (one basis, Z) from a full qubit, which needs coherent superpositions and X-basis measurements too — the heart of the critics' objections.

    Going deeper

    • Majorana zero modes are non-Abelian anyons: exchanging (braiding) them changes the encoded state in a way that depends only on topology, but braiding gives only Clifford gates, so magic states are still required.
    • Majorana 1 (February 2025) used an indium arsenide/aluminium 'topoconductor' and was designed to hold eight qubits; the accompanying Nature paper reported parity measurements but its editors stated it was not evidence of Majorana zero modes.
    • Critics, notably Henry Legg, argued the topological gap protocol can produce false positives from ordinary quantum-dot states and, in a 2026 Nature critique, questioned the tuning procedures and data selection; Microsoft rejects these criticisms.
    • Majorana 2 (June 2026) switched to lead, raising the superconducting gap from about 0.3 to about 1.3 meV, and reported Z-parity lifetimes of 22 ± 1 s in one wire of one tetron, versus 1-12 ms for Majorana 1.
    • Microsoft moved its target for a scalable machine from 2033 to 2029; through the DARPA US2QC programme it is one of two companies (with PsiQuantum) in the final Stage C of the Quantum Benchmarking Initiative.
    • Separately, Google (2023) and Quantinuum (2024) created and braided non-Abelian anyons as encoded states on ordinary qubits — simulated topological order, not a material-based topological qubit.

    Common mistakes & misconceptions

    • Saying Microsoft has a working topological quantum computer: as of 2026 the evidence for even one topological qubit is disputed.
    • Assuming topological means error-free: such qubits would still need error correction and magic-state distillation for universal computing.
    • Mixing up simulated anyons on qubit processors with material Majorana qubits; they demonstrate different things.

    4.9 NV Centres and Other Qubit Modalities¶

    Beyond the big five platforms, many other qubits exist: nitrogen-vacancy (NV) centres in diamond, colour centres in silicon, bosonic qubits stored in microwave cavities, nuclear spins in molecules (NMR), electrons floating on solid neon or liquid helium, and rare-earth ions in crystals. Many shine in sensing or networking rather than large-scale computing.

    Think of it like…

    They are like specialist vehicles — tractors, ambulances, racing cars: none replaces the family car, but each wins at its own job.

    Real-world example

    At the fictional Harbor Biotech, Dr Okafor uses an NV-diamond microscope at room temperature to map tiny magnetic fields from batteries. Meanwhile Photonic Inc. builds silicon T-centre qubits that emit telecom-band photons for networking, and Nord Quantique stores an error-correctable qubit inside a single superconducting cavity.

    How it works¶

    1. NV centre: a nitrogen atom beside a missing carbon atom in diamond traps electrons with spin 1.
    2. A green laser (about 532 nm) initialises the spin, and the brightness of red fluorescence reveals its state.
    3. Microwaves near 2.87 GHz rotate the spin; nearby carbon-13 nuclei serve as long-lived memory qubits.
    4. Distant NV centres are entangled by interfering emitted photons, linking network nodes.
    5. Other modalities follow the same pattern: find a well-isolated two-level system, a way to control it and a way to read it.

    Who uses it

    Quantum-sensing engineers, network researchers, materials scientists and start-ups such as Quantum Brilliance, Photonic Inc., Nord Quantique, Alice & Bob and EeroQ.

    Key takeaway

    NV centres are the flagship of room-temperature quantum sensing, not of large-scale quantum computing; NMR was historically important but cannot scale.

    Going deeper

    • NV centres have a 2.87 GHz zero-field splitting and are read by optically detected magnetic resonance; in isotopically pure diamond their spin coherence reaches milliseconds at room temperature.
    • Delft linked NV centres in a loophole-free Bell test (2015) and a three-node quantum network (Science, 2021), making NVs a leading quantum-network testbed.
    • Liquid-state NMR ran Shor's algorithm on the number 15 in 2001 with 7 qubits, but its ensemble 'pseudo-pure' states lose signal exponentially as qubits are added, so it is a historical platform.
    • Bosonic qubits store information in many photon levels of a superconducting cavity: cat qubits (Alice & Bob, AWS Ocelot), GKP states (Nord Quantique) and dual-rail qubits (Quantum Circuits, acquired by D-Wave in 2026).
    • Photonic Inc. (Canada) uses T-centres in silicon, which emit near 1,326 nm in the telecom O-band, as spin-photon interfaces for distributed computing; it is in DARPA QBI Stage B.
    • Electrons on solid neon (Argonne-led, Nature 2022) showed long coherence on an ultra-clean surface; rare-earth ions such as erbium and europium in crystals are studied as long-lived quantum memories for repeaters.

    Common mistakes & misconceptions

    • Believing 'room-temperature quantum computer' headlines about NV devices with a handful of qubits; they are sensors or small accelerators.
    • Treating NMR demonstrations as scalable quantum computing; the ensemble approach fails beyond about a dozen qubits.
    • Forgetting that bosonic qubits are still superconducting circuits that need dilution refrigerators.

    4.10 Quantum Annealers: D-Wave and Ising Machines¶

    Quantum annealers, built mainly by D-Wave, are special-purpose analogue machines: thousands of superconducting flux qubits slowly evolve from a simple starting state toward the lowest-energy state of a programmed optimisation problem. They are not universal gate-model computers, and a quantum speed-up on real optimisation problems remains unproven, but they are used commercially and as simulators of magnetic materials.

    Think of it like…

    It is like dropping a ball onto a bumpy landscape and shaking the table less and less so the ball settles into a deep valley — with quantum tunnelling sometimes letting it slip through thin hills instead of climbing over them.

    Real-world example

    Ravi, a planner at the fictional Harbourline Freight, maps a 300-truck routing problem to a QUBO and submits it to D-Wave's Leap hybrid solver. He gets good schedules in seconds, but before buying more time he benchmarks Gurobi and a tuned simulated-annealing code on the same instances — essential, because classical solvers are strong competitors.

    How it works¶

    1. Formulate the problem as a QUBO or Ising model: minimise Σh_i s_i + ΣJ_ij s_i s_j.
    2. Embed logical variables onto the hardware graph (Zephyr on Advantage2), using chains of physical qubits when needed.
    3. Anneal: the transverse field is reduced while the problem Hamiltonian is turned up, over microseconds to milliseconds.
    4. Read out all spins; repeat thousands of times to collect low-energy samples.
    5. Unembed, post-process and keep the best solutions; hybrid solvers mix quantum samples with classical heuristics.

    Who uses it

    Operations researchers, logistics and scheduling teams, materials physicists and D-Wave's commercial customers in manufacturing, telecoms and finance.

    Key takeaway

    Remember: Advantage (2020) has 5,000+ qubits with 15-way Pegasus connectivity; Advantage2 (generally available May 2025) has 4,400+ qubits with 20-way Zephyr connectivity — and neither count is comparable with gate-model qubits.

    Going deeper

    • D-Wave's history: D-Wave One (2011, 128 qubits), D-Wave 2000Q (2017), Advantage (2020, 5,000+ qubits, Pegasus 15-way) and Advantage2 (general availability 20 May 2025, 4,400+ qubits, Zephyr 20-way).
    • D-Wave reports Advantage2 offers about 40% higher energy scale, 75% lower noise and doubled coherence compared with Advantage; it is offered through the Leap cloud and for on-premises purchase.
    • Minor embedding maps each logical variable to a chain of strongly coupled physical qubits; dense problems need long chains, and Zephyr's higher degree shortens them and frees qubits.
    • In Science (2025), D-Wave claimed beyond-classical simulation of quench dynamics in spin glasses; a 2026 Flatiron Institute and Boston University tensor-network study reproduced key cases, which D-Wave disputes.
    • D-Wave also moved into gate-model computing: it demonstrated scalable on-chip cryogenic control in January 2026, agreed to buy Quantum Circuits, and targeted initial gate-model availability during 2026.
    • Quantum-inspired 'Ising machines' — Fujitsu's Digital Annealer and NTT's coherent Ising machine — tackle the same QUBOs with classical or optical hardware and are useful baselines.

    Common mistakes & misconceptions

    • Comparing D-Wave's 4,400 qubits with IBM's 156 as if they were the same kind of qubit.
    • Believing annealing solves NP-hard problems exactly or exponentially faster; it is a heuristic sampler.
    • Forgetting that quantum-inspired annealers such as Fujitsu's Digital Annealer are entirely classical computers.

    4.11 Cryogenics and Vacuum: Keeping Qubits Cold and Quiet¶

    Superconducting and spin qubits must be colder than outer space — about 10 millikelvin — so heat does not randomly flip them. Dilution refrigerators reach this continuously using a mixture of helium-3 and helium-4. Ion and atom machines need ultra-high vacuum instead, and photon detectors need about 1-4 kelvin.

    Think of it like…

    A dilution refrigerator is like a set of Russian dolls made of thermos flasks, each colder than the one around it, with the qubit chip inside the smallest, coldest doll.

    Real-world example

    Fatima, lab manager at the fictional Aurora University, installs a dilution refrigerator with stages at about 50 K, 4 K, 1 K (the still), 100 mK (cold plate) and 10 mK (mixing chamber). Cool-down takes a day or two. She learns that every extra coaxial line adds heat, and that at 20 mK the fridge can remove only tens of microwatts.

    How it works¶

    1. A pulse-tube cryocooler cools the 50 K and 4 K stages without a liquid-helium bath ('dry' fridge).
    2. The helium-3/helium-4 mixture condenses and circulates inside the refrigerator.
    3. In the mixing chamber, helium-3 crosses from a concentrated to a dilute phase, absorbing heat — the cooling step.
    4. The still evaporates helium-3 so it can be pumped out, cleaned and returned.
    5. Qubits sit on the mixing-chamber plate inside magnetic and infrared shields; signals reach them through attenuators and filters at each stage.

    Who uses it

    Cryogenic engineers, lab technicians, facility managers and vendors such as Bluefors, Oxford Instruments and Maybell; every superconducting or spin-qubit lab depends on them.

    Key takeaway

    Memorise the stage temperatures (about 50 K, 4 K, 1 K, 100 mK, 10 mK) and that cooling power drops from about a watt at 4 K to roughly 10-30 µW near 20 mK.

    Going deeper

    • Dilution cooling works because helium-3 dissolving from its concentrated phase into helium-4 absorbs heat continuously; commercial systems reach below 10 mK and run for months.
    • Bluefors (Finland) reports more than 1,500 dilution refrigerators installed and offers the KIDE platform for larger qubit counts; other vendors include Oxford Instruments (Proteox), Maybell Quantum and Leiden Cryogenics.
    • Helium-3 is scarce, produced mainly from tritium decay; in September 2025 Bluefors agreed to buy up to 10,000 litres a year of lunar helium-3 from Interlune for delivery from 2028 to 2037, if extraction succeeds.
    • Wiring is a heat problem: each superconducting qubit typically needs two or three lines, and the passive heat load of coaxial cables limits fridges to a few thousand lines — driving flexible cables, multiplexing and cryo-CMOS.
    • Ion and atom machines operate at ultra-high vacuum around 10^-11 mbar so background gas does not knock out qubits; some ion traps are cryogenically cooled to improve vacuum and reduce heating.
    • Modular systems need links between fridges: IBM's Quantum System Two houses several processors, and ETH Zurich ran a loophole-free Bell test across a 30 m cryogenic microwave link (Nature, 2023).

    Common mistakes & misconceptions

    • Thinking the whole computer sits at 10 mK: only the chip and a few components do; electronics stay at 4 K or room temperature.
    • Assuming cooling power scales freely: millikelvin stages remove only microwatts, so every cable and amplifier must be budgeted.
    • Ignoring helium-3 supply, a real constraint on how many large dilution refrigerators can be built.

    4.12 Control Electronics, Lasers and Cryo-CMOS¶

    Every qubit needs precisely timed signals: microwave pulses for superconducting and spin qubits, laser beams for ions and atoms, and fast readout with feedback. The control stack of waveform generators, FPGAs, lasers, optics and amplifiers is often larger and costlier than the qubits themselves, and scaling it is a central engineering challenge.

    Think of it like…

    It is like an orchestra: each qubit is a musician who needs cues timed to the nanosecond, and with a million musicians you cannot run a separate cable to each one.

    Real-world example

    Diego, a control engineer at the fictional Vantage Quantum, programs an FPGA controller so a mid-circuit measurement result decides the next gate within about a microsecond. His 100-qubit superconducting system needs hundreds of coaxial lines and racks of waveform generators and digitisers — which is why his team is evaluating cryo-CMOS controllers.

    How it works¶

    1. The compiler outputs a pulse schedule: frequencies, amplitudes, phases and timings for every qubit.
    2. Arbitrary waveform generators or RF system-on-chip converters synthesise the pulses, directly or via IQ mixing.
    3. Signals travel down attenuated lines (or optical paths for lasers) to the qubits.
    4. Readout signals return through amplifiers to analogue-to-digital converters.
    5. FPGAs demodulate and threshold results in real time, applying feed-forward or sending syndromes to a decoder.
    6. Calibration loops retune frequencies, amplitudes and timings regularly.

    Who uses it

    FPGA and RF engineers, laser and optics engineers, cryo-CMOS chip designers and calibration scientists at qubit makers and at control vendors such as Quantum Machines, Qblox, Zurich Instruments and Keysight.

    Key takeaway

    Real-time decoding sets the pace: with a surface-code cycle of about 1 µs (Willow: 1.1 µs), the decoder must keep up or a backlog of syndromes grows without limit.

    Going deeper

    • Commercial control stacks include Quantum Machines OPX1000, Qblox Cluster, Zurich Instruments SHFQC+ and Keysight's quantum control systems; Fermilab's open-source QICK runs on AMD RFSoC boards.
    • Cryo-CMOS places control chips at about 4 K to cut cabling: Intel's Horse Ridge II (22 nm FinFET) and Pando Tree, Microsoft and University of Sydney's Gooseberry, and D-Wave's 2026 on-chip cryogenic control demonstration.
    • Power is the catch: a 4 K stage removes about a watt, so cryo-CMOS must use only milliwatts per many qubits, and millikelvin stages allow only microwatts.
    • Ions and atoms rely on narrow-linewidth lasers (from vendors such as Toptica), acousto-optic modulators and deflectors, and spatial light modulators; laser intensity and phase noise directly limit gate fidelity.
    • Integrated photonics that delivers laser light through waveguides inside the trap chip (MIT Lincoln Laboratory and others) and electronic microwave control (Oxford Ionics, now IonQ) aim to shrink ion-trap optics.
    • Calibration is automated with dependency graphs of experiments (for example Google's 'Optimus' approach) and machine-learning tune-up for spin qubits; large chips are recalibrated at least daily.

    Common mistakes & misconceptions

    • Underestimating the control system: for many labs the electronics cost more and occupy more racks than the cryostat.
    • Assuming cryo-CMOS solves scaling by itself: its heat dissipation must fit the tiny cooling budgets of cold stages.
    • Ignoring classical latency: dynamic circuits and error correction fail if decisions arrive after qubits have decohered.

    Try it: Build a DRAG-shaped microwave pulse envelope in NumPy¶

    import numpy as np
t = np.linspace(0, 40e-9, 401)                         # 40 ns single-qubit pulse
sigma, beta = 8e-9, 0.5e-9
I = np.exp(-((t - 20e-9)**2) / (2 * sigma**2))          # Gaussian in-phase envelope
Q = -beta * np.gradient(I, t)                           # DRAG quadrature reduces leakage
f_lo, f_if = 4.9e9, 100e6                               # local oscillator + IF = 5.0 GHz drive
print('peak I', round(float(I.max()), 3), 'peak |Q|', round(float(abs(Q).max()), 3), 'drive', (f_lo + f_if) / 1e9, 'GHz')

    4.13 Hardware Performance Metrics¶

    Hardware is judged by a handful of numbers: T1 and T2 (how long qubits remember), gate fidelities (how accurate operations are), SPAM (state preparation and measurement errors), connectivity and speed, holistic benchmarks such as quantum volume, EPLG, CLOPS and algorithmic qubits, and — for error-corrected machines — logical error rates. Each number tells only part of the story.

    Think of it like…

    It is like judging cars by top speed, fuel economy, braking distance and lap time: no single number tells you which car wins a real race.

    Real-world example

    Zoe, a student, compares two devices for a 1,000-gate circuit. With 99.5% two-qubit fidelity the chance of no error is about 0.995^1000 ≈ 0.7%; with 99.9% it is about 37%. That is why the industry celebrates 'three nines' — and why 'four nines' would make 10,000-gate circuits plausible.

    How it works¶

    1. Measure T1 (energy relaxation) and T2 (dephasing, via Ramsey T2* and Hahn-echo T2E).
    2. Run randomised benchmarking for average single- and two-qubit gate fidelities (and interleaved RB for specific gates).
    3. Measure readout (assignment) fidelity and state-preparation errors.
    4. Run holistic benchmarks: quantum volume, layer fidelity/EPLG, XEB, CLOPS or application benchmarks.
    5. For error correction, measure logical error per cycle versus code distance and the suppression factor Λ.
    6. Report medians and full distributions with all qubits operating simultaneously, not just the best pair.

    Who uses it

    Hardware characterisation scientists, benchmarking teams, quantum software engineers choosing devices, analysts and procurement teams at HPC centres.

    Key takeaway

    Fidelity compounds: success ≈ F^N. 99.9% supports roughly 1,000-gate circuits; always ask whether a quoted number is a median, a mean, a best pair, isolated or simultaneous.

    Going deeper

    • T1 is energy relaxation; T2 is loss of phase coherence, with T2 ≤ 2T1. Typical values: transmons 100-300 µs, ions seconds or more, atoms seconds (Caltech about 12.6 s), silicon-28 spin qubits milliseconds or longer.
    • Randomised benchmarking (Knill et al., 2008; Magesan et al., 2011) fits the decay of random Clifford sequences to get an average error per gate that is insensitive to SPAM errors; gate-set tomography gives a more detailed error model.
    • Quantum volume (IBM, 2019) is 2^n for the largest n-qubit, depth-n random circuit passing a heavy-output test above 2/3; Quantinuum H2 reached 2^25 in 2025. It saturates as a scaling measure because verification needs classical simulation.
    • Layer fidelity and EPLG (IBM, 2023) measure the error of layers of simultaneous two-qubit gates along a 100-qubit chain; EPLG = 1 − LF^(1/n₂q). A Heron r3 device reached about 2.2×10⁻³.
    • Speed metrics: CLOPS (IBM, 2021) counts circuit layer operations per second; Willow's RCS chip reported 63,000 circuit repetitions per second. IonQ's #AQ (algorithmic qubits) derives from application benchmarks, and IonQ is now moving to physical and logical counts.
    • Logical metrics for error-corrected machines include the logical error per cycle (Willow distance 7: about 0.14% per cycle) and the suppression factor Λ (2.14) gained per distance step of two.

    Common mistakes & misconceptions

    • Quoting a best-pair fidelity as typical: median and worst-case qubits decide whether your circuit works.
    • Comparing isolated gate errors with simultaneous ones; crosstalk usually makes simultaneous errors larger.
    • Treating vendor-defined metrics such as #AQ as industry standards, or comparing them directly with quantum volume.

    Try it: How gate fidelity compounds over a circuit¶

    for F in (0.99, 0.995, 0.999, 0.9999):
    print(F, {N: f'{F**N:.1%}' for N in (100, 1_000, 10_000)})
# 0.999 -> {100: 90.5%, 1000: 36.8%, 10000: 0.0%}

    4.14 Roadmaps 2026-2030 and How to Read Them Sceptically¶

    Most quantum companies publish roadmaps promising fault-tolerant machines around 2028-2030. They are useful for planning careers, research and investment, but dates slip, definitions change and metrics are chosen to look good. This section summarises the main roadmaps as of October 2026 and shows how to read them like a scientist.

    Think of it like…

    Roadmaps are like weather forecasts: tomorrow's is fairly reliable, next month's is a rough guess — and you should always check the forecaster's track record.

    Real-world example

    Hannah, an analyst at the fictional Ridgeway Partners, builds a table: IBM — Kookaburra 2026, Starling 2029 (200 logical qubits); Quantinuum — Helios 2025, Sol 2027, Apollo 2029; IonQ — 256 qubits 2026, 2 million physical qubits 2030; Microsoft — scalable topological machine by 2029. She adds columns for 'past milestones met on time' and 'peer-reviewed evidence' before writing her report.

    How it works¶

    1. Check the track record: which earlier milestones were delivered, and on time?
    2. Check the metric: physical or logical qubits; errors corrected or only detected; results post-selected?
    3. Check the assumptions behind the counts: error rates, cycle times, code distance.
    4. Look for peer-reviewed papers and independent access (cloud availability) rather than press releases.
    5. Translate marketing words — 'advantage', 'fault-tolerant', 'logical qubit' — into precise definitions.
    6. Compare with independent evaluations such as DARPA's Quantum Benchmarking Initiative and community advantage trackers.

    Who uses it

    Investors, analysts, policy makers, procurement teams at HPC centres, journalists and students planning careers.

    Key takeaway

    Fault-tolerance dates to remember: IBM Starling 2029, Quantinuum Apollo 2029, Google 'by the end of the decade', Microsoft 2029 — and DARPA QBI asks whether utility-scale machines are feasible by 2033.

    Going deeper

    • IBM's 2025 roadmap: Loon (2025) tests qLDPC parts; Kookaburra (2026) combines logic and memory; Cockatoo (2027) links modules; Starling (2029) targets 200 logical qubits and 100 million gates; Blue Jay (2033+) about 2,000 logical qubits.
    • Roadmap names get recycled: IBM's 2022 'Kookaburra' was a 4,158-qubit multi-chip processor for 2025, but the 2025 roadmap redefined it as a qLDPC module for 2026 — compare content, not just names.
    • Quantinuum delivered H2 (56 qubits) and Helios (98 qubits, November 2025); Sol's first 2D-grid traps are in validation for late 2027, and Apollo (2029) is planned as fully fault tolerant. It listed on Nasdaq (QNT) in June 2026.
    • IonQ targets 256 qubits in 2026, 10,000 on a chip in 2027, about 1,600 logical qubits in 2028 and 2 million physical (up to 80,000 logical) by 2030, helped by buying the SkyWater fab (closed July 2026).
    • Slips are normal: Rigetti's 108-qubit system moved from late 2025 to April 2026 and launched at 99.1% rather than its 99.5% fidelity target; Microsoft's topological timeline moved from 2033 to 2029 alongside disputed evidence.
    • DARPA QBI acts as an independent check: nearly 20 companies entered Stage A, 11 reached Stage B in November 2025 (including IBM, IonQ, Quantinuum, QuEra and Atom Computing), and Microsoft and PsiQuantum are in Stage C.

    Common mistakes & misconceptions

    • Treating roadmap dates as facts: they are targets, and even strong companies slip by quarters or years.
    • Comparing physical-qubit targets across modalities, or mixing physical and logical counts in one chart.
    • Ignoring that 'logical qubit' definitions vary in code distance, error rate and whether errors are corrected or only detected.
    • Extrapolating straight lines: scaling problems such as wiring, yield and decoding often appear suddenly at new sizes.
