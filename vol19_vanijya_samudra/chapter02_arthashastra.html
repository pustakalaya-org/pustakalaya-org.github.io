    Chapter 2: The Arthaśāstra and Early Indian Economic Thought¶

    Chapter Overview

    This chapter introduces the Arthaśāstra, the most detailed early Indian text on the management of wealth, and places it within the wider body of Indian thinking about economic life. It describes the rediscovery of the text, its structure and the debate about its date and authorship, and then examines its economic ideas: the meaning of artha and vārttā, the revenue system and its officials, state enterprise and monopolies, and the regulation of labour, including slavery. The chapter closes with the later nīti literature, with the Tamil Tirukkuṟaḷ and the Indo-Persian writing on kingship and revenue, and with the economic ethics of the dharmaśāstras and of Buddhist and Jain traditions. The Arthaśāstra’s theory of kingship, diplomacy and law is treated in Volume 18, Rājanīti and Dharmaśāstra, and is not repeated here.

    Kauṭilya’s Arthaśāstra is the indispensable starting point for any history of economic thought in South Asia. No other early Indian text describes in such detail how a state was to raise revenue, run workshops, mines and farms, supervise trade and regulate labour. It must, however, be read for what it is: a normative treatise addressed to a king, compiled over several centuries, which describes how an ideal administration should work rather than how any particular state did work. This chapter introduces the text and its economic ideas. The detailed regulation of markets, prices, weights and customs is reserved for Chapter 7, coinage and the mint for Chapter 5, interest and partnerships for Chapter 6, and guilds for Chapter 4.

    2.1 The Text: Rediscovery, Structure and Date¶

    2.1.1 Rediscovery¶

    Although the Arthaśāstra was known by name and cited by later writers, no complete manuscript was known to modern scholarship until the beginning of the twentieth century. In 1905 a palm-leaf manuscript in Grantha script reached the Government Oriental Library at Mysore, where its librarian, R. Shamasastry, recognised its importance. He published portions in translation from 1905 onwards, an edition of the Sanskrit text in 1909 and a complete English translation in 1915 (Shamasastry 1915). Further manuscripts, including some in Malayalam script, were later found, and new editions followed, among them that of J. Jolly and R. Schmidt (1923–24) and T. Gaṇapati Śāstrī’s edition with his own Sanskrit commentary (1924–25). The standard critical edition is R. P. Kangle’s, published with an English translation and a volume of studies between 1960 and 1965 (Kangle 1960). Patrick Olivelle’s annotated translation of 2013 is now the standard English version (Olivelle 2013); L. N. Rangarajan’s rearranged translation of 1992 is widely read but reorders the text by topic (Rangarajan 1992). References in this volume follow Kangle’s numbering of book, chapter and sentence.

    The discovery had an immediate effect. A treatise that discussed revenue, state workshops, customs schedules and wage contracts in a dry, technical prose overturned the then common European view that ancient Indian thought had been concerned only with religion and philosophy. That effect has sometimes produced the opposite exaggeration, in which the Arthaśāstra is presented as a complete economics far ahead of its time. Both views obscure what the text actually is.

    2.1.2 Structure¶

    The text describes itself as having fifteen books (adhikaraṇa), 150 chapters (adhyāya) and 180 topics (prakaraṇa), with an extent equivalent to 6,000 verses (AŚ 1.1). The “verse” here is a unit of length; the text is overwhelmingly prose, with verses mostly at the ends of chapters. Table 2.1 lists the books and indicates where economic material is concentrated.

    Table 2.1. The fifteen books of the Arthaśāstra and their economic content (titles after Kangle and Olivelle)
    Book Title Subject and economic relevance
    1 Vinayādhikārika Training of the king; the four sciences, including vārttā; ministers and spies
    2 Adhyakṣapracāra Activities of the superintendents: revenue, treasury, mines, trade, customs, weights, agriculture, textiles, shipping. The main economic book
    3 Dharmasthīya Civil law: contracts, debts, deposits, slaves and workers, partnerships, sale
    4 Kaṇṭakaśodhana “Eradication of thorns”: policing artisans and traders, remedies for calamities such as famine
    5 Yogavṛtta Secret conduct; emergency replenishment of the treasury; salaries of officials
    6–7 Maṇḍalayoni; Ṣāḍguṇya Foreign policy; Book 7 includes a comparison of trade routes
    8 Vyasanādhikārika Calamities affecting the constituents of the state, including the treasury
    9–14 Military and secret practices Campaigns, war, confederacies, sieges, occult means
    15 Tantrayukti Methods of composing a scientific treatise

    2.1.3 Date and Authorship¶

    The text names its author as Kauṭilya (the manuscripts also have Kauṭalya), and a verse at its close identifies him as the one who rescued the land from the Nanda kings. Later tradition, including the Kāmandakīya Nītisāra, the Purāṇas and the drama Mudrārākṣasa, identifies this Kauṭilya or Viṣṇugupta with Cāṇakya, the minister who helped Candragupta Maurya to power in the late fourth century BCE. On this traditional view the Arthaśāstra is a Mauryan work of c. 300 BCE, and Kangle, among others, defended an early date (Kangle 1960).

    Modern scholarship has largely abandoned that view. Thomas Trautmann’s statistical study of word usage showed that different books of the text have different authors and that the work is a compilation (Trautmann 1971). Olivelle, building on this, distinguishes three stages: earlier treatises on particular subjects; a “Kauṭilya recension” that combined and reworked them, which he dates c. 50 BCE–125 CE; and a later “śāstric redaction”, c. 175–300 CE, which divided the work into chapters with closing verses and added material (Olivelle 2013). Mark McClish has argued that this redaction also reshaped the text to bring it into line with Brahmanical ideas of dharma (McClish 2019). The text that survives is therefore composite and, in its present form, belongs to c. 50 BCE–300 CE. It may preserve older material, and it cites by name earlier teachers whose views it often rejects (Bhāradvāja, Viśālākṣa, Parāśara, Piśuna and others), which shows that it drew on an established tradition of arthaśāstra writing. But it is not a document of the Mauryan administration, and its descriptions cannot be used, as they once were, as evidence for how the Mauryan empire was governed.

    Debate: Traditional and Academic Dating

    Traditional view. The Arthaśāstra was written by Cāṇakya (Kauṭilya, Viṣṇugupta), minister of Candragupta Maurya, c. 300 BCE, and describes the Mauryan state.

    Academic view. The text is a composite whose present form dates from c. 50 BCE–300 CE (Olivelle 2013); it is the work of several hands (Trautmann 1971); and the identification of Kauṭilya with Cāṇakya is first attested in later sources. Points of difference between the text and Megasthenes’ account of Mauryan Pāṭaliputra, preserved in later Greek writers (McCrindle 1877), are among the arguments against using it as a Mauryan record.

    Consequence for this volume. The Arthaśāstra is treated as evidence for ideas about economic administration current in the early centuries CE, not as a description of a working Mauryan economy. This dating is assumed wherever the text is cited in later chapters, and it is not restated there.

    2.2 Artha, the Aims of Life and Vārttā¶

    2.2.1 The Meaning of Artha¶

    The word artha covers wealth, material well-being, advantage and power. The Arthaśāstra defines its subject in its last book: artha is the livelihood (vṛtti) of human beings, and also the earth inhabited by human beings; the science that is the means of acquiring and protecting that earth is the arthaśāstra (AŚ 15.1.1–2). Economic life and political control are thus one subject. The king’s wealth depends on a populated, productive territory, and the productive territory depends on the king’s protection.

    Artha is one of the aims of human life (puruṣārtha). The older scheme was a triad (trivarga) of dharma, artha and kāma; the fourth aim, mokṣa (liberation), was added to make the familiar set of four. The Arthaśāstra discusses how the three should be balanced. A king should pursue pleasure without conflicting with dharma and artha, or the three equally, since they are bound to one another; but the text gives its own view in a short prose statement:

    artha eva pradhāna iti kauṭilyaḥ. arthamūlau hi dharmakāmāv iti.
    “Wealth alone is the chief thing, says Kauṭilya, for dharma and pleasure have wealth as their root.” (AŚ 1.7.6–7)

    The claim is not that wealth is the highest value but that the other aims cannot be pursued without it. The dharmaśāstras generally rank dharma first, and the ranking of the aims was a recurrent subject of debate in Sanskrit literature. A related idea is yogakṣema, “acquisition and security”: the king’s task is to secure what has not yet been obtained and to protect what has. In the training of the king the text also states that the king’s own good lies in the good of his subjects (AŚ 1.19.34). How these principles bear on kingship is the subject of Volume 18; for this chapter, their importance is that the text makes the productive economy the foundation of the state.

    2.2.2 The Four Sciences and Vārttā¶

    The Arthaśāstra names four sciences (vidyā) a king must study: philosophy or critical inquiry (ānvīkṣikī), the three Vedas (trayī), economics (vārttā) and government or the science of punishment (daṇḍanīti) (AŚ 1.2–1.4). Vārttā consists of agriculture, animal husbandry and trade. It is useful, the text says, because it yields grain, livestock, money, forest produce and labour, and because through it the king brings under control both his own people and his enemies, by means of the treasury and the army (AŚ 1.4.1–2).

    Three features of this classification deserve notice. First, vārttā is a body of practical knowledge, not a theory of markets; it covers the arts by which livelihood is produced. Second, it is listed among the sciences of the king, so economic knowledge is framed from the point of view of the ruler who taxes and protects. Third, labour (viṣṭi, unpaid service owed to the state) is listed among the products of vārttā, a reminder that the economy the text imagines rests in part on compulsion (Section 2.6).

    Trade figures in the Arthaśāstra chiefly as a source of revenue and of goods the state needs. One passage, however, shows the kind of practical reasoning the text applies to it: in weighing which routes a king should develop, it sets the views of earlier teachers against its own, preferring land routes to water routes for their reliability, the coastal route to the open sea, and the southern route to the Himalayan one for the volume of its high-value goods (AŚ 7.12). The passage is discussed in Section 10.2.3.

    2.3 Revenue and Fiscal Administration¶

    2.3.1 The Treasury and its Officers¶

    “All undertakings depend on the treasury” (AŚ 2.8.1): the Arthaśāstra’s fiscal chapters are organised around this principle. Two senior officers stand at the head of the fiscal system, and their titles are easily confused. The samāhartṛ, usually translated “Collector” (Kangle: “administrator”), is responsible for assessing and collecting revenue from all sources and for supervising the countryside through district officers (sthānika) and village accountants (gopa) who keep records of land, households, occupations, livestock, income and expenditure (AŚ 2.6, 2.35). The sannidhātṛ, usually translated “Treasurer” (Kangle: “director of stores”), is responsible for building and stocking the state’s storehouses: the treasury, the warehouse for commodities, the granary, the store for forest produce, the armoury and the prison (AŚ 2.5). Both are civil officials, not military officers or ministers in a modern sense.

    Accounts were kept in a records office (akṣapaṭala) where officials submitted their accounts at fixed times of the year (AŚ 2.7). The text is frank about the risk of embezzlement. It lists forty ways in which officials can misappropriate revenue (AŚ 2.8) and observes that it is as hard for an official handling the king’s money not to taste a little of it as it is not to taste honey or poison placed on the tongue, and that one can no more tell when an official is taking money than when a fish is drinking water (AŚ 2.9). The remedy is constant inspection, rotation and the use of informers.

    2.3.2 Sources of Revenue¶

    The text classifies revenue in two ways. The first is by source, the seven “bodies of revenue” (āyaśarīra) that the Collector must attend to (AŚ 2.6), summarised in Table 2.2. The second is by form: price received for state goods (mūlya), share (bhāga), surcharge (vyājī), fees and fixed payments, a charge for testing coins (rūpika), and fines (atyaya). Revenue is also divided into current, arrears and incidental receipts. Some of these technical terms are obscure, and translators differ on their exact meanings.

    Table 2.2. The seven “bodies of revenue” (āyaśarīra) in AŚ 2.6 (simplified, after Kangle and Olivelle)
    Source Main items
    Fort (durga), i.e. the capital Customs (śulka), fines, fees for weights and measures, the city superintendent, the mint, passes, liquor, slaughterhouses, yarn, oil, ghee, goldsmiths, the market (paṇyasaṃsthā), courtesans, gambling, building sites, guilds of artisans, temples, gate and outsiders’ dues
    Countryside (rāṣṭra) Produce of crown land (sītā), the share of private produce (bhāga), bali, kara, dues from merchants, river, ferry and boat dues, ports, pasture, road cess (vartanī), rope and “thief-rope” dues
    Mines (khani) Gold, silver, diamonds, gems, pearls, coral, conch shell, metals, salt
    Irrigation works (setu) Gardens, orchards, vegetable plots, irrigated fields
    Forests (vana) Game, produce and elephant forests
    Herds (vraja) Cattle, buffaloes, goats, sheep, donkeys, camels, horses, mules
    Trade routes (vaṇikpatha) Land and water routes

    The distinction between sītā and bhāga is basic. Sītā is the produce of land cultivated directly by the state under the superintendent of agriculture (sītādhyakṣa); bhāga is the share of the crop taken from other cultivators. The later history of this claim on the harvest is surveyed in Section 3.1. The customary share was one-sixth: the Arthaśāstra relates, as a story to be spread by the king’s agents, that when people were oppressed by anarchy they made Manu their king and assigned him one-sixth of their grain and one-tenth of their merchandise, as well as money, as his share (AŚ 1.13). The dharmaśāstras give similar but varying rates: Manu allows the king one-sixth, one-eighth or one-twelfth of grain, and a fiftieth of increments in cattle and gold (Manu 7.130) (Olivelle 2004). The meanings of bali and kara are debated; both appear to be additional levies. Users of irrigation works built by the state paid a separate water rate, graded according to whether water was lifted by hand, carried, raised by mechanical devices or drawn from rivers and tanks (AŚ 2.24). Customs (śulka) and tolls are treated in Chapter 7.

    The text expects the king to be restrained in ordinary times. Settlers on new land are to be given grain, cattle and money as advances, and remissions of tax, so that cultivation expands (AŚ 2.1). A verse in the chapter on emergency revenue compares the king to a gardener who should pick only ripe fruit, avoiding unripe fruit whose plucking causes resentment and damages the tree (AŚ 5.2). But the same chapter shows the other side. A king in financial difficulty may demand a larger share of grain from fertile regions, request “contributions” (praṇaya) from merchants and others, and, through secret agents, use fraudulent methods: staging miracles at shrines to attract donations, seizing the property of temples, or ruining wealthy subjects who are said to be disloyal (AŚ 5.2). The text limits such demands to once only, but it does not hide them.

    2.3.3 Expenditure and Salaries¶

    On the expenditure side, the Arthaśāstra prescribes a scale of annual salaries in paṇas (AŚ 5.3). It suggests that the wage bill should be kept within a quarter of revenue. The highest rate, 48,000 paṇas, is given to the king’s priest, preceptor, counsellor, the commander-in-chief, the crown prince, the queen mother and the chief queen; the lowest named rates, 60 paṇas, go to attendants and menial servants. The ratio between highest and lowest, about 800 to 1, reflects the steep hierarchy the text assumes. Whether any state ever paid such salaries is unknown; the figures are best read as a model of how a ruler might rank his servants.

    2.4 The Superintendents¶

    Book 2 describes some thirty superintendents (adhyakṣa) with charge of particular branches of the state economy. Their number and specialisation are the most striking feature of the book. They are not all of equal rank, and the text gives no organisational chart; the samāhartṛ and sannidhātṛ coordinate revenue and stores. Table 2.3 lists the main economic superintendents. The details of several belong to other chapters of this volume.

    The superintendents of horses, elephants, chariots and infantry (AŚ 2.30–33), of courtesans (2.27) and of slaughterhouses (2.26) complete the book. The market superintendent (saṃsthādhyakṣa) appears in Book 4 (Chapter 7). Megasthenes’ account of the boards that supervised crafts, trade and sales at Pāṭaliputra, preserved by Strabo, shows that Greek observers too saw an administration that took a close interest in commerce, though his offices do not match the Arthaśāstra’s (McCrindle 1877).

    2.5 State Enterprise: Crown Lands, Mines and Monopolies¶

    The Arthaśāstra imagines a state that is itself a major producer. It is not a description of a command economy: private cultivators, artisans and merchants are everywhere assumed, taxed and protected. But the state farms its own land, runs workshops and mines, and reserves certain trades to itself.

    Crown agriculture. The superintendent of agriculture cultivates crown land using slaves, hired labourers and convicts working off their fines, supplied with ploughs, oxen and the services of smiths, carpenters and other artisans (AŚ 2.24). He is to be versed in agriculture, irrigation and the care of plants; the text records rainfall norms for different regions and refers to a rain gauge kept at the granary (AŚ 2.5, 2.24). Land that cannot be sown by the state may be given to sharecroppers. The settlement of new lands, mainly with Śūdra cultivators, in villages of a hundred to five hundred families is described in AŚ 2.1, which also forbids entertainers and pleasure halls in villages lest they distract people from work.

    Mines and metals. The superintendent of mines (ākarādhyakṣa) is to be expert in the science of ores and metals and in testing gems, and to work with specialists. He identifies old and new workings from traces such as slag, charcoal and ash, and assesses ores by colour, weight and smell (AŚ 2.12). The text makes a practical distinction: mines that need heavy investment may be leased out for a share of the output or for a fixed rent, while those that can be worked cheaply should be worked by the state itself. Separate superintendents handle metal workshops, the mint, sea products and salt. A verse at the end of the chapter states that the treasury has its source in the mines, and that with treasury and army the earth is won. The archaeology of early Indian mining and metallurgy is treated in Volume 14, Rasāyana (Craddock 1995; Biswas and Biswas 1996).

    Monopolies and single channels. The text repeatedly recommends that the sale of goods produced by the state, and of certain strategic goods, be concentrated in a single channel (ekamukha), and it fines unauthorised production and sale of mine products and salt (AŚ 2.12). Liquor was to be produced and sold under the superintendent of liquor, with restrictions on private manufacture and close control of drinking houses (AŚ 2.25). The state’s own goods were to be sold through its agents or through merchants at fixed prices (AŚ 2.16; Chapter 7). These are fiscal monopolies designed to secure revenue and control, not a theory of state ownership.

    Textiles. The superintendent of yarn employs women without other support to spin, paying them by the quantity and quality of their yarn (AŚ 2.23). The passage is a rare, detailed glimpse of women’s paid work, and of the way dependent and marginal women were drawn into state production; it is discussed in Section 8.4.

    Thomas Trautmann has argued that the Arthaśāstra’s economics should be understood as a coherent model of its own, combining a large state sector with private enterprise under close supervision, rather than as an anticipation of later European doctrines (Trautmann 2012). Whether any kingdom approached this model is a separate question that the text cannot answer.

    2.6 Labour, Wages and Slavery¶

    The Arthaśāstra regulates several kinds of labour: hired workers (karmakara), slaves (dāsa), persons pledged for debt (āhitaka), convicts, and those who owed unpaid service to the state (viṣṭi). Its rules are among the most detailed in early Indian law, and they need to be described without either romanticising or ignoring their harshness (Chakravarti 1987).

    2.6.1 Hired Workers¶

    The rules on hired labour (AŚ 3.13–14) assume a contract made before witnesses. Wages are to be paid as agreed; if no wage was agreed, the text supplies defaults: a cultivator receives a tenth of the crop, a herdsman a tenth of the butter, and a trader a tenth of the goods he sells on another’s behalf. A worker who takes wages and fails to work, and an employer who fails to pay, are both fined. Joint work by guild workers and partners is treated under the heading of undertakings in partnership (saṃbhūya-samutthāna, AŚ 3.14), which deals with the division of earnings; the term means partnership, and it is discussed in Chapter 6.

    These are rules of contract, not a code of labour rights. The text does not provide for minimum wages, and its default rates protected the employer as much as the worker.

    2.6.2 Slaves and Pledged Persons¶

    The chapter on slaves (AŚ 3.13) recognises several ways of becoming a slave: birth in the household, inheritance, purchase, capture in war, self-sale and pledging for debt. Its central rule is that an Ārya may not be made a slave, although he may be pledged in distress. “Ārya” here means a free person, and the category explicitly includes Śūdras who were not born slaves. Relatives who sell or pledge a free minor are fined, the fine increasing with the varṇa of the person concerned, from Śūdra to Brahmin. The text then states that there is no offence for barbarians (mleccha) in selling or pledging their own children.

    Within this framework the text sets limits on the treatment of pledged persons and slaves. A pledged person may not be made to carry corpses or remove filth, and a pledged woman may not be beaten, stripped or sexually violated; if her master does so, he loses what he paid and she goes free. A slave may earn and keep property without detriment to the master’s work, may inherit from his father, and may buy his freedom by paying his price. The children of a master by his female slave, and the slave mother, are to be freed.

    These provisions have sometimes been described as unusually humane. It is fairer to say that the text regulates an institution it fully accepts. Its protections are graded by status and birth: they are strongest for free persons who have fallen into bondage through debt, while those born or bought into slavery, war captives and the children of mlecchas are left largely to their owners, and the fines themselves vary with varṇa. The state itself employed slaves and convicts on crown land, and its claim to the unpaid labour of its subjects (viṣṭi) is treated as normal. The dharmaśāstras likewise regulate slavery and assume varṇa-graded rights (Olivelle 2004; Kane 1930). For the history of slavery in early India, see Chanana (1960) and Chakravarti (1985).

    2.7 Calamities and the Economy¶

    Book 8 of the Arthaśāstra discusses calamities (vyasana) that may befall the constituents of the state: the king, ministers, countryside, fort, treasury, army and ally. It proceeds by debate, setting out the views of earlier teachers on which calamity is the more serious and then giving Kauṭilya’s own. Its economic interest lies in two places. In comparing a calamity of the treasury with one of the army, the text argues that the army depends on the treasury: without money, an army deserts to the enemy or turns on its own king (AŚ 8.1). And among the “divine” calamities it lists fire, flood, disease, famine and epidemic, weighing which does most damage to the productive population (AŚ 8.4). The practical measures the text prescribes against famine, including the release of stored grain and the employment of the hungry on public works paid in food, are described in Chapter 7. Book 8 is an analysis of the relative gravity of disasters for a king, not a theory of economic policy.

    2.8 Later Traditions of Nīti¶

    The Arthaśāstra was the culmination of one tradition and the source of another. Later works on nīti (polity, prudent conduct) drew on it, usually reducing its economic detail. Table 2.4 summarises the main texts.

    Table 2.4. Later works in the nīti and arthaśāstra tradition
    Text Date Character
    Kāmandakīya Nītisāra Probably mid-first millennium CE (estimates vary) Verse digest of polity that honours Viṣṇugupta as its source; much less economic detail than the Arthaśāstra
    Nītivākyāmṛta of Somadeva Sūri 10th c. CE Aphoristic prose on polity by a Jain author in the Deccan; absorbs much arthaśāstra material
    Mānasollāsa of Someśvara III c. 1131 CE Encyclopaedic Cālukya work on royal life, administration, revenue and the arts
    Śukranīti 19th c. CE (Gopal 1962) Compilation long presented as ancient; not evidence for early India

    The Śukranīti requires a particular warning, because it is still often cited as an ancient source on taxation, prices and administration. It was edited by Gustav Oppert in 1882 and translated by B. K. Sarkar in 1914. In 1962 Lallanji Gopal showed, on the basis of its content and vocabulary, including its descriptions of firearms, that the text in its present form was composed in the nineteenth century (Gopal 1962). It is a document of the nineteenth century, not of ancient or medieval economic thought.

    2.9 Beyond Sanskrit: The Tirukkuṟaḷ and Indo-Persian Thought¶

    The Sanskrit nīti literature was not the only body of Indian writing that reflected on wealth and the state before the colonial period. Two others deserve a place in a history of economic thought, although neither was written as economics: the Tamil Tirukkuṟaḷ, and the Persian literature of advice and ethics of the Delhi Sultanate and the Mughal court.

    2.9.1 The Tirukkuṟaḷ’s Book of Wealth¶

    The Tirukkuṟaḷ attributed to Tiruvaḷḷuvar is a collection of 1,330 couplets in 133 chapters of ten. Its date is uncertain; it is usually placed around the middle of the first millennium CE, though estimates range more widely (Zvelebil 1973). Its three books treat virtue (aṟam), wealth and polity (poruḷ) and love (iṉpam), a scheme parallel to the Sanskrit triad of dharma, artha and kāma. The book of wealth (Poruṭpāl) is the longest, with seventy chapters on the king, his ministers, the army, allies and fortresses, and the conduct of the citizen and the cultivator.

    Its economic ideas are stated as maxims, not as administrative rules. The ideal king is one who can create wealth, acquire it, guard it and distribute it well (Kuṟaḷ 385). A chapter on the making of wealth names the king’s lawful revenues as unclaimed property, customs and tolls (ulku), and what is taken from defeated enemies (756). A king who presses his subjects for money is compared to a robber who stands on the road with a spear and demands that travellers give (552). And the chapter on agriculture opens with the maxim that the world, however it turns, follows the plough, so that farming, for all its toil, is the first of occupations (1031) (Tiruvaḷḷuvar 1886). The Tirukkuṟaḷ thus shares the Arthaśāstra’s view that wealth is the foundation of the state, but it subordinates the pursuit of wealth more explicitly to virtue, it places the cultivator at the centre, and it does not frame its advice in terms of varṇa.

    2.9.2 Indo-Persian Writing on Kingship and Revenue¶

    The courts of the Delhi Sultanate and the Mughals produced a large Persian literature of political advice and ethics. It is used in this volume mainly as a source for the policies it describes, but it also contains reasoned views about revenue, prices and the household economy.

    The most original of the Sultanate writers on these questions was Ẓiyāʾ al-Dīn Baranī, whose Fatāwā-yi Jahāndārī (“Rulings on world-government”, written in the 1350s) argues that a Muslim ruler must supplement the religious law with state regulations (ẓawābiṭ) made by reason and necessity. Baranī presented ʿAlāʾ al-Dīn Khaljī’s price controls as an example of such regulation (Section 18.2.2), and he held strongly hierarchical views: wealth, learning and office, in his account, should be kept from the low-born (Habib and Khan 1961). The Persian ethical tradition (akhlāq), above all Naṣīr al-Dīn Ṭūsī’s Akhlāq-i Nāṣirī (1235), written in Iran and widely read in India, devoted a part of ethics to the management of the household (tadbīr-i manzil): how wealth should be acquired, preserved and spent. Ṭūsī divided society into four classes, the men of the pen, of the sword, of business and of the land, whose balance the ruler must keep. Muzaffar Alam has shown how far these akhlāq norms shaped Mughal political ideas, including those of Akbar’s minister Abū al-Faḍl (Alam 2004).

    Abū al-Faḍl’s Āʾīn-i Akbarī (completed in the 1590s) is chiefly an administrative description, the source of most of what is known about the Mughal revenue system (Section 18.5.1). But its order of presentation, from the imperial household and its workshops to the army, the revenue and the provinces, sets out a theory of an empire in which revenue is the due of a ruler who protects his subjects and keeps the classes of society in balance. These Sanskrit, Tamil and Persian traditions differed widely, but they shared a view of wealth from the ruler’s side, as something to be produced, protected and justly taken. None of them treated the market as a self-regulating system; that idea entered Indian debate with classical political economy in the nineteenth century (Chapter 20).

    2.10 Dharmaśāstra on Wealth and Trade¶

    The dharmaśāstras approach economic life from the standpoint of duty according to varṇa and stage of life. Trade, money-lending, agriculture and cattle-keeping are assigned to the Vaiśya (Manu 1.90), and Manu describes the knowledge a Vaiśya should have: the value of gems, pearls, coral, metals, cloth, perfumes and spices; the methods of sowing; weights and measures; the good and bad qualities of goods; the conditions of different regions; the probable profit and loss on merchandise; the wages of servants; and the languages of different peoples (Manu 9.326–333) (Olivelle 2004). In times of distress a Brahmin or Kṣatriya may live by trade, but certain goods, among them salt, sesame, liquor, meat, weapons and human beings, may not be sold by a Brahmin (Manu 10.85–94).

    The law books also regulate the instruments of commerce: the rate of interest, which Manu and Yājñavalkya grade by the varṇa of the debtor; deposits and pledges; partnership; sale and its rescission; and the conventions of guilds (Chapters 4 and 6). Several of the eighteen titles of law listed by Manu concern commercial dealings, including non-delivery of goods sold, rescission of purchase and breach of agreement (Manu 8.4–7). The king is told to fix prices and inspect weights and measures at regular intervals (Manu 8.398–403; Chapter 7). Taken together, the law books show that commerce was regarded as a legitimate and necessary activity, but one placed within a hierarchy of birth that determined who might pursue it, on what terms and with what penalties (Kane 1930; Olivelle 1999).

    2.11 Buddhist and Jain Economic Ethics¶

    2.11.1 Buddhism¶

    Early Buddhism grew up in the towns of the Ganga valley, and its texts are addressed in large part to the wealthy householder (gahapati) and the merchant or financier (seṭṭhi). Uma Chakravarti has shown how these social categories appear in the Pali canon: the gahapati as the head of a propertied household, often a landowner, and the seṭṭhi as a man of great wealth, prominent in urban life and among the Buddha’s lay supporters (Chakravarti 1987). Anāthapiṇḍika, the seṭṭhi of Sāvatthī who bought the Jetavana grove for the order, is the best-known example.

    The Pali texts offer an ethic of wealth for such people. The Sigālovāda Sutta (Dīgha Nikāya 31), the fullest statement of this ethic, advises the householder to accumulate wealth gradually, as a bee gathers nectar, and to divide it into four parts: one to be used for his needs, two to be invested in his work, and the fourth to be kept for times of misfortune. Right livelihood (sammā-ājīva) is part of the noble eightfold path, and the Aṅguttara Nikāya names five trades a lay follower should not engage in: trade in weapons, in living beings, in meat, in intoxicants and in poison (AN 5.177). The moral precepts listed in the opening suttas of the Dīgha Nikāya include abstention from cheating with scales, metal and measures. In the Kūṭadanta Sutta (Dīgha Nikāya 5), a king is advised to end banditry not by punishment but by giving seed and fodder to cultivators, capital to traders and food and wages to those in his service, so that people prosper and the country is at peace. These texts express an ethic of industry, thrift and generosity, directed at donors on whom the monastic order depended.

    2.11.2 Jainism¶

    Jain merchants have been prominent in western and southern India for many centuries. The lay Jain takes lesser vows (aṇuvrata), among them non-violence, truthfulness, non-stealing and the limitation of possessions (parigraha-parimāṇa), and Jain texts list occupations that a layman should avoid because they involve harm to living beings, such as trades in charcoal, lac, ivory and poisons and the clearing of forests (Jaini 1979). These restrictions, together with the high value placed on honesty and on generous giving, helped orient Jain communities towards trade and finance rather than agriculture. James Laidlaw’s ethnography of Jain merchants in Jaipur shows how a religion of renunciation and a life of commercial success are held together in practice, through gifting, fasting and a reputation for probity on which commercial credit itself depends (Laidlaw 1995).

    2.12 Reading the Arthaśāstra Today¶

    Since its rediscovery, the Arthaśāstra has been compared with Machiavelli, with European mercantilism, with Adam Smith and even with Keynes and modern development economics. Such comparisons can be useful as analogies. The text does recognise the importance of revenue for state power, it calculates the costs and gains of trade ventures, it regulates prices and weights, and it uses public works to relieve famine. But the analogies mislead when they become claims of priority or anticipation. The Arthaśāstra has no concept of a national economy, no theory of market equilibrium and no notion of rights against the state; its purpose is to strengthen a king, and its economic prescriptions serve that end. Roger Boesche reads it as a work of political realism (Boesche 2002); Trautmann stresses its distinct economic model (Trautmann 2012). Both approaches take the text on its own terms, which is the condition for using it well.
