    Chapter 9: Gift, Temple and Pilgrimage: Religious Institutions in the Economy¶

    Chapter Overview

    Religious institutions were among the largest holders of wealth in South Asia. Monasteries, temples, mosques, Sufi shrines and gurdwārās received land, money and livestock; they employed priests, cooks, musicians, accountants and craftsmen; they lent, invested and bought; and the pilgrimages and festivals they sustained moved people and goods across the subcontinent. This chapter looks at that economy. It begins with the theory of the gift (dāna) in the Brahmanical, Buddhist and Jain traditions, then turns to monasteries, to the south Indian temple as an economic institution and the hazards of temple wealth, to pilgrimage and fairs, to public works financed as gifts, to Islamic, Sikh, Christian and Parsi institutions, and to the laws by which the colonial and independent states came to regulate religious endowments. Earlier chapters have noted the endowments deposited with guilds (Chapter 4) and the lending of temple funds (Chapter 6); here the institutions themselves are the subject.

    9.1 Gift and Merit: The Theory of Dāna¶

    Every Indian religious tradition made giving a central duty of the householder, and each gave it a theory. In the Brahmanical texts the gift (dāna) is one of the duties of every twice-born man and the special duty of the king and the merchant; receiving gifts is one of the lawful livelihoods of the brahmin. The dharmaśāstras and the Purāṇas describe what makes a gift fruitful: the giver’s faith, a fitting time and place, an object that is the giver’s own and lawfully acquired, and above all a worthy recipient (pātra), usually a learned and virtuous brahmin. A gift to an unworthy person, the texts warn, is wasted or harmful to both parties. From the twelfth century this teaching was collected in large digests. The “book on gifts” (Dānakāṇḍa) of Lakṣmīdhara’s Kṛtyakalpataru, compiled for the Gāhaḍavāla court at Banaras, the Dānasāgara attributed to the Sena king Ballālasena of Bengal, and the section on gifts in Hemādri’s Caturvargacintāmaṇi, written for the Yādavas of Devagiri in the thirteenth century, classify gifts by object, occasion and recipient and set out the ritual for each (Kane 1930).

    The grandest gifts were the sixteen “great gifts” (mahādāna) listed in the Matsya Purāṇa and the digests. The first of them, the tulāpuruṣa, required the donor to be weighed against gold or other precious substances, which were then distributed to brahmins. Inscriptions of several dynasties record kings performing such gifts. For the economic historian their significance lies less in the ritual than in what they did: they converted the surplus a king had collected as revenue or taken in war into gifts to a priestly class, and they advertised the king’s wealth and piety at the same time.

    Most giving was more modest and more lasting. The land grants recorded on copper plates from the Gupta period onward (Section 3.2) were gifts in this sense: a brahmadeya gave a village or its revenue to brahmins, a devadāna to a temple deity, and a grant to a monastery to the Buddhist or Jain community. Such grants were often made in perpetuity and protected by imprecatory verses threatening the person who resumed them with long ages in hell. Whatever their religious purpose, they transferred rights over land and revenue on a very large scale and created religious landholders who had an interest in cultivation, irrigation and the settlement of new land.

    The Buddhist and Jain traditions taught the merit of giving with equal emphasis but directed it differently. For Buddhists the monastic community (saṅgha) was the “incomparable field of merit for the world”, and gifts of food, robes, lodging and medicine to monks were the foundation of lay religious life; the merit could be transferred to others, including dead relatives. For Jains, gifts to mendicants were hedged with rules that made the mendicant dependent on lay households that understood them, while gifts for temples, images, pilgrimages and the copying of texts became the main outlet for the wealth of Jain merchant families. Maria Heim has compared the three traditions’ theories of the gift and shown how each balanced the giver’s merit against the purity and freedom of the recipient (Heim 2004). James Laidlaw’s study of Jain merchants in Jaipur shows how, in the twentieth century, public giving remained bound up with credit and reputation: a family’s standing in the business community depended in part on visible religious generosity (Laidlaw 1995).

    Scholarly Note: Gift and economy

    Modern anthropologists, following Marcel Mauss, have used Indian material to argue that gifts create obligations and relationships rather than simply transferring goods. The Brahmanical texts complicate this: they often present the ideal gift as one that expects no return, and some recipients regarded gifts as dangerous because they carried away the giver’s sin. Historians therefore treat dāna neither as charity in the modern sense nor as disguised exchange, but as an institution with its own rules, which redistributed wealth towards religious specialists and institutions while confirming the status of donors (Heim 2004).

    9.2 Monasteries as Economic Institutions¶

    9.2.1 Buddhist Monasteries in India and Sri Lanka¶

    The Buddhist monastery began as a rains retreat for wandering monks and became, within a few centuries, a permanent establishment with buildings, lands, stores and servants. Gregory Schopen’s reading of the monastic codes alongside inscriptions has shown how far the large monasteries of the early centuries CE were engaged with property and money: the Mūlasarvāstivāda Vinaya regulates the lending of monastic funds at interest under written contracts and the management of permanent endowments (Schopen 2004). This evidence, and the debate over how far it applies to Indian Buddhism as a whole, is discussed in Volume 4, Buddha–Jina Prabhā. Here three economic points matter. First, monasteries were consumers on a large scale, and the location of the rock-cut monasteries of the western Deccan near the passes and the coastal ports points to their close relation with trade (Chapter 10) (Ray 1986). Second, they were investors, placing endowments with guilds whose interest paid for robes and food (Chapter 4). Third, from the Gupta period they became landholders. The great monastery of Nālandā was supported, according to the Chinese pilgrims, by the revenue of a large number of villages granted by kings: Xuanzang’s biographer speaks of about a hundred villages and Yijing of more than two hundred. Such figures are impressions, but copper-plate grants to Nālandā and to the monasteries of Valabhī confirm that monastic income came largely from land.

    Sri Lanka offers the fullest picture of a monastic economy, because its chronicles and inscriptions are unusually rich. R. A. L. H. Gunawardana’s study of the early medieval period shows the great monasteries of Anurādhapura and Polonnaruva as owners of estates and of irrigation works, receiving a share of the crop and dues for the use of water, employing tenants and labourers, and administering their property through lay officials (Gunawardana 1979). The monks themselves were forbidden to handle money or till the soil, so the management of monastic wealth required intermediaries. Gunawardana showed that the tension between the ideal of the propertyless monk and the reality of the landlord monastery was recognised within the tradition itself, and that it shaped the relations between the saṅgha and the kings, who both endowed the monasteries and tried to regulate them.

    9.2.2 Jain Temples and the Property of the Gods¶

    The Jain community has been, for most of its history, closely tied to trade, and the economics of Jain giving show it clearly. Jain doctrine treats property given to an image or a temple (devadravya, “the god’s wealth”) as inviolable: it may be spent only on the temple and its worship, and to use it for any other purpose is held to be a grave sin. Much of this fund is raised by the auction (bolī) of ritual privileges, such as the right to perform the first worship at a festival or to carry an image in procession, for which families bid publicly (Laidlaw 1995; Cort 2001). The bidding makes a family’s wealth visible and converts it into religious honour; it also places large sums under the control of the temple trustees.

    Jain temple trusts became some of the largest religious institutions of western India. The Ānandjī Kalyāṇjī Peḍhī of Ahmedabad, which grew out of arrangements made by the Śvetāmbara merchant community of that city in the eighteenth century, manages the temple-city of Śatruñjaya at Pālitāṇā and many other shrines; its long dispute with the rulers of Pālitāṇā over the dues payable for the protection of pilgrims was settled only under British arbitration. Seventeenth-century Ahmedabad merchants such as Śāntidās Jhaverī, who held the office of town headman (Chapter 4), were also builders of temples, and the pattern of merchant wealth flowing into temples, rest-houses (dharmaśālās) and animal refuges (pāñjrāpoḷs) continued into the twentieth century (Cort 2001). The giving was not only religious: the trusts and endowments of the merchant communities also provided credit, employment and relief within the community, and confirmed the standing of the families that controlled them.

    9.3 The South Indian Temple as an Economic Institution¶

    9.3.1 The Chola Temple¶

    From the tenth century the great temples of the Tamil country became economic institutions on a scale without earlier parallel in the subcontinent. Their walls carry thousands of inscriptions recording gifts, and these are among the richest sources for the economy of medieval south India. The temple built by Rājarāja I at Thanjavur, completed around 1010, is the outstanding example. Its inscriptions list the villages assigned to it across the Chola territories and in Sri Lanka, the gold, silver and jewels given by the king, his queens and officials, and the arrangements for its staff. One inscription assigns some four hundred women attached to the temple, with house-sites in two streets near it, and lists a long roll of other servants — dance-masters, singers, drummers, accountants, watchmen, potters, washermen, carpenters, goldsmiths and others — each entitled to a fixed share of paddy from the temple’s lands (Nilakanta Sastri 1955).

    The temple’s wealth did not lie idle. Money and gold endowments were lent to village assemblies and to individuals, and the interest, often paid in paddy, met the cost of lamps, food offerings and festivals; endowments of sheep and cattle were entrusted to shepherds, who supplied ghee for the lamps (Chapter 6, Section 6.4.2). James Heitzman’s analysis of the Chola inscriptions shows that such endowments linked the temple to a wide network of villages, landholders and officials, and that giving to the temple was one of the main ways in which kings, chiefs and local magnates built and displayed their power (Heitzman 1997). Kenneth Hall and R. Champakalakshmi have shown how temple towns such as Kāñcīpuram, Kumbakōṇam and Thanjavur grew as centres of exchange, where merchants’ associations organised markets around the temple and supplied its needs (Hall 1980; Champakalakshmi 1996). The temple was thus at once a landlord, an employer, a lender, a large consumer of goods and a focus of urban growth.

    9.3.2 Tirupati under Vijayanagara¶

    Burton Stein’s study of the inscriptions of the temple of Veṅkaṭeśvara at Tirumala–Tirupati between the mid-fifteenth and the late sixteenth century showed another side of the temple economy (Stein 1960). Donors, among them kings, nobles, officials and merchants, gave cash endowments to the temple for the performance of particular offerings. The temple invested much of this money in the irrigation works of villages it held or controlled, building and repairing tanks and channels; the increased produce paid for the food offerings, and a share of the consecrated food (prasāda) returned to the donors, who could distribute or sell it. Stein argued that the temple thereby acted as an agency for agrarian development in a dry region, channelling the wealth of the Vijayanagara elite into irrigation.

    Arjun Appadurai and Carol Breckenridge drew a broader conclusion from such evidence. In their view the south Indian temple was a redistributive centre: gifts flowed to the deity, and the deity returned them in the form of consecrated food and “honours” — shares in ritual, places in procession, the right to receive prasāda in a particular order — which defined the rank of every participant (Appadurai and Breckenridge 1976). The temple’s economy was therefore also an economy of status. Disputes over honours were among the most persistent sources of litigation in the colonial courts, and Appadurai’s study of the Śrī Pārthasārathi temple at Madras traces how they changed when colonial law redefined the temple’s trustees and their powers (Appadurai 1981).

    9.3.3 Who Was Excluded¶

    The temple economy had its excluded. Its lands were cultivated largely by tenants and labourers, many of them from castes that were forbidden to enter the temple they supported. The women dedicated to the temple as dancers and ritual specialists (devadāsīs) held recognised rights and property in the medieval period, but their position declined over the following centuries, and by the nineteenth century dedication was widely associated with concubinage and exploitation; the practice was prohibited in the Madras Presidency by an Act of 1947. The movements for temple entry in the twentieth century, among whose successes was the Travancore proclamation of 1936, are discussed in Volume 24, Samanvaya. The temple’s wealth was redistributed, but along lines that reproduced the hierarchy of the society around it.

    9.4 Temple Wealth and Its Hazards¶

    The wealth that temples accumulated — gold and silver vessels, jewels for the images, coin from offerings — was not only invested; much of it was stored. Temple treasuries withdrew precious metal from circulation, and in a crisis they were an obvious reserve for rulers who needed money. The Kashmiri chronicle Rājataraṅgiṇī of Kalhaṇa (1148–49) describes how King Harṣa of Kashmir (r. 1089–1101), short of funds for his wars and his court, appointed an officer to strip the temples of their metal images, and Kalhaṇa condemns him for it. Temples were also targets in war between Indian kings, who carried off the images and treasures of their rivals’ shrines as trophies.

    The raids of Maḥmūd of Ghazna into north India between about 1000 and 1027, culminating in the sack of the temple of Somanātha in 1026, are the best-known example of the plunder of temple wealth; the treasure was carried to Ghazna and helped to finance Maḥmūd’s state. Romila Thapar has shown how differently the event was remembered in Persian chronicles, Sanskrit inscriptions and later nationalist writing (Thapar 2004). Richard Eaton’s survey of the evidence for the desecration of temples under Indo-Muslim states between the twelfth and the eighteenth centuries argues that most recorded cases concerned temples closely associated with the authority of enemy kings, attacked in the course of conquest or rebellion, and that the practice continued an older pattern in which Indian kings had seized the state deities of their rivals (Eaton 2000). The argument remains debated, but it is agreed that the concentration of wealth in temples made them both economic and political targets.

    The risk did not end with the medieval period. The East India Company’s administration of the finances of the temple at Purī in the early nineteenth century, and the later supervision of temple funds by government boards (Section 9.10), raised the same question in a new form: who was entitled to the wealth given to a god? The treasure recorded when the vaults of the Padmanābhasvāmī temple at Thiruvananthapuram were opened under the supervision of the Supreme Court in 2011 showed how much could still be held in a single shrine; how such holdings should be valued, guarded and used is a question of law and religion that the courts have answered case by case.

    9.5 Pilgrimage, Fairs and Sacred Towns¶

    9.5.1 The Economy of the Tīrtha¶

    Pilgrimage has been one of the great movers of people in South Asia, and pilgrims were also customers, carriers and traders. The geography of pilgrimage and its literature are treated in Volume 21, Bhūgola; its economic side is considered here. A pilgrimage place (tīrtha) supported a resident population of priests and pilgrims’ guides, lodging-keepers, boatmen, barbers, sellers of flowers, images and ritual goods, and makers of the souvenirs that pilgrims carried home. At Gayā, where Hindus perform the rites for the dead (śrāddha), the hereditary Gayāwāl priests held the exclusive right to officiate and lived on the fees and gifts of pilgrims; L. P. Vidyarthi’s study of the “sacred complex” of Gayā described the organisation of this priestly community and its clients (Vidyarthi 1961). At Haridvār, Prayāga, Banaras and other centres the pilgrims’ guides (paṇḍās) kept registers in which generations of a family’s visits were recorded, so that a pilgrim’s descendants returned to the same guide. At Purī the temple kitchens of Jagannātha cooked food offerings on a very large scale, and the consecrated food was sold to pilgrims in the temple’s own market.

    9.5.2 Fairs and Trade¶

    Many pilgrimage festivals were also great fairs (melās). The fair held at Sonpur, at the confluence of the Gaṇḍak and the Ganges, at the full moon of Kārttika was famous for its trade in cattle, horses and elephants; the fair at Puṣkar in the same month for camels and cattle; and the bathing festivals at Haridvār drew horse-dealers from the north-west as well as pilgrims. Such fairs were, in effect, periodic markets on a regional or supra-regional scale, timed by the religious calendar and protected by the sanctity of the occasion. Rulers taxed them, and merchants used them to buy and sell, collect debts and settle accounts. The connection between festival and market was old: the Tamil poems and the Buddhist texts already describe festival crowds as occasions of trade (Chapter 7).

    9.5.3 Taxing the Pilgrim¶

    Because pilgrims were numerous and their movements predictable, states taxed them. Sultanate and Mughal rulers levied dues on Hindu pilgrims at the major centres; Akbar abolished the pilgrim tax in 1563, early in the reign, as part of the measures by which he sought the support of his non-Muslim subjects, and Abū al-Faḍl presents the decision as a matter of principle. The East India Company, inheriting the practice from earlier rulers in several places, levied pilgrim taxes at Purī, Gayā and Prayāga in the early nineteenth century and for a time supervised the finances of the Jagannātha temple, until evangelical criticism of its “connection with idolatry” led to the abolition of the taxes in 1840 (Volume 21, Bhūgola). The episode shows how large the revenue of pilgrimage could be, and how difficult it was for a state to treat it as an ordinary source of income.

    9.5.4 The Hajj¶

    The pilgrimage to Mecca linked India’s ports to the Red Sea. Surat, the Mughal empire’s principal port in the seventeenth century, was known as the “gate of Mecca”, and the annual pilgrim ships, some of them owned by the emperor and his family, carried goods as well as passengers; the capture of the imperial ship Ganj-i Sawāʾī in 1695 (Chapter 18) was a blow both to the Hajj and to trade. Akbar appointed an officer to lead the annual pilgrim caravan and sent large sums for distribution in Mecca and Medina, and members of the imperial family, among them his aunt Gulbadan Begam, made the pilgrimage. Pilgrims financed their journeys by carrying goods for sale, and the Hajj season shaped the shipping calendar of the western Indian Ocean alongside the monsoon (Chapter 14) (Pearson 2003; Das Gupta 1979).

    9.6 Public Works as Gift: Iṣṭa and Pūrta¶

    Much of what a modern state provides through taxation was provided in pre-modern India through gifts. The dharmaśāstra tradition distinguished two kinds of meritorious act: iṣṭa, the sacrifices and gifts offered in ritual, and pūrta, works of public benefit — the digging of wells and tanks, the building of temples and rest-houses, the planting of gardens and groves, and the distribution of food. The Purāṇas and the digests praise pūrta as open to all, including those not entitled to perform Vedic sacrifices, and the medieval digests devote sections to the merit of each kind of work (Kane 1930).

    The inscriptions show the doctrine in practice. Kings, queens, ministers, merchants and village assemblies recorded the excavation of tanks and wells, the endowment of water stalls (prapā) on roads, and the foundation of feeding houses for travellers and pilgrims. In Gujarat the monumental step-wells (vāv) were among the most ambitious of these works: the Rāṇī kī Vāv at Pāṭaṇ is traditionally attributed to a Caulukya queen of the eleventh century, and the step-well at Aḍālaj near Ahmadābād bears an inscription of 1499 recording its construction by a queen, Rūḍā; a second step-well of the same year in Ahmadābād was built by a lady of the sultan’s household. In the south, rest-houses with free kitchens (chatrams) were endowed along the pilgrim routes, and the Maratha rulers of Thanjavur built a series of them on the road to Rāmeśvaram in the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries. Muslim rulers and nobles built wells, sarāʾīs and bridges as charitable works, often endowed as waqf; the network of rest-houses on the roads is described in Chapter 10, and the architecture of wells and tanks in Volume 16, Vāstu–Śilpa.

    For the economic historian, the significance of this tradition lies in how infrastructure was financed and maintained. A tank or a well built as a gift was usually endowed with land or money for its upkeep, and its maintenance depended on the endowment being managed honestly and on the continued interest of the donor’s family or the local community. Where these held, such works lasted for centuries; where they failed, the works silted up or fell into ruin. Colonial officials who surveyed the tanks of south India in the nineteenth century found many of them in disrepair and debated whether the state or the village should restore them, a debate that continues in the management of tank irrigation today (Volume 15, Kṛṣi and Jīva Vijñāna).

    9.7 Islamic Endowments and Sufi Shrines¶

    Islamic law provided its own instrument for permanent religious and charitable giving, the waqf: property, usually land, buildings or shops, made inalienable and dedicated in perpetuity to a pious or charitable purpose, such as the upkeep of a mosque, a school (madrasa), a Sufi hospice or a public fountain, with a manager (mutawallī) to administer it. A waqf could also provide for the founder’s descendants, on condition that the benefit passed ultimately to a charitable purpose. In India, as elsewhere in the Islamic world, endowed markets and shops were often attached to mosques and shrines, so that the commercial life of a town and the support of its religious institutions were bound together.

    The Delhi sultans and the Mughals also supported religious men and institutions through revenue grants. Under the Mughals the madad-i maʿāsh (“aid for subsistence”) was a grant of the revenue of a small area of land to scholars, holy men, the heads of Sufi hospices and their families, and sometimes to widows; it was administered by the ṣadr, the officer in charge of religious grants. Such grants were made to Hindus as well as Muslims, and Irfan Habib noted that their holders often became petty landholders who invested in cultivation (Habib 1999).

    The shrines of Sufi saints were centres of a distinctive economy. The dargāh of Muʿīn al-Dīn Chishtī at Ajmer, which Akbar visited repeatedly, received land grants and gifts from emperors, nobles and ordinary pilgrims of every faith; its hereditary attendants (khādims) lived on offerings, and the communal kitchen (laṅgar) fed visitors from the two great cauldrons that are said to have been given by Akbar and Jahangir. The annual death-anniversary festival (ʿurs) of a saint was also a fair, at which pilgrims, traders and performers gathered. P. M. Currie’s study of the Ajmer shrine traces how its endowments, its hereditary servants and the imperial patronage it received developed over the centuries (Currie 1989). As with Hindu temples, the shrine’s income was redistributed in food, hospitality and patronage, and its control was a matter of status and frequent dispute.

    9.8 The Sikh Gurdwārā and the Langar¶

    The Sikh community developed its own economic institutions from the time of the early Gurus. The communal kitchen (laṅgar), associated with Guru Nānak’s settlement at Kartārpur and organised by his successors, fed all who came, and the obligation to sit and eat in a common line regardless of caste was one of its central meanings; Sikh tradition relates that Akbar ate in the laṅgar at Goindvāl before meeting Guru Amar Dās. The upkeep of the laṅgar and of the growing community was met by offerings, and Sikhs were expected to give a tenth of their income (dasvandh). From the time of the fourth and fifth Gurus the offerings were collected by appointed representatives (masands), who brought them to the Guru at the great festivals. As the masands became powerful and, in the view of the later tradition, corrupt, Guru Gobind Singh abolished the institution, an act the tradition associates with the founding of the Khālsā, traditionally dated 1699 (Grewal 1990).

    The control of gurdwārā property became a political question in the colonial period. Many shrines had passed under the management of hereditary custodians (mahants), some of whom treated them as private property. The Gurdwara Reform Movement of 1920–1925 sought to bring them under the control of the community, and after a long and sometimes violent struggle the Sikh Gurdwaras Act of 1925 placed the principal shrines of the Punjab under an elected body, the Shiromani Gurdwara Parbandhak Committee (Volume 18, Rājanīti and Dharmaśāstra). The laṅgar of the Golden Temple at Amritsar, which feeds very large numbers every day, is today the best-known example of a religious institution redistributing resources on a massive scale.

    9.9 Christian and Parsi Institutions¶

    The smaller religious communities built economic institutions of their own. The Syrian Christian churches of Kerala held lands and received offerings, and their trustees, drawn from the leading families of each parish, managed church property much as temple trustees managed the property of deities; the merchant privileges granted to the Christians of Kollam in the ninth century are discussed in Section 4.7. The Portuguese brought the religious orders of Catholic Europe. In Goa and the Estado’s other territories the Jesuits, Franciscans, Dominicans and Augustinians acquired villages, rice lands and coconut groves by grant and purchase, and lived on their rents. The Jesuits were among the largest of these corporate landholders, and when the Portuguese crown expelled the order from its dominions in 1759 their estates were confiscated (Subrahmanyam 1993). Protestant missions of the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries, beginning with the Danish mission at Tranquebar, financed schools, presses and hospitals partly from abroad and partly from local endowments.

    The Parsi community of western India developed an especially rich tradition of charitable trusts. The Parsi panchāyat of Bombay, which emerged in the eighteenth century as the community’s governing body, came to administer funds for fire temples, the towers of silence, housing for poor Parsis and relief of the needy. In the nineteenth century the great Parsi merchants of the China trade (Chapter 19) turned much of their wealth into trusts: Jamsetjee Jejeebhoy, the first Indian to be made a baronet, endowed hospitals, schools and relief funds, and later the Tata family founded the trusts that financed the Indian Institute of Science at Bangalore (Palsetia 2001). Parsi philanthropy, organised through trust deeds and managed by trustees under English law, became a model for the modern charitable trusts of other Indian communities, and it shows how the old idea of meritorious giving was adapted to the legal forms of the colonial city (Birla 2009).

    9.10 Religious Endowments and the State¶

    9.10.1 Colonial Regulation¶

    Pre-colonial rulers had protected, endowed, taxed and occasionally plundered religious institutions, but they had rarely administered them. The East India Company at first took over the supervisory role of the rulers it replaced. Bengal Regulation XIX of 1810 and Madras Regulation VII of 1817 gave the Boards of Revenue a general superintendence over religious and charitable endowments, to prevent their misappropriation. Evangelical opinion in Britain objected to a Christian government managing Hindu and Muslim shrines, and the Religious Endowments Act of 1863 withdrew the government from direct management, transferring it to committees and trustees subject to the civil courts.

    The courts then became the main arena. They had to decide who owned temple property, who could manage it and on what terms, and in doing so they recognised the installed deity as a juristic person that could own property through a human manager (Volume 18, Rājanīti and Dharmaśāstra). Islamic endowments followed a parallel course. In 1894 the Privy Council held that a waqf whose benefit went mainly to the founder’s family, with charity only as a remote final purpose, was invalid; Muslim opinion protested, and the Mussalman Wakf Validating Act of 1913 restored the validity of family endowments (Kozlowski 1985). Colonial law on charitable trusts and income tax, as Ritu Birla has shown, drew a line between “public” charity, which received legal and fiscal privileges, and the “private” religious giving of family firms, which was treated with suspicion (Birla 2009).

    From the 1920s the provincial governments, now partly under Indian ministers, returned to direct supervision. The Madras Hindu Religious Endowments Act of 1926–27, passed by a Justice Party ministry, created a statutory board with power to supervise temple finances, settle schemes of management and apply surplus funds to religious, educational and charitable purposes. Its successor, the Madras Hindu Religious and Charitable Endowments Act of 1951, created a government department that still administers many thousands of temples in Tamil Nadu. Comparable laws followed in other states, and the Wakf Act of 1954 set up boards for Islamic endowments.

    9.10.2 Debates¶

    State supervision of religious endowments has been controversial since its beginnings. Its defenders argued that trustees and hereditary managers misused vast properties, that temple wealth should serve the public, and that reform of temple practice, including the admission of all castes, required the authority of the state. Its critics have argued that it subjects religious institutions to political control, diverts their income, and applies to Hindu institutions a regime not applied equally to those of other religions. The constitutional and legal questions are treated in Volume 18 and the ritual ones in Volume 6, Tantra–Āgama. For the economic historian, the long history traced in this chapter explains why the question matters: religious institutions have been, from the Buddhist monastery to the modern temple board, among the largest holders of land and liquid wealth in South Asia, and whoever controls them controls a significant part of the economy of the places in which they stand.

    9.11 Assessment¶

    Monasteries, churches and waqfs played comparable roles in medieval Europe and the Islamic world; what was unusual in South Asia was the number of religious economies working side by side. Their history cuts two ways. Temples managed endowments but were not banks (Chapter 6), and religious giving was never pure piety detached from interest: the inscriptions show that gifts to gods and monks were among the principal ways in which rulers, merchants and landholders turned wealth into authority, and that the benefits of religious wealth were distributed according to rank.
