    Preface¶

    This is Volume 19 of the series Bhāratīya Jñāna Paramparā: Vāṇijya and Samudra: Economy, Trade and Seafaring. It brings together two subjects that are often treated separately: the history of economic thought and commercial institutions in South Asia (vāṇijya, trade, and vārttā, the economic arts), and the history of seafaring and maritime trade (samudra, the sea). They belong together. The merchants who formed guilds, lent money and paid tolls on the roads of the Gangetic plain were often the same people, or worked through the same networks, as those who freighted ships at Bharukaccha, Kāveripaṭṭinam or Cambay.

    The book moves from ideas and institutions to networks and then to the long early modern and colonial transformation. Chapters 2 to 8 deal with economic thought (the Arthaśāstra, the dharmaśāstra, the Tirukkuṟaḷ, Indo-Persian writing on revenue and Buddhist and Jain ethics of wealth), land and the agrarian surplus, guilds and merchant associations, coinage, credit and banking, markets and their regulation, and the organisation of craft production; Chapter 9 examines the place of religious institutions — monasteries, temples, shrines and pilgrimage — in the economy. Chapter 10 follows the overland routes within South Asia, across Central Asia and over the Himalaya. Chapters 11 to 17 turn to the sea: seaborne trade from the Harappan period to the Indo-Roman trade, the Indian Ocean world between c. 600 and 1500, ships and shipbuilding, the monsoon and navigation, port cities and maritime law, the connections with Southeast Asia, and the coastal communities whose knowledge sustained all of this. Chapters 18 and 19 treat the Sultanate, Vijayanagara and Mughal economies, the arrival of the European companies, the trade in slaves, and the colonial economy, including the long-running debates over deindustrialisation and the “drain of wealth”. Chapter 20 follows Indian economic thought from Ranade to the planners of the 1950s, and Chapter 21 considers legacies and the uses of this history today.

    Primary texts are quoted only where the wording and the reference could be confirmed, and figures are given only where a source can be named. This matters especially for the maritime chapters, where the textual evidence is thin. Some claims that are widely repeated are presented here as what they are, open questions or debates: whether the basin at Lothal was a dock, how much of the Yuktikalpataru’s ship lore reflects practice, what the long-run estimates of India’s share of world output can and cannot tell us, and how the economic effects of colonial rule should be measured. The Indian Ocean was never an Indian lake: Arab, Persian, Gujarati, Tamil, Chinese, Southeast Asian, Swahili, Armenian and Jewish merchants all shared it, and the chapters try to give each of them their place. Dates are given as the range accepted in current scholarship, with traditional dates labelled as such; the treatment of the Arthaśāstra’s date in Chapter 2 is an example.

    The Arthaśāstra’s theory of kingship and law is treated in Volume 18 (Rājanīti and Dharmaśāstra), the travellers and the geography of the subcontinent in Volume 21 (Bhūgola), crafts and textiles in Volume 17 (Śilpa–Kāru) and metallurgy in Volume 14 (Rasāyana); this volume refers to them rather than repeating them. References to standard editions and studies are given in the text and collected in the select bibliography, and a glossary of principal terms is provided at the end. A survey of this scope cannot be exhaustive, and it is not a substitute for the specialist literature it cites. Every quotation and figure should, before scholarly use, be checked against the sources named.

    Nikhil Kumar Rajput
    Ramanujan College, University of Delhi
    Guru Pūrṇimā, 2026
