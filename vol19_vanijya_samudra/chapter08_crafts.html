    Chapter 8: Craft Production and Industry¶

    Chapter Overview

    This chapter is about how production was organised, not about how things were made. The agrarian base on which all craft and trade rested, including the early medieval land grants and the debate over “Indian feudalism”, is the subject of Chapter 3. This chapter looks at the main settings in which goods were made: the household, the craft village and caste, the guild, the workshop of the state or the court, the temple, and the merchant who advanced money to producers. It then follows the leading sectors as parts of the economy: textiles, which were for most of recorded history India’s chief manufactured export; metals; gems and beads; and pottery. Caste, unfree and coerced labour, and the work of women are treated as part of the subject, not as asides. Techniques are covered in depth in Volume 17, Śilpa–Kāru (crafts and textiles), and Volume 14, Rasāyana (chemistry and metallurgy). Shipbuilding is treated in Chapter 13.

    8.1 Beginnings: Harappan Craft Production¶

    The cities of the mature Harappan period (c. 2600–1900 BCE) supported a great deal of specialised craft work. Excavations at Harappa, Mohenjo-daro, Chanhu-daro, Lothal and Dholavira have found workshops for stone beads, above all long barrel beads of carnelian drilled with specially made stone drills, for shell bangles and inlay, for steatite seals, for copper and bronze objects, and for glazed faience ornaments. Indus faience is a glazed, sintered quartz paste, not glass; glass appears in South Asia only in the first millennium BCE. Harappan carnelian beads, including etched beads, reached Mesopotamia, where they were found in the royal graves at Ur. Long-distance trade was therefore already one of the outlets for specialised craft (Kenoyer 1998; Possehl 2002; Ratnagar 2004).

    The textile evidence is equally early. Mineralised cotton fibres preserved inside a copper bead from a Neolithic burial at Mehrgarh (6th millennium BCE) are among the earliest known evidence for the use of cotton (Moulherat et al. 2002). Fragments of cotton cloth survive at Mohenjo-daro, attached to metal objects. Silk has also been identified at Harappa and Chanhu-daro. These are fibres of wild, not domesticated, silk moths, dated to the later third millennium BCE (Good et al. 2009). Chinese silk was certainly imported later, but India had its own wild silks long before it.

    How Harappan production was controlled is debated. The uniform weights, bricks and seal styles suggest strong common standards. Workshops were spread through residential quarters, though, and not concentrated in a palace, and Kenoyer and others see a network of specialist households and lineages rather than a single state-run industry (Kenoyer 1998).

    8.2 Forms of Organisation¶

    From the early historic period onward, four broad forms of organising production existed side by side. Their weight changed from period to period and from craft to craft.

    8.2.1 Household, Village and Quarter¶

    Most craft production in Indian history took place in households. A potter’s or weaver’s family worked as a unit, with women and children doing much of the preparatory work: cleaning and spinning cotton, preparing clay, winding yarn. Spinning in particular was done by women in peasant households across the subcontinent. The Pali Jātaka stories describe villages of smiths (kammāra-gāma) and of carpenters (vaḍḍhaki-gāma), and streets or quarters of particular crafts in towns. The concentration of one craft in one place allowed shared access to raw materials and buyers (Chakravarti 2002; Sharma 1983). Ananda Coomaraswamy’s The Indian Craftsman (1909), written under the influence of the Arts and Crafts movement, made this hereditary village and guild craftsman the ideal of a society not yet divided by industrial labour; his book is better read as a critique of industrialism than as a description of how crafts were organised (Coomaraswamy 1909).

    8.2.2 Guilds¶

    Artisans in many towns were organised in śreṇīs (guilds) from at least the last centuries BCE. The inscriptions that record them, above all the Nasik endowments with weavers’ guilds and the Mandasor record of the silk-weavers from Lāṭa, show guilds trusted to hold capital, building temples and keeping a common identity even when some members changed occupation. They are discussed in Chapter 4 (Thaplyal 1996; Ray 1986).

    8.2.3 State, Court and Temple¶

    States and courts ran their own workshops for goods they needed in quantity or wanted to control: arms, cloth for the court and the army, coin, and luxury goods for display and gift (Sections 8.4 and 8.5). In south India from about the tenth century, the great temples were also economic centres. They owned land, employed artisans and settled weavers around them, and endowments to temples were often managed by artisan and merchant bodies (Ramaswamy 1985; Champakalakshmi 1996).

    8.2.4 Merchants and Advances¶

    Finally, merchants organised production by advancing money or raw material to artisans against future delivery. This system, best documented for textiles in the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries, is discussed in Section 8.7. Table 8.1 summarises these forms of organisation.

    8.3 Caste, Status and Labour¶

    Craft skills in India were very often hereditary, and crafts were often linked to particular jātis (endogamous birth groups). Marriage within the group and training within the family helped to keep technical knowledge within a group, and so did a reluctance to teach outsiders. But the link between caste and occupation was neither fixed nor universal. Pali texts show people changing occupation. Guild inscriptions show weavers turning to other work. Groups rose and fell in status over the centuries (Chakravarti 1987).

    Status mattered, and it was unequal. The Pali texts list “low crafts” (hīna-sippa) such as basket-making, pottery, weaving, leather-work and barbering. The Brahmanical law books place most artisans among the Śūdras or among the “mixed castes”. The tenth chapter of the Manusmṛti ascribes a whole range of occupations to castes it explains as the offspring of mixed unions. This is a theory of social order, not a census, but it expressed and justified real hierarchies (Olivelle 2004). Workers in leather, and others whose work was held to be polluting, were treated as untouchable. Some artisan groups contested their place. In the Tamil country, the five metal-, wood- and stone-working crafts grouped as the Kammāḷar claimed descent from Viśvakarman and a status equal to that of Brahmins. Artisan and merchant groups there were also aligned in the medieval “right-hand” and “left-hand” caste divisions (valaṅkai, iṭaṅkai), whose disputes are recorded in inscriptions (Champakalakshmi 1996).

    Not all labour was free. The Arthaśāstra has separate rules for slaves (dāsa) and hired labourers (karmakara) (AŚ 3.13–14). In its own state workshops it used convict labour and the labour of women in dependent positions (Section 8.4). The Nāradasmṛti (c. 5th–6th century CE) distinguishes five kinds of dependent worker: the student, the apprentice, the hired servant, the overseer and the slave, and it lists fifteen kinds of slave (Kane 1930). In early modern Bengal and elsewhere, weavers tied by advances could become dependent on those who advanced them, and the line between contract and coercion was not always clear (Section 8.7).

    8.3.1 Apprenticeship¶

    Most artisans learned in the family. Where a boy learned from a master outside his family, the law books regulated the relationship. The Nāradasmṛti, in its title on breach of contract of service, describes the apprentice (antevāsin). With the consent of his kin, a youth who wishes to learn a craft lives with a master for an agreed period. The master teaches him, feeds him in his own house, does not set him to other work, and treats him like a son. An apprentice who leaves a blameless master before the end of the term may be forced back and punished, and one who has learned the craft early must still serve out the term, the master keeping the fruits of his work (paraphrased after (Kane 1930)). These rules show apprenticeship as a contract, enforced by law, that gave the master a period of bound labour in return for training. The sources do not describe formal grades of novice, journeyman and master of the European kind.

    8.4 State Workshops in the Arthaśāstra¶

    The Arthaśāstra (Chapter 2) describes a state that produces as well as taxes. Book 2 assigns superintendents (adhyakṣa) to mines and metals (2.12), to gold and the goldsmiths’ workshop (2.13–14), to forest produce (2.17), to the armoury (2.18), and to yarn and textiles (the sūtrādhyakṣa, 2.23). These are prescriptions for an ideal kingdom, not records of an actual one (Olivelle 2013; Kangle 1960).

    The chapter on the sūtrādhyakṣa is the clearest picture of state textile production. The superintendent is to have yarn spun by women who had few other means of support: widows, crippled women and unmarried girls, women who had left home as renunciants, women working off fines, the mothers of courtesans, aged female servants of the king, and temple women whose service had ended (AŚ 2.23). Wages were to be set by the quality and quantity of yarn. Women who did not leave their houses were to receive raw material and return yarn through the superintendent’s female servants. The text adds rules on how the superintendent must behave towards these women, and penalties for misappropriation on both sides. The same chapter has weaving, rope-making and the making of armour and straps done by artisans on wages, and by men working off fines (Olivelle 2013). The arrangement gave a livelihood to women without other support, and it gave the state their labour cheaply; the text sees no tension between the two, and a modern reader should keep both in view.

    Book 4 opens with a chapter on “guarding against artisans” (kārukarakṣaṇa, AŚ 4.1), which shows the state as regulator. It sets penalties for weavers who return less cloth than the yarn they received, for washermen who damage or mix up clothes, and for goldsmiths who adulterate metal, and it sets rules on wages and delays (Kangle 1960). Outside the Arthaśāstra, the fragments of Megasthenes (c. 300 BCE) preserved by Strabo say that armour-makers and shipbuilders worked for the king alone and received wages and rations from him (McCrindle 1877). Neither source sets out a general code of wages or of protection for workers; the rules on hired labour are rules of contract (Section 2.6.1).

    8.5 The Kārkhānas of the Sultanate and Mughal Courts¶

    The Delhi Sultanate and its successors organised much court production in kārkhānas (Persian “workshops”). These were departments that both made and stored goods for the ruler’s household, army and gift-giving. According to the Arab writer al-ʿUmarī, Muḥammad bin Tughluq (r. 1325–1351) kept some four thousand silk-weavers making robes of honour. The chronicler Shams Sirāj ʿAfīf describes about three dozen kārkhānas under Fīrūz Shāh Tughluq (r. 1351–1388), each under a noble (Habib 2008; Raychaudhuri and Habib 1982).

    The Mughal kārkhānas are better documented. The Āʾīn-i Akbarī describes the imperial workshops for textiles, arms, perfumes and other goods. The French physician François Bernier, writing in the 1660s, described large halls at Delhi where embroiderers, goldsmiths, painters, lacquer-workers, tailors and other artisans worked under a master. He thought the artisans poorly paid and dependent on the nobles’ favour. Large nobles kept kārkhānas of their own (Richards 1993; Habib 2008). These workshops produced some of the finest goods of the period. They served the court, though, not the market, and most manufacture for sale, including almost all export textiles, was done outside them.

    8.6 Textiles: The Leading Manufacture¶

    8.6.1 Cotton Cloth from the Periplus to the Companies¶

    For most of recorded history, cotton cloth was India’s most important manufactured product and its chief manufactured export. The Periplus of the Erythraean Sea (mid-first century CE) names cotton cloth among the exports of Barygaza (Bharuch) and of the ports of the south and east coasts, including fine muslins from the Ganges region (Casson 1989). The Arthaśāstra lists the best cotton cloth as coming from Madhurā (Madurai), Aparānta (the western coast), Kaliṅga, Kāśī, Vaṅga, Vatsa and Mahiṣa (AŚ 2.11). The same chapter lists the fine dukūla of Vaṅga, and silks, including cīnapaṭṭa from “the land of Cīna” (Olivelle 2013).

    In the medieval centuries, Indian cotton cloth moved through the whole Indian Ocean. Block-printed and resist-dyed Gujarati cottons excavated at Fustat (Old Cairo) date mostly from the later medieval centuries. Indian trade cloths kept for generations as heirlooms in eastern Indonesia have yielded radiocarbon dates going back to about the thirteenth and fourteenth centuries (Guy 1998). Indian cloth also went to East Africa and, through Gujarati, Arab, Persian and Southeast Asian merchants, to almost every port of the Indian Ocean (Chapter 12).

    From the seventeenth century, the Dutch, English and French East India Companies made Indian cottons a mass consumer good in Europe, West Africa and the Americas. The scale was such that England passed the Calico Acts (1700 and 1721) to protect its own woollen and silk industries from Indian printed cottons. The essays in Riello and Roy (2009) trace this “clothing of the world” between 1500 and 1850. The main export regions were Gujarat, the Coromandel coast (painted and printed chintz, and plain cloth) and Bengal (muslins and plain cottons) (Chaudhuri 1978; Prakash 1985; Arasaratnam 1986).

    8.6.2 Producers¶

    Weaving was mostly done in households of hereditary weaving castes, with spinning done by women of many groups. Vijaya Ramaswamy’s study of medieval south India follows two such communities, the Kaikkōḷar and the Sāliyar. It shows their links to temples, the loom taxes they paid, their migrations in search of better conditions and their rise in status (Ramaswamy 1985). Technique changed over time too. Irfan Habib has argued that the spinning wheel and the carding bow reached India with the Sultanate and are first securely attested in the fourteenth century. Earlier spinning was done with the hand spindle (Habib 2008).

    8.6.3 Silk¶

    India’s indigenous silks came from wild moths: tasar, eri and muga. Chinese silk was imported in the early historic period. Domesticated mulberry sericulture spread later, and by the Mughal period Bengal, especially the region around Kasimbazar, was a major producer of raw silk for the Indian market and for the Dutch and English Companies (Prakash 1985). Gujarat’s double-ikat paṭolā were in demand as prestige textiles in Southeast Asia (Guy 1998). Dyes and dyeing, including the mordant chemistry of Indian chintz, are treated in Volume 17, Śilpa–Kāru.

    Historical Note: The Dhaka muslin “thumb-cutting” story

    A popular story says that the East India Company cut off the thumbs of Bengal’s muslin weavers to destroy their industry. No contemporary evidence supports this as a practice or a policy. The story seems to have grown out of a report by William Bolts (1772) that some winders of raw silk had cut off their own thumbs to escape being forced to work for the Company. That report, whatever its accuracy, describes coercion of a different kind. The coercion itself is documented, especially after 1765: weavers were bound by advances to the Company’s agents and prevented from selling to others (Section 8.7). The decline of Dhaka muslin in the nineteenth century had economic causes, which are debated (Chapter 19). The thumb-cutting story is a myth.

    8.7 Putting-Out and the Dādanī System¶

    Weavers rarely had the capital to buy yarn and to wait for a distant buyer. Merchants therefore advanced money, or sometimes yarn, against the delivery of cloth of specified type, length and quality by a fixed date. In Bengal this advance was called dādanī, and the merchants who took it from the European companies were dādanī merchants. They in turn passed advances through brokers (dalāl) and local agents to weavers in the villages. Similar chains of advance operated on the Coromandel coast and in Gujarat (Chaudhuri 1978; Prakash 1985; Arasaratnam 1986).

    Historians disagree about how this system shaped the weavers’ position. Prasannan Parthasarathi argues that before the mid-eighteenth century, south Indian weavers had considerable bargaining power. They could refuse advances, sell to rival buyers or move away, and they used these options in disputes (Parthasarathi 2011). Others stress the weavers’ indebtedness to merchants. After the Company gained political power in Bengal in 1757–1765, it replaced independent merchants with its own salaried agents (gumāshtas). It bound weavers to deliver only to the Company, and it used force to collect cloth. This transition from a competitive to a coercive system is a central theme of the early colonial economy (Prakash 1998). Chapter 18 places the advance system in the wider early modern economy, and Chapter 19 follows its colonial transformation.

    8.8 Metals¶

    The technology of Indian metallurgy is treated in Volume 14, Rasāyana. This section notes only the forms of production and trade.

    8.8.1 Iron and Steel¶

    Iron working spread across the subcontinent during the late second and early first millennia BCE. Iron tools mattered for clearing land and for cultivation, though historians debate how far iron caused the second urbanisation. Iron was smelted by specialist communities, many of them in forest and hill regions, in small bloomery furnaces, and the iron was traded to towns and villages. The best-known monument of Indian iron is the pillar now at Mehrauli in Delhi. It was set up c. 400 CE by a king named Candra, usually identified with Candragupta II, and it stands over seven metres high. Its resistance to rust has been explained by a protective surface film formed on its phosphorus-rich iron (Biswas and Biswas 1996). Making it required the forge-welding of many blooms, so a substantial team of smiths was needed.

    Crucible steel, known in English as “wootz”, was made in small closed clay crucibles in which iron and carbon-bearing material were heated together until a high-carbon steel formed. The word “wootz” comes from ukku, the word for steel in Kannada and Telugu. The production centres were in south India and Sri Lanka, not in northern India. Archaeological evidence includes crucible fragments from Kodumanal in Tamil Nadu, in contexts of the late centuries BCE and early centuries CE, sites in Sri Lanka, and in later centuries the Deccan: for example Konasamudram in Telangana and Gatihosahalli in Karnataka. Steel cakes were traded to Persia and the Arab world, where smiths forged them into the watered-pattern blades that Europeans later called “Damascus” steel. European scientists studied samples sent from India from the 1790s onward (Srinivasan and Ranganathan 2004; Craddock 1995). The production was small in scale, carried out by village specialists. Its importance lay in quality and in long-distance demand.

    8.8.2 Zinc and Brass¶

    Zinc boils at a lower temperature than that needed to smelt it from its ore, so metallic zinc can only be produced by distillation. At Zawar in Rajasthan, lead, silver and zinc ores were mined from the later first millennium BCE. Brass with a fairly high zinc content was made in the north-west in the early centuries CE by the cementation process, which does not require metallic zinc. Distillation of zinc at Zawar, using downward-distilling clay retorts in banks of furnaces, is dated from about the ninth century CE, and it reached a large scale in the following centuries. This is the earliest known industrial production of metallic zinc; earlier dates sometimes given for zinc distillation in India, in the fourth century CE or even BCE, are not supported by the archaeological evidence. In Europe, zinc distillation on a commercial scale began with William Champion at Bristol, who patented his process in 1738. Whether he drew on Asian knowledge is uncertain (Craddock 1995; Biswas and Biswas 1996). Bidri ware, a zinc alloy inlaid with silver, is associated with Bidar in the Deccan and probably dates from the Bahmanī period. It is an example of a court-patronised metal craft.

    8.9 Gems and Beads¶

    Until diamonds were found in Brazil in the 1720s, India was the world’s main source of diamonds. The principal mines were the alluvial workings of the Krishna valley in the Golconda kingdom, such as Kollur, and the mines of Panna in central India. The French jeweller Jean-Baptiste Tavernier visited the Golconda mines in the seventeenth century and described large numbers of labourers digging and washing gravel under contractors who leased the mines from the ruler. He also described the dealers and cutters who bought on the spot (Raychaudhuri and Habib 1982). Gem knowledge was codified in Sanskrit texts. The Arthaśāstra (2.11) describes how gems were to be examined for the treasury. The Bṛhatsaṃhitā of Varāhamihira (6th century CE) has chapters on gems. A genre of “examination of gems” (ratnaparīkṣā) texts also exists, though dating its members is difficult (Biswas and Biswas 1996). Pearl fishing in the Gulf of Mannar is discussed in Chapter 17.

    Bead-making is one of the longest-lived Indian craft industries. After the Harappan carnelian beads, stone beads of carnelian and agate were made at many early historic sites. In western India, carnelian and agate from the Rajpipla hills have been worked at Khambhat (Cambay) for many centuries. At Arikamedu near Puducherry and at other sites of the Indo-Roman period, excavations have found evidence for the production of stone beads and of small drawn glass beads. Small monochrome glass beads of this “Indo-Pacific” type are found across the Indian Ocean and Southeast Asia (Tomber 2008; Ray 2003).

    8.10 Pottery¶

    Pottery was the most widespread craft of all. It was mostly made by village potters (kumbhakāra) for local use. Two wares of the early historic period, however, show organised production for wider markets.

    • Northern Black Polished Ware (NBPW). This is a fine, glossy ware made from about the sixth century BCE (its beginning is variously dated between the seventh and fifth centuries) to about the second century BCE, at its peak in the Gangetic towns of the mahājanapada and Mauryan periods. It is regarded as a luxury tableware. Its wide distribution and consistent quality imply specialist workshops, but where and by whom it was made is still being studied.

    • Rouletted Ware. This is a fine grey-black ware with bands of rouletted decoration, of about the second century BCE to the second century CE. It was once thought to be Mediterranean. It is now regarded as Indian, and laboratory analyses point to a source in lower Bengal. It was carried along the east coast of India and to Sri Lanka and Southeast Asia, and its distribution is one of the markers of the Bay of Bengal trade (Tomber 2008; Ray 2003).

    8.11 Women and Work¶

    Women’s labour ran through every sector described in this chapter, but it is hard to see in the sources. The normative texts were written by men, most of them brāhmaṇas, and describe what women ought to do rather than what they did. Inscriptions record gifts, not wages. Company records count pieces of cloth, not the hands that spun the yarn. What follows brings together the main kinds of evidence. It does not claim more than they can show, and it does not turn women’s work into either a story of hidden equality or one of simple oppression.

    8.11.1 Spinning and the Textile Household¶

    Spinning was the most widespread form of women’s work for cash or for the market. The Arthaśāstra’s superintendent of yarn, discussed above (Section 8.4), drew on the labour of women without other support, and household spinning by women of peasant and artisan families is attested in every period (Section 8.2). In the great age of the export trade in the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries, the cloth bought by the European companies depended on the yarn of very large numbers of women spinning part-time in rural households in Bengal, on the Coromandel coast and in Gujarat. The output of a single loom needed the work of several spinners.

    In weaving households the division of labour was usually by gender. In most regions men worked the loom, while women and children cleaned and carded the cotton, wound bobbins and prepared and sized the warp, though practice varied. In Assam and Manipur, by contrast, weaving on the household loom was above all women’s work, and the cloth of eri and muga silk woven in the household was both for family use and for sale. Women’s work in weaver households was essential to output but was counted, and paid, as part of the household’s work under its male head.

    This dependence had a harsh consequence in the nineteenth century. When machine-spun yarn from Britain, and later from Indian mills, became cheap, hand-spinning collapsed far more quickly and completely than hand-weaving, which survived by using mill yarn. The loss of earnings fell mainly on women, often the poorest, for whom spinning had been one of the few sources of independent income (Section 8.12). When Gandhi made the spinning wheel and khādī the symbol of the national movement, he was reviving above all a form of women’s work (Chapter 21).

    8.11.2 Women as Donors¶

    The donative inscriptions of the early historic Buddhist sites show that some women controlled wealth in their own names. Among the many donors recorded on the railings and gateways of the stūpas at Sāñcī and Bharhut are nuns (bhikhunī) and laywomen. Some are named alone, others as the wife, mother or daughter of a householder, a merchant or a craftsman (Lüders 1912). The same is true of the rock-cut monasteries of the western Deccan, at Karle, Nasik, Kanheri, Junnar and elsewhere. Here women of merchant and artisan families gave cisterns, pillars and cells, and the queen mother Gautamī Balaśrī dedicated a cave at Nasik in the reign of Vāsiṣṭhīputra Puḷumāvi (Ray 1986). These gifts show that women could hold and dispose of property, especially in commercial families, and that public generosity was one way in which they and their families gained standing. They do not tell us how the women had acquired the wealth, or how far they could have spent it without the consent of their kin. Women appear as donors in the guild inscriptions too, but not as guild officers (Section 4.8).

    8.11.3 Strīdhana and Women’s Property¶

    The dharmaśāstras recognised a category of women’s own property, strīdhana. The Manusmṛti lists six kinds: what was given before the nuptial fire, what was given at the bridal procession, what was given as a token of love, and what was received from a brother, a mother or a father (Manu 9.194). It also rules on how this property passed to the woman’s children after her death. The same text, in a much-quoted verse, declares that a wife, a son and a slave have no property of their own, and that what they earn belongs to the man to whom they belong (Manu 8.416) (Olivelle 2004). The tension between these rules is real and was never fully resolved. Later commentators extended or narrowed the category. Vijñāneśvara’s Mitākṣarā (late eleventh or early twelfth century), the most influential commentary on the Yājñavalkyasmṛti, defined strīdhana broadly to include property a woman acquired by inheritance, purchase, partition and other means, while the Bengal school of the Dāyabhāga defined it more narrowly. The Yājñavalkyasmṛti itself placed the wife first among the heirs of a man who died without a son (Kane 1930). How far these rules governed practice is uncertain. Jewellery, the most common form of strīdhana, was a store of value that a family could draw on in hard times, and it was often under the practical control of the husband’s household.

    8.11.4 Markets, Merchant Households and Wage Work¶

    Women were prominent in petty trade. The retailing of fish, vegetables, milk and other perishables was in many regions largely in women’s hands. The best-documented examples come from fishing communities, where women who did not go to sea sold the catch (Chapter 17). In Manipur the large market of Imphal, known as Ima Keithel (“mothers’ market”), has long been run by women traders. These were not marginal activities. They supplied towns with food and gave women some cash of their own, though usually in small amounts and on terms set by wholesalers and moneylenders.

    In the great merchant households women were rarely traders in their own right, but their position mattered to the business. James Laidlaw’s study of Jain merchant families in Jaipur describes how women’s religious practice, above all their public fasts, was celebrated by the whole family and bore on its reputation for piety and restraint. That reputation was part of the creditworthiness on which merchant families depended (Laidlaw 1995). Women of the Mughal court sometimes invested directly in trade: the ship Raḥīmī, owned by the emperor Jahāngīr’s mother, was seized by the Portuguese in 1613, an incident that led to an open breach between the Mughals and the Portuguese.

    At the bottom of the economy women did much of the heaviest work. They transplanted, weeded and harvested rice, carried earth on construction sites, worked in salt pans and processed fish (Chapter 17). Many enslaved people in Indian history were women, employed above all in domestic service and given, sold or pledged as property; the texts discuss them chiefly from the owner’s point of view (Chakravarti 1985). In the colonial period women were recruited in large numbers for the tea gardens of Assam and worked in the coal mines of eastern India until underground work by women was prohibited in the 1930s. In the Bengal jute mills, Samita Sen has shown, the share of women in the workforce fell over the early twentieth century, as employers, the state and workers’ own families came to treat the male worker as the family breadwinner (Sen 1999).

    The evidence is uneven. It is rich for the law books and for a few modern ethnographies, and thin for the women who did most of the work. Its general direction is clear, however. Women’s labour was indispensable to India’s craft and commercial economy, and it was usually less well rewarded, less secure and less visible than men’s.

    8.12 Craft Production after 1800¶

    What happened to Indian crafts under colonial rule is the subject of the deindustrialisation debate, whose positions, from the nationalist economists and Amiya Kumar Bagchi to Morris D. Morris and Tirthankar Roy, are set out in Section 19.3. Two parts of the picture are broadly accepted. First, India’s exports of cotton cloth collapsed in the early nineteenth century and the country became an importer of machine-made cloth. Second, the effects on employment varied greatly by region and sector. The often-quoted fall in India’s share of world manufacturing output, from about a quarter in 1750 to under 2 per cent in 1900, comes from Paul Bairoch’s long-run estimates (Bairoch 1982). Like Maddison’s estimates of output (Section 18.5.6), they are rough reconstructions, and the fall in India’s share reflects the industrialisation of Europe and North America as well as any decline in India itself.
