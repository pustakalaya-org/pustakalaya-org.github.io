    Chapter 3: Land, Agrarian Surplus and Revenue¶

    Chapter Overview

    Every form of production and trade described in this volume rested on agriculture. This short chapter gives the agrarian economy a place of its own. It first surveys the state’s claim on the harvest from the Arthaśāstra to the British settlements, as a guide to the fuller treatments in other chapters. It then looks at the early medieval land grants and the debate over “Indian feudalism”, at the village and locality assemblies of the Tamil country, at irrigation as an investment of the surplus, and at the ways in which the surplus entered the wider economy of exchange.

    Throughout the period covered by this volume the great majority of people lived from the land, and the land was the main source of wealth and of state revenue. The agrarian surplus fed the artisans and the towns. It supplied the raw materials of the leading crafts: cotton, indigo and other dyes, sugarcane, oilseeds and hides. And the revenue taken from it paid for the courts, temples and armies that were the chief buyers of fine manufactures and imported luxuries. How crops were grown, irrigated and stored is the subject of Volume 15, Kṛṣi and Jīva Vijñāna. This chapter looks only at the economy of surplus, revenue and exchange.

    3.1 The State’s Claim on the Harvest¶

    The ruler’s right to a share of the crop is the one constant in the fiscal history traced in this book. The normative texts state it as a fraction. The Arthaśāstra knows a customary sixth (bhāga) besides the produce of crown land (sītā) and further levies whose meaning is debated, and Manu allows the king a sixth, an eighth or a twelfth of grain (Section 2.3.2). In practice the fraction was a starting point rather than a rule. Rulers raised or lowered the demand, collected it in kind or in money, and shared it with the intermediaries through whom it was gathered. Table 3.1 sets out the main forms the claim took and where each is discussed.

    Table 3.1. The state’s claim on the harvest: main forms and where they are treated in this volume
    Period Form of the claim Treated in
    Arthaśāstra and dharmaśāstra (early centuries CE) Customary share (bhāga) of a sixth; crown land worked by the state; water rates; further levies (bali, kara) Section 2.3.2
    Early medieval kingdoms, c. 400–1300 Royal share and dues transferred by grant to brāhmaṇas, temples and monasteries Section 3.2
    Chola and later Tamil country Dues assessed and collected through village and locality assemblies; temple lands Section 3.3; Chapter 9
    Delhi Sultanate, 1206–1526 Revenue assigned as iqṭāʿ; under ʿAlāʾ al-Dīn Khaljī, half the produce of the Doab by measurement Section 18.2.1
    Vijayanagara and the nāyakas Territories held on condition of military service and dues Section 18.3
    Mughal empire Zabṭ cash rates per unit of area; jāgīr assignments; zamīndārs as collectors Section 18.5
    Marathas Chauth and sardeshmukhī on revenue assessed by others; saranjām assignments Section 18.5.9
    British India, from 1793 Money demand on a proprietor or cultivator under the Permanent, ryotwari and mahalwari settlements; land saleable for arrears Section 19.5

    Three features run through this long history. The demand was assessed on land or crop, not on income, so that it fell on cultivators in bad years as well as good, and remission depended on the ruler’s discretion. It was collected through layers of intermediaries, from village headmen and hereditary accountants (Section 6.7) to chiefs, zamīndārs, assignees and revenue farmers, whose rights formed a hierarchy of claims on the same harvest. And a growing part of it was demanded in money, which obliged cultivators to sell their crops and tied the village to the grain merchant and the moneylender (Section 3.5). The scale of the demand is harder to know than its form. The textual fractions are norms; Irfan Habib’s judgement that the Mughal demand often approached half of the produce rests on the schedules of the Āʾīn-i Akbarī (Habib 1999); and colonial assessments, heavy at first, fell in real terms during the later nineteenth century as prices rose (Section 19.5).

    3.2 Land Grants and Copper-Plate Charters¶

    The best evidence for rural society in early medieval India is the land grant. The earliest epigraphic grants that transfer land together with fiscal privileges are those of the Sātavāhana kings to Buddhist monks in the western Deccan, for example at Nasik, in the early centuries CE. They free the granted field from the entry of royal officers and from the king’s right to dig for salt (Ray 1986). From the Gupta period onward such grants multiply. By the end of the first millennium thousands had been issued across the subcontinent, most of them engraved on copper plates (tāmraśāsana) that the donee kept as a title deed.

    Three kinds of grant are especially common. An agrahāra was land, often a whole village, given to one or more brāhmaṇas. In Tamil inscriptions a village granted to a community of brāhmaṇas is a brahmadeya. A devadāna (Tamil tēvatāṉam) was land given to a temple, and land given to Jain and Buddhist establishments appears in Tamil inscriptions as paḷḷiccantam. Grants followed a set form. They name the donor and his genealogy, the donee, the land and its boundaries, and the officials and villagers who were informed. They list the rights transferred: the king’s share of the crop and other dues, and often exemption from the entry of royal soldiers and police (acāṭabhaṭapraveśya). They declare the gift valid “for as long as the moon and the sun endure”, and they end with conventional verses, ascribed to Vyāsa, which promise long ages in heaven to the giver of land and as many in hell to anyone who takes it back (Salomon 1998). The Damodarpur copper plates from north Bengal, which record the sale of state land for religious gifts with the approval of the district council, are discussed in Section 4.6.3.

    What the grants transferred was, in most cases, a right to revenue rather than the soil itself. The cultivators stayed on the land and now paid their dues to the donee instead of the king. Some grants go further and transfer fines, the right to forced labour (viṣṭi) or the obligation of the villagers to obey the donee. Grants to brāhmaṇas and temples also carried agriculture, the written calendar, Sanskrit learning and the ritual authority of the king into areas that had been forest or the land of tribal peoples. Historians agree on these facts. They disagree sharply about what they meant.

    Debate: Was there “Indian feudalism”?

    R. S. Sharma, in Indian Feudalism (1965) and later work, argued that the multiplication of land grants from the Gupta period onward produced a feudal social order in India (Sharma 1965, 1983). In his account, kings paid priests, officials and vassals (sāmantas) with land rather than cash, and they gave away fiscal, administrative and judicial rights with it. This created a class of landed intermediaries between the state and the peasant and fragmented political power. Peasants were burdened with new dues and forced labour and were tied more closely to the land. Trade, towns and the use of coin declined, and villages became more self-sufficient (Sharma 1987). Sharma built on D. D. Kosambi, who had spoken of feudalism “from above”, created by royal grants, and “from below”, through the rise of landholders within the village (Kosambi 1956).

    The thesis was criticised from several directions. Harbans Mukhia asked whether India had had feudalism at all. He argued that the Indian peasant kept control of the process of production, that there was no serfdom and no demesne worked by labour services on the European model, and that a category built for medieval Europe did not fit Indian agrarian relations; Sharma replied, and the debate continued for many years (Mukhia 1981). B. D. Chattopadhyaya proposed an “integrative” reading. In his view the land grants accompanied the spread of settled agriculture into new areas, the turning of tribal groups into peasants, the formation of many regional and local states, and the growth of new markets and temple centres. That is a picture of expansion, not of decline (Chattopadhyaya 1994). For the Chola country, Burton Stein described a “segmentary state” in which the king’s ritual sovereignty reached much further than his administrative control, and the real units of rural society were the peasant localities (nāṭu) (Stein 1980). Other historians of south India have pointed to the Chola land surveys and revenue records as evidence of a stronger state than Stein allowed. The coin evidence is discussed in Section 5.7, and the fate of guilds and towns in Section 4.6.5.

    Most historians now accept that land grants transformed rural society and that the change varied greatly from region to region. Fewer accept the word “feudalism” or a single story of decline.

    3.3 Village and Locality Assemblies in the South¶

    In the Tamil country, inscriptions from about the eighth century onward show rural society organised through assemblies. The ūr was the assembly of the landholders of an ordinary village. The sabhā (or mahāsabhā) was the assembly of the brāhmaṇa landholders of a brahmadeya. The nagaram was the body of merchants of a market town, often one for each locality. The nāṭu, a cluster of villages, had its own assembly of leading peasant landholders (nāṭṭār). These bodies assessed and collected dues, managed common land and water, received endowments, and acted together with temples and merchant groups (Champakalakshmi 1996; Hall 1980). Kenneth Hall has argued that the nagaram served as the market of its nāṭu, linking the agrarian localities to wider trade (Section 4.7).

    The best-known records of a sabhā are two inscriptions of 919 and 921 CE, in the reign of the Chola king Parāntaka I, at Uttaramērūr near Kanchipuram. They lay down how the brahmadeya’s committees (vāriyam) were to be chosen, by drawing palm-leaf tickets from a pot (kuḍavōlai). They also set qualifications for candidates: ownership of tax-paying land and a house, an age range, Vedic learning, and exclusion of men who had failed to render accounts and of their relatives. There were committees for the annual supervision of the village, for gardens and for tanks. The records are often cited in modern India as evidence of “ancient democracy”. They show a careful system of rotation and accountability, but it was confined to a landholding brāhmaṇa élite. The cultivators and labourers who worked the land had no voice in it.

    3.4 Irrigation as Investment¶

    Irrigation is the clearest case of investment of the agrarian surplus. The Junagadh rock inscription of the Śaka ruler Rudradāman (c. 150 CE), one of the earliest long inscriptions in Sanskrit, records the repair of the Sudarśana reservoir near Girnar. According to the inscription, the reservoir had been built under Candragupta Maurya by his provincial governor Puṣyagupta and provided with conduits under Aśoka. A storm had breached it. Rudradāman had it rebuilt from his own treasury, the inscription says, without oppressing the people with taxes, forced labour or “benevolences”. Three centuries later, under Skandagupta, the dam burst again in 455–456 CE and was rebuilt the following year, as recorded in another inscription on the same rock (Salomon 1998). The stone thus records more than seven centuries of public investment in a single irrigation work.

    In the south the typical work was the tank (Tamil ēri, Kannada kere), an earthen embankment holding back monsoon run-off to irrigate rice fields below it. Hundreds of inscriptions record the building of tanks, the cutting of sluices and channels, and endowments of land or money for desilting and repair. The donors include kings, village assemblies, temples, merchants and individual landholders (Champakalakshmi 1996). Tanks needed regular collective labour and a body to organise it, which is one reason for the importance of the village assemblies and their tank committees. A Tamil inscription at Takuapa in peninsular Thailand even records a tank placed under the protection of a merchant association (Chapter 12). Gifts for tanks and wells as works of religious merit are discussed in Section 9.6.

    3.5 From Surplus to Exchange¶

    The agrarian surplus entered the wider economy in several ways. Revenue in grain fed the households of kings, donees and temples. Revenue in cash, and the need to pay it, pushed cultivators to sell part of their crop. Temples that received land redistributed its produce to priests, musicians, cooks, artisans and pilgrims, and they lent grain and money to village assemblies (Section 6.4.2). The weekly or periodic rural markets (haṭṭa) named in early medieval inscriptions connected villages to towns (Chapters 7 and 10). Some crops were grown above all for sale and export: pepper in Malabar, cotton in Gujarat and the Deccan, sugar and indigo in many regions, rice for the coastal trade (Chapter 12). Under the Mughals, Irfan Habib has argued, the demand for revenue in cash was one of the main forces drawing the peasant economy into the market (Habib 1999). Craft production and trade were large and sophisticated, but they always depended on this agrarian base. Bad harvests and famine, which no part of the economy escaped, were the clearest reminder of it (Section 7.7).

    Further Reading¶

    The feudalism debate can be followed in Sharma (1965), Mukhia (1981) and Chattopadhyaya (1994), and the south Indian agrarian order in Stein (1980), Hall (1980) and Champakalakshmi (1996). Salomon (1998) introduces the land grants as inscriptions. For the Mughal agrarian system the essential work is Habib (1999), and for the colonial settlements Kumar (1983).
