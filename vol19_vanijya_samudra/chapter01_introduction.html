    Chapter 1: Introduction: Economy, Trade and the Sea in South Asian History¶

    Chapter Overview

    This chapter sets out the scope of the volume. It explains why economic thought, commercial institutions and seafaring are treated together; sketches the geography that shaped South Asian trade; offers an outline of the main periods from the Harappan cities to the colonial economy; reviews the kinds of evidence on which the history rests and their limits; and flags the debates that recur through the book, from the interpretation of Lothal to the measurement of colonial “drain”. It ends with a plan of the chapters.

    1.1 Vāṇijya and Samudra¶

    Two Sanskrit words give this volume its title. Vāṇijya (also vaṇijyā) is trade, the activity of the vaṇij or merchant. Samudra is the sea. The Sanskrit tradition of statecraft placed trade within a larger field it called vārttā, “livelihood” or the economic arts. The Arthaśāstra, a composite treatise that reached its present form between about 50 BCE and 300 CE and that tradition attributes to Kauṭilya, minister of Candragupta Maurya (Section 2.1.3), lists vārttā as one of the four bodies of knowledge a ruler must master, alongside philosophy (ānvīkṣikī), the Veda (trayī) and the science of government (daṇḍanīti), and defines it as agriculture, animal husbandry and trade (AŚ 1.4.1) (Olivelle 2013; Kangle 1960). The same text treats wealth (artha) as the foundation on which the other aims of life rest. These ideas, and the institutions that grew up around them, are the subject of the first group of chapters.

    The second subject is the sea. For more than four thousand years the coasts of the subcontinent were linked to the Persian Gulf, the Red Sea, East Africa, Sri Lanka, Southeast Asia and China. Ships and the monsoon winds, the ports and their merchants, and the laws and customs that governed them are the subject of a second group. The two subjects are not separate in practice. The guild members whose endowments are recorded in the cave inscriptions of Nasik, the moneylenders and hundī brokers of Gujarat and the Coromandel, and the weavers whose cloth filled the holds of ships bound for the Red Sea or Melaka belonged to the same economic world as the ship-owners and pilots. The volume therefore treats commerce and seafaring as a single history.

    Three cautions frame what follows. First, “Indian” economic history is the history of a very large and varied region over a very long time. No single “Indian commercial civilisation” existed with a fixed set of institutions; there were many economies, regional and changing, connected by trade. Second, South Asia never owned the Indian Ocean. Its ports were shared, and often dominated, by merchants from elsewhere: Arab, Persian, Armenian, Jewish, Chinese, Malay, Javanese and Swahili traders worked alongside Gujarati, Tamil, Malayali, Bengali and Sinhala ones, and the chapters on the sea try to give all of them their place. Third, the evidence is uneven, and some of the most widely repeated statements about Indian commerce and seafaring, such as that the basin at Lothal was the “world’s first dockyard” or that India produced a third of the world’s output, go beyond it. Where a question is debated, this book sets out the positions; the best-known claims of this kind are gathered and examined in Section 21.7.

    1.2 Geography and the Shape of Trade¶

    The subcontinent’s geography shaped its economy in lasting ways. In the north, the alluvial plains of the Indus and the Ganga supported dense agrarian populations and the surpluses on which towns and states depended. The great rivers were themselves routes of trade: river craft carried grain, salt, timber and cloth along the Ganga and its tributaries until the railway age (Deloche 1993). The north-western mountain passes linked the plains to Afghanistan, Iran and Central Asia; through them came horses, which South Asia bred poorly and imported in large numbers for many centuries, and through them went cotton textiles, indigo, sugar and spices. The Deccan plateau and the peninsula, rich in minerals, cotton and forest products, were tied to the north by routes along the river valleys and across the Vindhyas, and to the sea by the ports of both coasts (Chapter 10).

    The sea frames the peninsula on three sides. The west coast, from Sindh and Gujarat to Malabar, faces the Arabian Sea and, beyond it, the Gulf, the Red Sea and East Africa. The east coast, from Bengal and Odisha to the Coromandel, faces the Bay of Bengal and Southeast Asia. Sri Lanka stands at the junction of the two. Along both coasts, river mouths, estuaries and lagoons provided harbours, though many silted up and moved over the centuries, so that the great ports of one period (Bharukaccha, Tāmralipti, Kāveripaṭṭinam) were replaced by others (Cambay, Surat, Hughli, Masulipatnam) in later ones.

    Above all, the Indian Ocean is governed by the monsoon. In the northern summer the winds blow from the south-west; in winter they reverse and blow from the north-east. This seasonal reversal made long voyages possible in sailing ships, but it also imposed a strict calendar: ships had to leave and return within fixed windows, the west coast was largely closed to shipping at the height of the south-west monsoon, and merchants often had to wait months in foreign ports for the wind to turn. The rhythm of the monsoon is the single most important fact in the history of Indian Ocean trade (Chaudhuri 1985; Pearson 2003); it is described in Chapter 14.

    Key Insight: A Useful Simplification

    For convenience this book sometimes speaks of three overlapping zones of South Asian trade, a working scheme of its own rather than a classification taken from any one historian: the northern plains, oriented by river and road towards Central Asia and Iran; the peninsula, with its own internal networks; and the coastal belts of Gujarat, Konkan and Malabar, Coromandel and Bengal, which faced the ocean. The division is a heuristic, not a description of fixed regions: goods, merchants and capital constantly crossed between the zones, and the coastal belts often had stronger links with ports across the sea than with the interior.

    1.3 An Outline History¶

    The chapters that follow are arranged by theme, not by period. This section offers a short chronological frame to which they can be related. The dates are those accepted in current scholarship; traditional dates, where they differ, are noted as such in the chapters.

    1.3.1 The Harappan Civilisation¶

    The cities of the mature Harappan or Indus civilisation (c. 2600–1900 BCE) used standardised weights based on a binary and then decimal progression, produced seals that probably marked ownership and identity, and specialised in crafts such as long carnelian beads, shell working and copper and bronze metallurgy (Kenoyer 1998; Possehl 2002). Harappan objects and seals found in Mesopotamia and the Gulf, and Akkadian references to the land of Meluhha (generally identified with the Indus region) and its ships, show that the Harappans traded by sea with the Gulf and Mesopotamia, probably through intermediaries in Dilmun (Bahrain) and Magan (Oman) (Ratnagar 2004). The large brick basin at Lothal in Gujarat was interpreted by its excavator, S. R. Rao, as a tidal dock; other scholars have read it as a reservoir or tank, and its function remains debated (Rao 1979; Leshnik 1968). Chapter 11 sets out the evidence.

    1.3.2 Early Historic Urbanisation¶

    After the decline of the Harappan cities, the second great phase of urbanisation came in the Ganga valley in the mid-first millennium BCE. Towns such as Rājagṛha, Śrāvastī, Kauśāmbī, Vārāṇasī and Ujjayinī grew within the territorial states (mahājanapadas) of which Magadha became the most powerful. This period saw the first coinage of the subcontinent (silver punch-marked coins, from perhaps the fifth or fourth century BCE), the spread of the Northern Black Polished Ware, the organisation of artisans and traders into corporate groups, and the rise of Buddhism and Jainism, both of which drew important support from merchants and householders (Thapar 2002; Chakravarti 1987). The Maurya empire (c. 321–185 BCE), whose most famous ruler Aśoka reigned c. 268–232 BCE, unified most of the subcontinent and maintained the royal highways later described by Greek observers (McCrindle 1877).

    1.3.3 The Early Centuries CE¶

    The centuries around the beginning of the Common Era were a period of intense commercial activity. The Kuṣāṇa empire straddled the routes between India, Central Asia and China; the Sātavāhanas in the Deccan and the Western Kṣatrapas in Gujarat and Malwa controlled the ports and the routes behind them; and in the far south the Tamil chiefdoms of the Caṅkam age traded with Roman Egypt through ports such as Muziris and Arikamedu. Roman coins, amphorae and tableware in India, and Indian pottery, pepper and Tamil-Brāhmī inscriptions in Egyptian Red Sea ports, attest to this trade (Tomber 2008; De Romanis 2020). Guilds made permanent endowments to Buddhist monasteries in the western Deccan, and their inscriptions are among the best evidence for commercial organisation in early India (Ray 1986; Thaplyal 1996).

    1.3.4 From the Guptas to c. 1000 CE¶

    The Gupta period (c. 320–550 CE) is known for its gold coinage and for the guild seals of Vaiśālī. For the centuries that followed, historians disagree about the economy. R. S. Sharma argued for a decline of towns, trade and coinage and a shift to a “feudal” order, while others, notably B. D. Chattopadhyaya, see the formation of new regional states, market centres and networks rather than decline (Sharma 1987; Chattopadhyaya 1994). In the south, the period saw the growth of temple-centred economies and of powerful merchant associations, such as the Ayyāvoḷe Five Hundred and the Maṇigrāmam, whose inscriptions appear from Karnataka to Sumatra (Abraham 1988; Champakalakshmi 1996).

    1.3.5 The Indian Ocean World, c. 600–1500¶

    The rise of Islam and of the Abbasid caliphate, and the expansion of Tang and Song China’s maritime trade, created a vast trading world from the Red Sea to the South China Sea. Indian ports took part in it alongside Arab and Persian ports in the Gulf, the Śrīvijaya polity in Sumatra, and Chinese ports such as Quanzhou. The Chola kings of south India sent expeditions to Sri Lanka and, in 1025, raided the ports of Śrīvijaya. Jewish merchants whose letters survive in the Cairo Geniza traded between Egypt, Aden and the Malabar coast (Goitein and Friedman 2008). These networks are the subject of Chapter 12.

    1.3.6 Sultanate, Vijayanagara, Mughal¶

    From the thirteenth century the Delhi Sultanate, and from the sixteenth the Mughal empire, ruled large parts of the subcontinent and created monetised, revenue-based states with large cities, courts and workshops. Vijayanagara dominated the south from the fourteenth to the sixteenth century. Gujarati, Bengali and Coromandel textiles were exported across the Indian Ocean and, from the seventeenth century, to Europe. The arrival of the Portuguese in 1498, and later of the Dutch, English and French companies, changed the political conditions of trade without, at first, displacing Asian merchants (Raychaudhuri and Habib 1982; Das Gupta and Pearson 1987). Chapter 18 treats this period.

    1.3.7 The Colonial Economy¶

    After Plassey (1757) the English East India Company became a territorial power in Bengal and, by the mid-nineteenth century, across the subcontinent. The economic consequences of colonial rule (for manufacturing, shipping, agriculture, famine, and the transfer of resources to Britain) were debated by Indian nationalists such as Dadabhai Naoroji and R. C. Dutt, and are still debated by historians today (Kumar 1983; Roy 2000). Chapter 19 presents that debate. Table 1.1 gives a simplified chronology of the periods discussed in this volume.

    1.4 Wealth and Its Ethics¶

    Indian thought did not treat commerce with the suspicion found in some ancient traditions. The scheme of the four aims of life (puruṣārtha) places artha, material well-being and power, beside dharma, kāma and mokṣa. The Arthaśāstra makes wealth the root of the others; the dharmaśāstra literature, while ranking dharma first, regulates trade, interest, partnership and the duties of the merchant in detail (Chapters 2 and 6). The Buddhist canon is full of merchants: the banker Anāthapiṇḍika, who gave the Jetavana grove to the order, is among the most celebrated lay followers, and the Jātaka stories regularly feature caravan leaders and sea voyages (Chakravarti 1987; Chakravarti 2002). Jain merchant communities, especially in western India, combined great commercial success with an ethic of restraint and gift-giving that anthropologists have studied closely (Jaini 1979; Laidlaw 1995).

    This tradition had a harsher side. The same texts that praise honest trade assume a hierarchy in which the merchant’s status, the interest he may charge, and the penalties he faces depend on his varṇa; the Arthaśāstra and the law books regulate slavery and bonded labour; and the state’s interest in trade was largely an interest in revenue. Merchants themselves were drawn from many castes and communities, and their position in society varied greatly between regions and periods (Thapar 2002). These questions are taken up where they arise, especially in Chapters 2, 4, 6 and 8.

    1.5 The Evidence and Its Limits¶

    The history in this volume rests on several kinds of evidence, each with its own strengths and problems.

    Normative texts
    The Arthaśāstra, the Dharmasūtras and Dharmaśāstras (Manu, Yājñavalkya, Nārada, Bṛhaspati, Kātyāyana) and their commentaries describe how trade, credit and guilds should be regulated. They are invaluable for categories and concepts but are prescriptive and hard to date; they do not show how often their rules were applied (Olivelle 1999; Kane 1930).
    Narrative and poetic literature
    The Pali Jātakas and Vinaya, Jain narrative literature, Sanskrit drama and prose, and the Tamil Caṅkam poems describe markets, caravans, ports and ships. They are literary works, written for other purposes, and their details must be used with care.
    Inscriptions
    Thousands of stone and copper-plate inscriptions record gifts, endowments, tolls, guild agreements and royal grants. They are the most reliable dated evidence for institutions such as guilds and merchant associations (Salomon 1998; Lüders 1912).
    Coins
    Coin finds and hoards show the circulation of money, the reach of states and the direction of trade (Chapter 5).
    Archaeology
    Excavated sites, from Harappan towns to Arikamedu, Pattanam and the Red Sea ports of Berenike and Myos Hormos, provide physical evidence of goods and their movement (Ray 2003; Tomber 2008).
    Foreign accounts
    Greek and Roman writers (Megasthenes as quoted by later authors, the Periplus Maris Erythraei, Pliny, Ptolemy), Chinese pilgrims and officials, Arabic and Persian geographers, the Jewish merchants of the Cairo Geniza, Ma Huan, Ibn Baṭṭūṭa and the Portuguese chroniclers offer external views, often precise on goods and prices but limited by their authors’ interests and misunderstandings (Casson 1989; Goitein and Friedman 2008). The travellers themselves are treated in Volume 21, Bhūgola.
    Company and state archives
    From the seventeenth century the records of the European companies and of Mughal and successor states make quantitative economic history possible for the first time (Chaudhuri 1978; Prakash 1998).

    Two kinds of evidence are conspicuously absent. No Indian merchant’s account books survive from before the early modern period, and no pre-modern Indian sea charts are known. Claims that rely on either should be treated with caution.

    1.6 How the History Has Been Written¶

    The economic history of South Asia has been written from several standpoints, and each has left its mark on the questions asked and on the claims that circulate today.

    Colonial administrators and orientalists. Officials of the East India Company and the Crown compiled gazetteers, revenue reports and statistical surveys that remain essential sources, but they often assumed that pre-colonial India had been static, its villages self-sufficient and its economy shaped by caste and custom rather than by markets. The rediscovery of the Arthaśāstra in 1905 and the publication of inscriptions and coins challenged this picture by revealing an ancient literature of revenue, trade and administration (Shamasastry 1915).

    Economic nationalism. From the 1870s Indian writers turned the colonial record against itself. Dadabhai Naoroji’s argument that Britain drained wealth from India, and R. C. Dutt’s history of the economy under Company and Crown, made economic history a central part of the nationalist case (Naoroji 1901; Dutt 1902; Chandra 1966). In the same period Radhakumud Mookerji’s Indian Shipping (1912) and R. C. Majumdar’s Corporate Life in Ancient India (1918) set out to show that India had possessed a great maritime tradition and sophisticated commercial institutions (Mookerji 1912; Majumdar 1918). These works were pioneering, and much of their evidence is still used; but they were also written to restore national pride, and some of their claims, for instance about ship lore or the “Greater India” of Southeast Asia, are more confident than the evidence allows.

    Marxist and social history. From the 1950s D. D. Kosambi, R. S. Sharma and Irfan Habib placed the economy at the centre of Indian history, asking about modes of production, surplus extraction, the condition of peasants and artisans, and the reasons why capitalism did not develop in pre-colonial India (Kosambi 1956; Sharma 1983; Habib 1969). Their questions, and the debates they provoked, shaped the field for half a century.

    Trade, merchants and the Indian Ocean. Auguste Toussaint’s History of the Indian Ocean (French 1961, English 1966) was an early attempt to write the ocean’s history as a whole (Toussaint 1966). From the 1960s historians such as Ashin Das Gupta, K. N. Chaudhuri, Om Prakash, Sanjay Subrahmanyam and Sinnappah Arasaratnam rebuilt the history of Indian merchants and of the Indian Ocean from Asian and European company archives, showing that Asian merchants remained active and powerful long after 1500 (Das Gupta 1979; Chaudhuri 1985; Prakash 1998; Subrahmanyam 1990; Arasaratnam 1986). For the ancient period, Himanshu Prabha Ray, Ranabir Chakravarti and others combined archaeology, inscriptions and texts to write a history of trade not centred on states (Ray 2003; Chakravarti 2002).

    Global and comparative history. Since the 1990s the “great divergence” debate has asked why Western Europe, rather than South or East Asia, industrialised first. Historians such as Prasannan Parthasarathi and Tirthankar Roy have offered sharply different answers, and economic historians have produced new estimates of output and income (Parthasarathi 2011; Roy 2012; Broadberry et al. 2015).

    This volume draws on all of these traditions. It also tries to avoid a temptation common to several of them: to read the past mainly as evidence for or against a present-day argument about India’s place in the world.

    1.7 Recurring Debates¶

    Several debates run through the volume. The largest concerns the size of the pre-modern Indian economy: Angus Maddison’s long-run estimates, which put India’s share of world output at roughly a quarter around 1500–1700, are examined with their caveats in the debate box of Section 18.5.6. Other debates concern the interpretation of particular evidence: whether Lothal had a dock (Chapter 11); how far the ship lore of the Yuktikalpataru reflects real practice (Chapter 13); whether the early medieval period saw urban and commercial decline (Chapters 3, 4 and 5); how the Indic culture of Southeast Asia should be understood, as “Indianisation” or as local adoption and adaptation (Chapter 16); what the Chola expeditions of the eleventh century were for (Chapter 12); and how to measure the economic effects of colonial rule (Chapter 19). In each case the volume tries to set out the evidence and the main positions rather than to settle the question.

    1.8 Plan of the Volume¶

    The volume has twenty-one chapters. They are not divided into formal parts, but they fall into four groups, and the chapters within each group are best read in order.

    Ideas and institutions (Chapters 2–9). Chapter 2 examines the Arthaśāstra and the wider tradition of economic thought, including the Tamil Tirukkuṟaḷ, Indo-Persian writing on revenue, and Buddhist and Jain ethics of wealth; the Arthaśāstra’s theory of the state is treated in Volume 18, Rājanīti and Dharmaśāstra. Chapter 3 turns to the agrarian base: the state’s claim on the harvest across the periods of the volume, the early medieval land grants and the debate over “Indian feudalism”. Chapter 4 turns to guilds and merchant associations, Chapter 5 to coinage and money, Chapter 6 to credit, banking and commercial law, Chapter 7 to markets, prices, weights and their regulation, and Chapter 8 to the organisation of craft production (techniques are treated in Volume 17, Śilpa–Kāru, and Volume 14, Rasāyana). Chapter 9 examines religious institutions — Buddhist and Jain monasteries and temples, the south Indian temple, pilgrimage centres and fairs, Islamic endowments and Sikh gurdwārās — as holders, redistributors and investors of wealth.

    Overland trade (Chapter 10). The routes within the subcontinent and the connections across Central Asia, including the Silk Roads and the Indian merchant diaspora in Iran, Central Asia and Russia, and the routes across the Himalaya and the north-east.

    The sea (Chapters 11–17). Seaborne trade from the Harappans to the Indo-Roman trade; the Indian Ocean world from c. 600 to 1500; ships and shipbuilding; the monsoon and navigation; port cities, port administration and maritime law; the connections with Southeast Asia; and the fishing and seafaring communities of the coasts.

    Transformation and legacy (Chapters 18–21). The early modern economy from the Sultanate to the late eighteenth century, including the arrival of the European companies and the trade in slaves; the colonial economy and the decline of Indian shipping; Indian economic thought from Ranade to the Second Five-Year Plan; and the legacies of this history, with a caution about its uses and misuses today.

    1.9 A Note on Terms and Conventions¶

    Sanskrit, Pali and Prakrit words are given in the International Alphabet of Sanskrit Transliteration (IAST); Tamil words in a standard transliteration; Arabic and Persian words in a simplified scholarly form. Familiar modern place names (Surat, Calicut, Kolkata) are given in their ordinary English spelling. Dates are given as BCE and CE. References to the Arthaśāstra (AŚ) follow the book, chapter and sentence numbering of Kangle’s edition, as used in Olivelle’s translation. A glossary of principal terms is at the end of the volume.

    A number of English words record the history told here. Calico takes its name from Calicut, chintz from the Hindi chīṇṭ, catamaran from the Tamil kaṭṭumaram (“tied logs”), and pepper, through Greek and Latin, ultimately from an Indian word related to Sanskrit pippalī (long pepper). They are reminders that South Asian goods travelled further than South Asian names for them.

    Further Reading¶

    For general frameworks see Thapar (2002) and Kulke and Rothermund (2004); for economic history over the long run, Roy (2012); for early trade, Chakravarti (2002) and Ray (2003); for the Indian Ocean, Chaudhuri (1985), McPherson (1993) and Pearson (2003); for the early modern and colonial periods, the two volumes of the Cambridge Economic History of India (Raychaudhuri and Habib 1982; Kumar 1983).
