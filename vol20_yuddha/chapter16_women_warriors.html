    Chapter 16: Women and War: Vīrāṅganās, Rulers and Victims¶

    16.1 Introduction¶

    War in South Asia, as elsewhere, was overwhelmingly the business of men, and the texts that describe it were written by men. Yet women appear in the history of Indian warfare in many roles: as rulers and regents who commanded armies, as defenders of besieged forts, as guards and soldiers, as patrons of warriors, as the mothers and widows celebrated in war poetry, and, very often, as the victims of war — captured, enslaved, or driven to death to avoid capture. The figure of the vīrāṅganā, the “heroic woman” who takes up arms, has a special place in Indian popular culture, from the folk theatre of north India to modern cinema (Hansen 1988).

    This chapter surveys the evidence. It tries to avoid two distortions. One presents Indian women as having been uniformly passive and secluded until modern times; the other presents a long tradition of women warriors as though women’s military participation had been normal. The truth lies between: women who fought and commanded were exceptional, usually members of ruling families acting in the absence of men, and their exceptional status is precisely why they were remembered.

    16.2 Early Evidence¶

    16.2.1 The Vedic hymns¶

    The Ṛgveda contains a few references that later writers have read as evidence of women fighters. One hymn (RV 1.116.15) praises the Aśvins for giving Viśpalā an iron leg when her leg was cut off “in the contest”, so that she could run again. Viśpalā is usually taken to be a woman warrior; some scholars, however, interpret her as a racing mare, and the passage is too brief to settle the question (Jamison and Brereton 2014). Another hymn (RV 10.102) describes Mudgalānī, the wife of Mudgala, driving her husband’s chariot to victory in a race or contest. These passages show that women could be imagined in martial roles, but they do not establish a tradition of women’s military service.

    16.2.2 Women guards¶

    Greek and Sanskrit sources agree on one practice: the king was guarded by armed women. Megasthenes reported that the Mauryan king was protected in his palace by women bodyguards, and the Arthaśāstra prescribes that the king, on rising, should be surrounded by women armed with bows (AŚ 1.21) (Kangle 1960–1965; Olivelle 2013). Women guards of the inner palace are mentioned in Sanskrit drama and in accounts of later courts, including those of Vijayanagara and the Mughals; the Mughal harem was guarded by armed women, some of them from Central Asia and Abyssinia.

    16.2.3 The epics and the goddess¶

    The epics have few women fighters. Draupadī, wife of the Pāṇḍavas, is a fierce advocate of war after her humiliation at the dice game, but she does not fight; Kaikeyī in the Rāmāyaṇa is said to have driven her husband’s chariot in a battle of the gods and demons; Citrāṅgadā, princess of Maṇipūra and wife of Arjuna, is a ruler’s heir. Later traditions, such as Satyabhāmā’s part in Kṛṣṇa’s battle with the demon Naraka, told in the Harivaṃśa and the Purāṇas, expand women’s martial roles. The most powerful image of female martial power is divine: the goddess Durgā, who in the Devīmāhātmya (c. fifth to sixth century) receives the weapons of all the gods and slays the buffalo-demon Mahiṣa and his armies. The warrior goddess was the patron of many royal lineages, but her worship did not imply that human women should fight.

    16.2.4 Tamil war poetry¶

    The Tamil Caṅkam poems of war give a particularly vivid picture of women’s relation to war — not as fighters but as mothers, wives and widows of warriors. In one famous poem of the Puṟanāṉūṟu, a mother who hears that her son fled from battle goes to the battlefield intending to punish herself if the report is true, finds him dead with his wounds in front, and rejoices more than on the day she bore him (Hart and Heifetz 1999). Poems by women poets such as Auvaiyār, who was also an envoy between kings, express the heroic ethic, and others lament its cost. These poems show how women participated in, and were bound by, a culture that exalted death in battle.

    16.3 Medieval Rulers and Commanders¶

    16.3.1 Raziya¶

    Raziya (r. 1236–1240), daughter of Iltutmish, is the only woman to have ruled the Delhi Sultanate. Her father designated her as his heir, and after a struggle she took the throne. She appeared in public, rode an elephant and led her armies in person; the historian Minhāj al-Sirāj, who served her, praised her as a just and capable ruler who lacked only the qualification of being a man. She was overthrown by a faction of Turkic nobles and killed in 1240 (Jackson 1999).

    16.3.2 Rudramā Devī¶

    Rudramā Devī ruled the Kākatīya kingdom of Warangal in the Telugu country from about 1262 to 1289. She took the masculine title Rudradeva Mahārāja in her inscriptions, faced revolts by subordinate chiefs and attacks by the Yādavas of Devagiri, and maintained her rule through a network of military commanders loyal to her. The Venetian traveller Marco Polo, who visited the coast of her kingdom, described it as ruled by a wise queen. Cynthia Talbot’s study of medieval Andhra places her reign in the context of the Kākatīya state’s military and social organisation (Talbot 2001).

    16.3.3 Durgāvatī¶

    Rāṇī Durgāvatī of the Gond kingdom of Garha-Katanga in central India was born into the Chandela dynasty and married into the Gond royal house; after her husband’s death she ruled as regent for her young son. In 1564 the Mughal governor Āṣaf Khān invaded her kingdom. Durgāvatī led her forces in person, mounted on an elephant; after initial resistance her army was defeated near Narhi, and, wounded, she killed herself rather than be captured. Abū’l-Faẓl, Akbar’s historian, recorded her courage. She became a heroine of the Gond people and of Indian nationalism.

    16.3.4 Chand Bībī¶

    Chand Bībī (d. 1600), a princess of Ahmadnagar who married into the ruling family of Bijapur, acted as regent in both sultanates. In 1595–96, as regent of Ahmadnagar, she organised the defence of the city against a Mughal army led by Akbar’s son Murād. When the besiegers blew a breach in the wall with a mine, she is said to have directed its repair under fire; the Mughals withdrew after a negotiated settlement. She was killed in 1600, during factional conflict in the city, shortly before Ahmadnagar fell to the Mughals (Eaton 2005). Her career shows that the figure of the woman ruler-commander was not confined to any one religious tradition.

    16.3.5 Abbakka of Ullal¶

    Abbakka Chowta, queen of the small Tulu principality of Ullal near Mangalore in the mid-sixteenth century, resisted Portuguese attempts to control the trade of the coast, in alliance with the Zamorin of Calicut and others. Portuguese sources record expeditions against Ullal; much of what is now told of her, including her death in captivity, comes from later tradition, which has made her one of the best-known resisters of European expansion in coastal Karnataka.

    16.4 Cennabhairādevī of Gersoppa¶

    Cennabhairādevī, queen of the small Jain-ruled principality of Gersoppa (Nagire) in the hinterland of the Kanara coast, is said to have ruled for several decades in the second half of the sixteenth century. Her territories produced much of the pepper exported from the Kanara ports, and later writers report that the Portuguese called her the “pepper queen”. She traded with the Portuguese but resisted their attempts to control the pepper trade. Her reign has not received a critical study in English, and the details commonly given, including the length of her rule and her conflicts with the Portuguese, rest on regional histories and tradition that this volume has not been able to check against the Portuguese records or the Kannada inscriptions; they are given here with that caution.

    16.5 Women at the Mughal Court¶

    The Mughal court offers several examples of women’s involvement in war and politics. The imperial harem was guarded by armed women, known in Persian sources as urdubegī, many of them from Central Asia or Africa, who were trained in the use of the bow and other weapons. Women of the imperial family were active in politics: Nūr Jahān, the wife of Jahāngīr, issued orders and coins in her own name and was a skilled hunter, reported to have shot tigers from an elephant. In 1626, when the general Mahābat Khān seized Jahāngīr in a coup at a crossing of the Jhelum, Nūr Jahān led an attempt to rescue him, directing the attack from an elephant; it failed, but she later outmanoeuvred Mahābat Khān and secured the emperor’s release (Richards 1993). Women of noble families managed estates and sometimes defended them, and the widows of commanders sometimes kept their husbands’ troops together.

    16.6 The Seventeenth and Eighteenth Centuries¶

    16.6.1 Tārābāī¶

    Tārābāī (1675–1761), widow of the Maratha king Rājārām, led Maratha resistance against Aurangzeb after her husband’s death in 1700. As regent for her young son, she directed the war against the Mughals, appointed commanders and kept the Maratha state alive through the hardest years of the Deccan wars (Gordon 1993). After Aurangzeb’s death, the release of her nephew Shāhū by the Mughals led to a civil war that she lost; she remained a powerful figure in Maratha politics for decades.

    16.6.2 Keladi Cennammā and Ahilyābāī Holkar¶

    Cennammā, queen of the Nāyaka kingdom of Keladi in Karnataka (r. 1672–1697), gave refuge to Rājārām when he fled south from the Mughals in 1690 and successfully resisted the Mughal forces sent against her. Ahilyābāī Holkar (r. 1767–1795), ruler of the Maratha state of Indore, is remembered mainly as a just administrator and a builder of temples and pilgrim facilities across India, but she also maintained the Holkar army and directed its affairs through her commander Tukojī Holkar.

    16.6.3 Velu Nācciyār and Kuyili¶

    Velu Nācciyār, queen of Sivaganga in Tamil Nadu, lost her husband and her kingdom in 1772 when the forces of the Nawab of Arcot and the East India Company attacked Sivaganga. According to the accounts now current, she took refuge with the poligar of Virupachi and, with the support of Haidar Ali of Mysore and of the Marutu brothers (Section 15.7), recovered her kingdom in 1780. Tamil tradition tells that her woman commander Kuyili set herself on fire and destroyed an enemy powder magazine; the story, as told today, rests on oral tradition and later writing. No critical study of the queen’s career is cited here, and the details should be taken as those of regional history and tradition. Both women are now honoured in Tamil Nadu as early resisters of colonial rule.

    16.7 Women Rulers in the South¶

    In south India, several women ruled as regents and directed military affairs. Rāṇī Maṅgammāḷ, regent of the Nāyaka kingdom of Madurai from 1689 to about 1704, conducted the kingdom’s relations with the Mughals, who were then advancing into the south, and with the neighbouring Tanjavur and Mysore kingdoms; she is remembered for her public works and for her diplomacy. The queens of the Keladi and other Nāyaka kingdoms, the queens of Kerala’s matrilineal ruling houses and the women rulers of small Maṟavar and poligar states all played military roles at times. Their histories, recorded partly in inscriptions and court chronicles and partly in local tradition, show that women’s rule was not exceptional in the south Indian political world.

    16.8 Sikh Women in Tradition¶

    Sikh tradition remembers women who took part in the wars of the Gurus’ time. The best known is Māī Bhāgo, who, according to tradition, led a band of Sikhs who had deserted Guru Gobind Singh back to his side and fought with them at the battle of Muktsar (Khidrana) in 1705. The Khalsa’s ideals of equality and of the duty to defend the faith gave women a place in Sikh martial memory, and today Sikh women perform gatka at festivals and in competitions (Dhavan 2011).

    16.9 Begum Samrū¶

    One of the most remarkable figures of eighteenth-century north India was Begum Samrū (c. 1750s–1836), the ruler of the small principality of Sardhana near Meerut. Born in obscure circumstances, she became the companion and later the wife of Walter Reinhardt, known as Sombre or Samrū, a European mercenary commander who had served several Indian rulers and received Sardhana as a jāgīr. After his death in 1778 she inherited command of his mercenary army of infantry and artillery, which she led in the service of the Mughal emperor Shāh ʿĀlam II and others; she is reported to have taken part in campaigns in person. She converted to Catholicism and built a large church at Sardhana. After the British conquest of the region in 1803 she made terms with the East India Company and kept her principality until her death. Her career illustrates the world of the eighteenth-century military entrepreneurs, in which a woman of humble origins could command an army (Roy 2011).

    16.10 The Nineteenth Century¶

    16.10.1 Cennammā of Kittūr¶

    Cennammā of Kittūr (1778–1829), widow of the ruler of the small state of Kittūr in north Karnataka, refused to accept the East India Company’s decision not to recognise her adopted son as heir. In October 1824 her forces resisted the Company’s attempt to take control; the collector of Dharwar, St John Thackeray, was killed. A larger force took Kittūr in December, and Cennammā died in captivity in 1829. She is commemorated in Karnataka as one of the earliest Indian rulers to fight the Company.

    16.10.2 The rising of 1857¶

    The rising of 1857 produced the most famous of India’s women warriors. Lakṣmībāī, Rāṇī of Jhānsī (d. 1858), was the widow of the ruler of Jhānsī, whose state the Company had annexed under the “doctrine of lapse” after it refused to recognise her adopted son. When the rising broke out she at first negotiated with the British, and then, accused of complicity in the killing of Europeans at Jhānsī, took command of its defence. Sir Hugh Rose besieged and stormed Jhānsī in March and April 1858; the Rāṇī escaped by night and joined the rebel forces under Tātyā Ṭope. She was killed in battle near Gwalior in June 1858. Her British opponents acknowledged her courage. Joyce Lebra-Chapman’s study separates the historical Rāṇī from the legend that grew around her in songs, novels and nationalist history (Lebra-Chapman 1986).

    Other women of 1857, at Jhānsī and Lucknow, are described in the next section. Kathryn Hansen has traced how the figure of the vīrāṅganā became part of north Indian popular theatre and print culture, admired but also contained as an exception to normal womanhood (Hansen 1988).

    16.11 The Women of Jhānsī and Lucknow¶

    The defence of Jhānsī in 1858 involved many women besides the Rāṇī. Contemporary British accounts and later Indian tradition mention women carrying ammunition, food and water to the defenders on the walls and working the guns. Among the Rāṇī’s companions, tradition remembers the attendants Sundar and Mundar and Jhalkārībāī, a woman of the Kolī community who is said to have disguised herself as the Rāṇī to cover her escape. At Lucknow, Begum Hazrat Mahal, who led the resistance in the name of her young son, issued proclamations answering the British, and continued to resist after the fall of the city; she eventually took refuge in Nepal, where she died in 1879. The British accounts of the fighting at Sikandar Bagh in November 1857 mention a woman sniper killed in a tree, whom later tradition identifies with Ūdā Devī, a Pāsī woman. Jhalkārībāī and Ūdā Devī have become heroines for Dalit communities in north India (Section 16.15). These women came from very different social backgrounds — royal, courtly and humble — and their memory has been taken up by different communities in modern India.

    16.12 The Begums of Bhopal¶

    The princely state of Bhopal in central India was ruled by women for most of the period from 1819 to 1926 — an unusual succession of four women rulers. Qudsia Begum governed as regent after her husband’s assassination in 1819; her daughter Sikandar Begum ruled from the 1840s to 1868, reformed the administration and the army, and supported the British during the rising of 1857, for which she was rewarded; Shāh Jahān Begum and Sultān Jahān Begum followed her. The Begums of Bhopal commanded the state’s forces in a formal sense, reviewed troops and, in Sikandar Begum’s case, were known for their skill in riding and their interest in military affairs. Their rule shows that the role of the woman ruler with military responsibilities continued into the colonial period, within the limits set by British paramountcy.

    16.13 Women as Victims of War: Jauhar and Captivity¶

    16.13.1 Jauhar¶

    The darkest chapter of women’s relation to war in India is the jauhar, the collective death by fire of the women of a besieged fort to avoid capture, usually followed by a last charge of the men. Contemporary or near-contemporary sources record jauhars at Chanderi in 1528, during Bābur’s siege, and at Chittor in 1535 and 1568; Amīr Khusrau’s account of ʿAlāʾ al-Dīn’s capture of Ranthambhor in 1301 describes the burning of the women of the fort, which suggests that similar events occurred earlier (Sreenivasan 2007).

    The best-known story of jauhar, that of Rāṇī Padminī of Chittor, whose beauty is said to have drawn ʿAlāʾ al-Dīn Khaljī to besiege the fort in 1303, comes from Malik Muḥammad Jāyasī’s Sufi poem Padmāvat, written in 1540, more than two centuries after the siege. Ramya Sreenivasan’s study shows how the story was retold and transformed by Rajput bards, colonial writers and nationalists, each giving it new meanings (Sreenivasan 2007). Padminī is a literary heroine whose historical existence cannot be established.

    The jauhar has been interpreted in opposite ways. Rajput tradition celebrates it as the supreme expression of honour and courage; feminist historians have pointed out that it was the product of a social order that valued women chiefly as bearers of family honour, and that the women’s “choice” was made under the most extreme pressure. Both perspectives recognise the reality of the fear of capture: women captured in war were enslaved, given to the victors or sold, by armies of every religion. A history of Indian warfare that celebrates heroism without acknowledging this suffering is incomplete.

    16.13.2 Captivity and enslavement¶

    Inscriptions and chronicles from many periods record the capture of women as spoils of war: Cōḻa and Cāḷukya inscriptions boast of seizing enemy queens, the chronicles of the Delhi Sultanate record the enslavement of captives in great numbers, and Rajput, Maratha and other armies took captives in their turn. The suffering of women in war was one of the realities that the rules of dharmayuddha (Chapter 12) addressed, at least in principle, by forbidding the harming of non-combatants.

    16.14 Women against Colonial Rule¶

    In the twentieth century women took part in armed resistance to colonial rule in several ways. In the hills of Manipur and the Naga country, Gaidinliu (1915–1993), a young Rongmei Naga woman, led a religious and political movement against British rule in the early 1930s; she was captured in 1932 and imprisoned until 1947; Jawaharlal Nehru, who met her in prison in 1937, called her “Rani”, the title by which she is remembered. In Bengal, women joined revolutionary societies: Pritilata Waddedar led an attack on a European club in Chittagong in 1932 and took her own life to avoid capture, and Bina Das fired at the Governor of Bengal at a university convocation in the same year. These women were few, but they broke with the assumption that political violence was a male domain, and they are remembered as heroines in Bengal and the north-east.

    The women’s regiment of the Indian National Army, named after the Rāṇī of Jhānsī, is discussed below (Section 16.17). Its existence shows how the memory of the vīrāṅganā was mobilised by nationalists in the struggle for independence.

    16.15 Representing the Vīrāṅganā¶

    The figure of the woman warrior has been represented in many ways in Indian culture. In north Indian folk theatre and popular print, as Kathryn Hansen has shown, the vīrāṅganā was a figure of admiration who took on male roles in a crisis — often after the death of her husband — and returned to female roles afterwards, so that her heroism confirmed rather than challenged the normal order (Hansen 1988). Nationalist writers made the Rāṇī of Jhānsī a symbol of the nation, as in Subhadra Kumari Chauhan’s Hindi poem “Jhānsī kī Rānī” (1930), one of the most widely memorised poems in Hindi. In recent decades, Dalit writers and activists have recovered figures such as Jhalkārībāī and Ūdā Devī as heroines of their own communities. Feminist historians have asked what these representations reveal about the constraints on women’s lives and about the uses of women’s images in politics. The study of the vīrāṅganā is thus as much a study of memory and representation as of military history.

    16.16 Women in Hero-Stones and Memorial Stones¶

    The memorial stones of medieval India give a glimpse of women’s relation to war at the local level. Most hero-stones commemorate men, but some commemorate women who died fighting, often in defence of cattle or village. Far more numerous are the memorial stones of women who died with their husbands, by self-immolation on the husband’s funeral pyre or otherwise; in Karnataka these are called mahāsatī stones, and they usually show a raised right arm with bangles, the sign of the married woman, sometimes alongside the hero’s image. In Rajasthan, satī memorials are found at many villages and forts. These stones were worshipped, and some satī sites became places of pilgrimage.

    The memorial stones show how the ideology of the heroic death extended to women, and they are evidence of the practice of widow immolation, which was more common in some regions, communities and periods than others. The practice was criticised by some Indian thinkers long before the nineteenth century, and it was prohibited in British India by Lord William Bentinck’s regulation of 1829, after a campaign in which the Bengali reformer Rammohun Roy played a prominent part. Its glorification in modern times, as in the controversy that followed the death of Roop Kanwar in Rajasthan in 1987, led to further legislation. A history of women and war in India must include these stones and the lives they commemorate, alongside the stories of queens and commanders.

    16.17 The Twentieth Century and After¶

    In the twentieth century, women entered military roles in new ways. In 1943 Subhas Chandra Bose’s Indian National Army, formed from Indian prisoners of war and civilians in Southeast Asia, created a women’s unit named the Rani of Jhansi Regiment, commanded by Lakshmi Swaminathan (later Sahgal) — a deliberate evocation of the vīrāṅganā tradition. After independence, women served in the Indian armed forces first as doctors and nurses; from the 1990s they were commissioned in other branches, from 2016 as fighter pilots, and following a Supreme Court judgment in 2020 they became eligible for permanent commissions in the army. Advocates of women’s military service have often invoked historical figures such as Lakṣmībāī; but the modern changes owe more to changing ideas of citizenship and equality than to any continuity with the past.

It is not a complete list, and for several figures the details rest partly on later tradition, as noted in the text. Read together, the entries bear out the argument with which the chapter began: almost every woman in it before the twentieth century held command as a widow, a regent or the heir of a ruling house, in a moment of crisis, and was remembered because her role was exceptional.
