    Chapter 20: Legacies: Revival, Myth and Memory¶

    20.1 Introduction¶

    The colonial conquest ended the independent military traditions of the subcontinent, and the armed forces that succeeded the colonial army have been described in Chapter 19 (Section 19.20). This final chapter follows what happened to the warrior’s arts outside the army. It falls into three parts. The first traces the revival of Indian martial arts and physical culture since the late nineteenth century, which turned arts tied to vanished military systems into sports, performances, health practices and symbols of identity, and the claims that have grown up around them. The second examines the question of Indian influence on the martial arts of other Asian countries, which has produced some of the most persistent myths in the field, above all the story of Bodhidharma and Shaolin kung fu. The third gathers the popular claims examined throughout the volume, points readers to the sources, and sets out the questions that remain open and the conclusions the volume has reached. Chess, the board game of the four-limbed army, is treated with its ancestor caturaṅga in Section 2.20, and the debate over an Indian strategic tradition at the end of Chapter 7.

    20.2 The Revival of Indian Martial Arts¶

    20.2.1 Physical culture and nationalism¶

    As Chapter 19 explained, the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries saw a movement of physical culture in India, inspired partly by the desire to answer colonial stereotypes of Indian weakness and partly by the international vogue for gymnastics and bodybuilding. Gymnasia (vyāyāmśālā, tālīm, akhāṛā) were founded in Maharashtra, Bengal, Punjab and elsewhere; wrestling, club-swinging, mallakhamb, stick-fighting and Indian forms of exercise were combined with European gymnastics and drill. Institutions such as the Hanuman Vyayam Prasarak Mandal at Amravati, founded in 1914, and the Baroda physical culture circle around D. C. Mujumdar, whose Encyclopedia of Indian Physical Culture appeared in 1950, documented and promoted Indian exercises and martial arts (Mujumdar 1950). Mark Singleton has shown how the same milieu contributed to the formation of modern postural yoga (Singleton 2010).

    20.2.2 From combat to sport and performance¶

    After independence the regional martial arts were reorganised as sports, with associations, federations, standardised rules and competitions; as performing arts, used in dance, theatre and film; and as heritage, promoted by state governments and tourism departments. Kaḷarippayaṟṟu associations were formed in Kerala from the late 1950s, silambam and gatka federations later; the Sports Authority of India, established in 1984, and the Khelo India programme, launched in 2018, have included indigenous martial arts such as gatka, kaḷarippayaṟṟu, thang-ta and mallakhamb in national competitions. Kaḷarippayaṟṟu has influenced experimental theatre and dance in India and abroad, and the action choreography of Indian cinema draws on kaḷarippayaṟṟu, silambam and gatka.

    This transformation has been both a rescue and a change. Arts that might have disappeared are now practised by many thousands of people, including women and members of communities once excluded from them. But sport and performance select and standardise: the healing knowledge of the kaḷari, the ritual of the akhāṛā, the lineage-specific forms of silambam, are hard to fit into competitive formats. Practitioners and scholars debate how to preserve the depth of the traditions while widening participation (Zarrilli 1998; Alter 1992).

    20.2.3 Wrestling and archery¶

    Traditional wrestling has had the most visible modern success, through the Olympic medals won by Indian freestyle wrestlers since 2008, many of them trained in akhāṛās (Chapter 14). Modern archery, though using modern equipment, has produced Indian champions, many of them from tribal communities with their own traditions of the bow (Chapter 4).

    20.3 Martial Arts and Yoga¶

    The revival also produced a common claim that yoga and the Indian martial arts were once a single discipline, or that yoga postures derive from combat stances. There are genuine connections. Both share a vocabulary of posture and breath; armed ascetics, including Nāth yogis and the nāgā orders, were soldiers as well as renouncers (Pinch 2006); kaḷarippayaṟṟu incorporates exercises that resemble yoga postures; and the physical culture movement of the early twentieth century helped shape both modern yoga and the revived martial arts (Singleton 2010). But the specific claims often made — that the “warrior” poses of modern yoga preserve ancient battle stances, or that classical yoga texts teach martial techniques — are not supported by the sources.

    20.4 Science and the Claims Made for Martial Arts¶

    Promoters of Indian martial arts often claim scientific support for their effectiveness or their health benefits: that marma and varmam points correspond to nerve centres, that traditional exercises produce exceptional fitness, that traditional diets are optimal, or that particular arts cure specific diseases. Some of these claims are plausible in general terms — regular, vigorous exercise is good for health, and traditional exercises such as the daṇḍ and baiṭhak are effective forms of strength training — but many are not supported by published research, and some are contradicted by it. Studies of martial arts training as exercise exist, but they do not validate the traditional theories of vital points or of the body’s “energy” channels.

    The responsible course is to distinguish three kinds of statement: historical statements about what traditions taught and practised, which can be checked against sources; general statements about the benefits of exercise, which are well supported; and specific medical or physiological claims, which need clinical evidence. Indian martial arts are valuable cultural traditions and forms of physical education whether or not their traditional theories are scientifically validated. Their value does not depend on exaggerated claims, and such claims, when they are shown to be false, can damage the credibility of the traditions themselves.

    20.5 Martial Arts in Cinema and Popular Culture¶

    Cinema has been a powerful medium for the representation of Indian martial traditions. The stunt films of the 1930s and 1940s, including those of the actress Fearless Nadia, popularised sword fights and acrobatics; the mythological films of all periods staged the battles of the epics; and historical films have dramatised the lives of Śivājī, the Rāṇī of Jhānsī, Pṛthvīrāja, Rāṇā Pratāp and many others. Malayalam cinema made the heroes of the Kerala ballads famous; Tamil cinema used silambam in fight scenes; and recent big-budget films have used kaḷarippayaṟṟu and other traditional arts in their action sequences. Television serials of the Rāmāyaṇa and Mahābhārata in the late 1980s reached hundreds of millions of viewers and shaped popular ideas of epic warfare.

    Cinema has also spread myths: films have presented Bodhidharma as the founder of Chinese martial arts, epic weapons as ancient technology, and historical battles with little regard for evidence. The historian’s task is not to condemn popular culture, which has its own purposes, but to make the evidence available so that viewers can distinguish history from legend.

    20.6 Heritage, Tourism and Documentation¶

    Indian martial arts are now promoted as cultural heritage by central and state governments, tourism departments and cultural organisations. Kaḷarippayaṟṟu demonstrations are a regular feature of tourism in Kerala; gatka and mallakhamb are performed at national events; the Hola Mohalla festival and the Mysore Dasara attract large audiences. Such promotion has brought support and recognition, but it can also encourage spectacle at the expense of depth, and it often repeats unverified claims of antiquity.

    Serious documentation of these arts — recording their techniques, terminology, lineages, rituals and oral traditions, and the life histories of their masters — has been undertaken by scholars such as Zarrilli and Alter, by cultural institutions and by practitioners themselves (Zarrilli 1998; Alter 1992). Much remains to be done. The palm-leaf manuscripts of kaḷari and varmam lineages, the oral epics of warrior heroes, the arms and armour in princely and private collections, and the memories of elderly practitioners are all valuable sources that are at risk of being lost. Their careful study, using the methods of history, ethnography and conservation, is one of the most important tasks for the future of the field.

    20.7 The Diaspora¶

    Indian martial arts have also travelled with the Indian diaspora. Silambam is practised by Tamil communities in Malaysia, Singapore and Sri Lanka; gatka by Sikh communities in Britain, North America and elsewhere, where it is performed at religious festivals and processions; kaḷarippayaṟṟu is taught in schools in Europe, North America and East Asia, often by teachers trained in Kerala; and Punjabi wrestling and kabaḍḍī tournaments are held in Britain and Canada. In the diaspora these arts often serve as markers of community identity and as ways of transmitting culture to new generations, as well as forms of exercise and self-defence (Green and Svinth 2010).

    20.8 Bodhidharma, Shaolin and Kung Fu¶

    20.8.1 The legend¶

    A widely repeated story holds that the Indian Buddhist monk Bodhidharma, the legendary founder of Chan (Zen) Buddhism in China, came to the Shaolin monastery in Henan in the early sixth century, found the monks too weak to sit long in meditation, and taught them exercises from which Shaolin kung fu, and through it the martial arts of China, Korea and Japan, developed. In Indian versions of the story Bodhidharma is a Pallava prince from Kāñcī who was trained in kaḷarippayaṟṟu or another Indian martial art, so that Indian martial arts become the source of all East Asian ones. The story has been popularised in films and books in India and elsewhere.

    20.8.2 The evidence¶

    Historical research has shown that the story has no basis in the early sources. The earliest Chinese references to Bodhidharma, from the sixth and seventh centuries, describe him as a foreign meditation master — a brahmin from south India in one account, a monk from Persia in another — and say nothing of martial arts, or indeed of Shaolin (Broughton 1999). His association with Shaolin developed later in Chan hagiography. Meir Shahar’s study of the Shaolin monastery shows that Shaolin monks are first recorded as fighters in the early seventh century, when they helped the future Tang emperor Li Shimin, and that the monastery’s famous martial arts — first staff-fighting and later bare-handed styles — developed mainly in the Ming and Qing periods (fourteenth to nineteenth centuries), drawing on Chinese military practice, Daoist gymnastics (daoyin) and Chinese medicine (Shahar 2008).

    The attribution of martial arts to Bodhidharma first appears in the Yijin jing (“Sinew-Changing Classic”), a manual of exercises composed in the early seventeenth century (around 1624), whose forged prefaces, attributed to famous generals of the Tang and Song periods, claim that Bodhidharma left the text at Shaolin. Shahar shows that its author was a Daoist and that its exercises derive from the Chinese tradition. The legend became widespread in the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries through popular fiction and martial arts manuals (Shahar 2008).

    The Bodhidharma story is therefore a legend, not history. This does not mean that India had no influence on Chinese culture — the transmission of Buddhism from India to China was one of the great cultural exchanges of history — but there is no evidence that Chinese martial arts derive from Indian ones.

    20.9 India and Southeast Asia¶

    Claims of Indian influence on the martial arts of Southeast Asia are more plausible in general terms, because Indian cultural influence on the region is well documented. The “Indianised” kingdoms of the first and early second millennia CE — Funan, Champa, Angkor, Śrīvijaya, Majapahit and others — adopted Sanskrit as a language of court and religion, Indian political ideas, the epics (the Rāmāyaṇa became the Thai Ramakien, the Khmer Reamker and the Javanese Kakawin Rāmāyaṇa), and the war elephant (Trautmann 2015). Sanskrit military terms entered Southeast Asian languages, and the Nītisāra of Kāmandaka was adapted in Old Javanese.

    The martial arts of the region — Thai muay and krabi krabong, Malay and Indonesian silat, Filipino arnis, Khmer bokator — are, however, local developments with their own histories, documented mostly from recent centuries. Claims that they descend directly from kaḷarippayaṟṟu or silambam rest on general resemblances, which can be explained by common human solutions to the problems of fighting, and on a few shared terms, not on historical evidence of transmission. The clearest case of Indian martial arts in Southeast Asia is modern: silambam was carried to Malaya by Tamil migrants in the nineteenth and twentieth centuries and is practised there today (Green and Svinth 2010).

    20.11 Studying Indian Military History: Sources and Resources¶

    Readers who wish to pursue the subjects of this volume further will find a wide range of sources and resources.

    Texts in translation.

    The Arthaśāstra is available in the translations of Kangle, Rangarajan and Olivelle (Kangle 1960–1965; Rangarajan 1992; Olivelle 2013); the Mahābhārata in the complete translation of K. M. Ganguli and the partial critical translations of the Chicago project (Ganguli 1883–1896; Buitenen 1973–1978; Fitzgerald 2004); the Mānava Dharmaśāstra in Olivelle’s edition (Olivelle 2005); the Agni Purāṇa in Mitra’s Sanskrit edition and Gangadharan’s translation (Mitra 1870–1879; Gangadharan 1984–1987); the Mahāvaṃsa in Geiger’s translation (Geiger 1912); the Tamil war poems in the translation of Hart and Heifetz (Hart and Heifetz 1999); Bābur’s memoirs in Thackston’s translation (Thackston 1996); and the Āʾīn-i Akbarī in Blochmann’s (Blochmann 1873).

    Archives.

    The archives of the Indian states and of the colonial government are vast. The National Archives of India in New Delhi, the state archives — among them the Maharashtra State Archives at Pune, which hold the records of the Peshwas’ government, and the Tamil Nadu Archives at Chennai — and the India Office Records at the British Library in London hold military records, correspondence and reports, much of which has been little studied. Persian, Marathi, Rajasthani and other documentary collections offer further material.

    Museums and monuments.

    The arms and armour collections mentioned in Chapter 5, the regimental museums of the Indian Army, the forts and battlefields protected by the Archaeological Survey of India and the state archaeology departments, and the many hero-stones preserved in museums and villages are all primary sources for the study of Indian warfare.

    Modern scholarship.

    The bibliography of this volume lists the principal modern studies. Among them, the works of Chakravarti and Sarva Daman Singh on early India, Olivelle and McClish on the Arthaśāstra, Upinder Singh on political violence in ancient India, Jackson on the Delhi Sultanate, Gommans and Kolff’s reader on the period 1000–1800, Gommans and Irvine on the Mughals, Gordon on the Marathas, Kolff on the military labour market, Trautmann on elephants, Roy on warfare and ethics, Alavi, Peers, Streets and Omissi on the colonial armies, and Zarrilli and Alter on martial arts provide a foundation for further study.

    20.12 Open Questions¶

    Many questions in the history of Indian warfare remain open. How were armies actually recruited, paid and supplied in the early historic and early medieval periods, beyond the prescriptions of the texts? When and how did the Indian traditions of crucible steel develop? What was the real scale of the Cōḻa overseas expeditions? How did the military labour market of the Ganges valley operate before the fifteenth century? How did forest and hill peoples fight, and how did their military traditions interact with those of the states around them? What can the thousands of unpublished hero-stones and inscriptions tell us about local warfare? How did women experience war, beyond the few queens whose names are recorded? And how much of the technical knowledge of the martial arts is genuinely old, and how much is the product of the modern revival? These questions can be answered only by patient research in texts, inscriptions, archives and the field. The traditions described in this volume deserve that research.

    20.13 Conclusion: Reading the Military Traditions of India¶

    This volume has surveyed more than three thousand years of warfare and martial culture in South Asia, from the walled towns of the Indus civilisation to the sepoy armies and the modern revival of the martial arts. A few conclusions may be drawn.

    First, Indian military history is rich and varied, and much of it is well documented. It deserves to be studied with the same critical care as the military history of other civilisations, using inscriptions, chronicles, archaeology and the comparative perspective of historians such as Gommans, Kolff, Trautmann and Roy.

    Second, the Indian textual tradition of war is impressive in its range: the Arthaśāstra’s realism, the Mahābhārata’s moral depth, the dharmaśāstras’ rules of war, the technical literatures on elephants, horses and arms, and the Persian, Tamil, Rajasthani, Marathi and Assamese writings on war. But these texts must be dated and read for what they are. The idea of a complete ancient “science of war” preserved from Vedic times is a modern construction, and claims of ancient super-weapons are myths.

    Third, Indian warfare was shaped by exchange and adaptation. The chariot, the war elephant, the mounted archer, gunpowder and the drilled infantry of the eighteenth century each transformed it, and Indian rulers and soldiers adopted new methods quickly. Rulers of every religion and region fought each other, recruited across communities and borrowed from their enemies.

    Fourth, the ethics of war in Indian traditions combined a real concern for restraint with a frank acceptance of deception and force, and practice often fell short of the ideals. The most valuable part of this inheritance may be its honesty about the costs of war.

    Finally, the martial arts that survive today are living traditions, transformed by colonialism and revival, which carry real knowledge of the body and of combat, and real cultural meaning. They are best served not by exaggerated claims but by careful documentation, critical history and safe practice.
