    Chapter 3: The Texts of War: Arthaśāstra, Epic, Nīti and Dhanurveda¶

    3.1 Introduction¶

    Readers who approach the subject through popular books often meet the statement that ancient India possessed a complete written “science of war”, the dhanurveda, handed down from the Vedic sages. The reality is more interesting and more complicated. Indian literature contains a great deal about war, but it is scattered across many genres — statecraft (arthaśāstra and nītiśāstra), law (dharmaśāstra), epic, Purāṇa, court poetry, technical treatises on elephants, horses and machines, and, from the thirteenth century, Persian manuals and chronicles. The texts that bear the name dhanurveda are late arrivals in this literature, and most of them are compilations whose dates are uncertain.

    This chapter introduces the principal texts, with the best available estimates of their dates. Its purpose is to give readers a map of the sources used in the rest of the book and a sense of what kind of evidence each provides. Table 3.2, near the end of the chapter, summarises the discussion.

    3.2 The Arthaśāstra¶

    3.2.1 Date and authorship¶

    The Arthaśāstra, “the science of material gain” or statecraft, is a prose treatise in fifteen books. It names its author as Kauṭilya (or Kauṭalya), and tradition identifies him with Cāṇakya (also called Viṣṇugupta), the minister who helped Candragupta Maurya to power around 321 BCE. Modern scholarship, beginning with Thomas Trautmann’s statistical study of its vocabulary and style, has shown that the text is not the work of a single author of the Mauryan period (Trautmann 1971). It contains layers of different date. Olivelle, its latest editor and translator, argues that an original treatise was composed between about 50 BCE and 125 CE and revised into its present form between about 175 and 300 CE (Olivelle 2013). This volume therefore dates the Arthaśāstra c. 50 BCE–300 CE, and treats the attribution to a minister of Candragupta as traditional. Hartmut Scharfe’s investigations into the text’s political vocabulary support a similar view of its composite character (Scharfe 1993). Mark McClish has since argued that the text as we have it is the product of a redaction that reorganised an earlier treatise on government and brought it into line with brahmanical norms of law and kingship; on this reading the Arthaśāstra’s accommodation of dharma, discussed in Chapters 7 and 12, belongs largely to its later layer (McClish 2019).

    The text was lost to modern scholarship until a manuscript was discovered in Mysore and published by R. Shamasastry, who translated it in 1915 (Shamasastry 1915). R. P. Kangle’s critical edition, translation and study (1960–1965) remains fundamental (Kangle 1960–1965); there are later translations by L. N. Rangarajan and Patrick Olivelle, and a selection by Mark McClish and Olivelle (Rangarajan 1992; Olivelle 2013; McClish and Olivelle 2012).

    3.2.2 Military content¶

    The Arthaśāstra is not primarily a military treatise; most of it concerns administration, revenue, law and diplomacy. But its outlook is that of a ruler who expects to fight, and it contains more systematic military material than any other early Sanskrit work:

    • Book 2 (on the activities of superintendents) describes the construction of forts (2.3–4), the armoury and its superintendent (2.18), and the superintendents of horses (2.30), elephants (2.31–32), and chariots, infantry and the army commander (2.33). It specifies the training, feeding and medical care of horses and elephants, the classes of weapons and armour to be stored, and the qualities of a commander.

    • Book 7 sets out the “six measures” of foreign policy (ṣāḍguṇya): peace, war, staying quiet, marching, seeking shelter and the dual policy. It also defines three kinds of war (AŚ 7.6): open war (prakāśayuddha), concealed war (kūṭayuddha), involving surprise attacks and deception in the field, and “silent war” (tūṣṇīṃyuddha), the use of secret agents to kill or subvert enemy leaders.

    • Book 9 concerns preparations for a campaign: assessment of one’s own strength and the enemy’s, the right season for marching, and the six kinds of troops available, from hereditary and hired troops to forest peoples, whose relative reliability it discusses (Section 7.5).

    • Book 10 deals with war itself: the laying out of the camp, the march, the protection of the army, battlefields suitable for each arm, the functions of each arm, and the battle arrays (vyūha) (Chapter 7).

    • Books 12–14 deal with the weak king facing a stronger enemy, with the capture of forts by siege and subversion, and with secret practices, including poisons and magical recipes of doubtful efficacy.

    The tone throughout is pragmatic. The Arthaśāstra advises the king to fight only when he can win, to prefer diplomacy and subversion to battle when they are cheaper, and to use deception freely. It is the most important single source for the institutions of war in early India, but it is a normative manual. It tells us what a learned theorist thought a king should do; it does not tell us that any particular king did it.

    3.3 The Epics¶

    3.3.1 The Mahābhārata¶

    The Mahābhārata, in its critical edition some 75,000 verses (about 100,000 in the traditional reckoning), grew over many centuries, roughly 400 BCE to 400 CE (Brockington 1998). Its central narrative is a war between two sets of cousins, the Pāṇḍavas and the Kauravas, culminating in an eighteen-day battle at Kurukṣetra. Several of its books are therefore military in content:

    • the Ādiparvan describes the education of the princes by Droṇa and the tournament in which they display their skills;

    • the Udyogaparvan describes the diplomacy that preceded the war, the embassies, the assembling of allies and the mustering of armies;

    • the battle books (Bhīṣma-, Droṇa-, Karṇa- and Śalyaparvan) describe the fighting day by day, with named arrays (vyūha), single combats, the use of divine weapons and the deaths of the great heroes;

    • the Sauptikaparvan describes the night massacre of the sleeping Pāṇḍava camp by Aśvatthāman;

    • the Śāntiparvan, after the war, contains Bhīṣma’s long instruction to Yudhiṣṭhira on the duties of kings (rājadharma), including rules for the conduct of war, and on conduct in emergencies (āpaddharma) (Fitzgerald 2004).

    The epic’s battle descriptions are poetry, governed by heroic conventions: they focus on duels between champions, exaggerate numbers, and mix the plausible with the supernatural. Their value lies in the vocabulary and ideals of war they preserve, and in the moral arguments about war that run through the whole poem (Chapter 12). Alf Hiltebeitel’s study of the battle books shows how they are also shaped by ritual and mythic patterns (Hiltebeitel 1976).

    3.3.2 The Rāmāyaṇa¶

    The Rāmāyaṇa of Vālmīki tells of Rāma’s war against Rāvaṇa, king of Laṅkā, in its sixth book, the Yuddhakāṇḍa. It describes the building of a causeway across the sea, the siege of Laṅkā, a city of walls, moats and gates, and a series of battles between Rāma’s army of monkeys and bears and the demons of Laṅkā. Its military details are fewer and more fantastic than those of the Mahābhārata, but the poem’s account of Rāma’s training by the sage Viśvāmitra, who gives him divine weapons, became a model for later descriptions of royal education.

    3.4 Buddhist and Jain Literature¶

    Buddhist and Jain texts are not usually thought of as sources for military history, but they contain much that bears on it. The Pali canon, compiled over several centuries and written down in Sri Lanka by tradition in the first century BCE, describes the kingdoms and republics of the Buddha’s time and their wars. In the Mahāparinibbāna Sutta, King Ajātasattu of Magadha sends his minister to ask the Buddha whether he can conquer the Vajjian confederacy; the Buddha replies indirectly that the Vajjis will not decline as long as they meet often in assembly, act in concord, respect their elders, their women and their shrines, and follow their ancient customs. The passage is a reflection on the strength of republican institutions in war, and Magadha’s later conquest of the Vajjis is attributed in the tradition to the use of a minister to sow dissension among them.

    The Jātaka stories, which tell of the Buddha’s previous lives, contain many tales of kings, armies, sieges and archers, and they mention military training at Takkasilā (Taxila), the celebrated centre of learning in the north-west, where princes are said to have gone to learn the arts, including archery. These stories are folk tales in Buddhist dress and cannot be dated precisely, but they reflect the ideas of their time about kingship and war. Popular claims that Taxila was “the world’s first university” or a “military academy” founded in the seventh century BCE go far beyond this evidence; Taxila was a city where individual teachers taught pupils, not an institution of the later monastic kind.

    Jain canonical texts, compiled and edited over many centuries, describe the wars of the Magadhan king Kūṇika (Ajātasattu) and, as Chapter 2 noted, credit him with machines of war. Jain literature also developed a distinctive treatment of war in the story of Bharata and Bāhubali, the two sons of the first Jina, Ṛṣabha: when their armies meet, the brothers agree to fight a series of single combats rather than sacrifice their soldiers, and Bāhubali, on the point of victory, renounces the world.

    3.5 Court Poetry and Historical Writing¶

    Sanskrit court poetry (kāvya) often describes war, especially in the conventional “conquest of the quarters” (digvijaya), in which the hero marches around India subduing the kings of each region. Kālidāsa’s Raghuvaṃśa (c. fourth to fifth century) describes such a campaign by King Raghu in its fourth canto. Bāṇa’s Harṣacarita (seventh century) describes the mustering and march of Harṣa’s army in vivid detail, with its elephants, horses, camels, baggage, camp followers and the damage it did to the fields. Bilhaṇa’s Vikramāṅkadevacarita (late eleventh century) celebrates the wars of the Cāḷukya king Vikramāditya VI. These works are panegyrics, but their authors were courtiers who had seen armies and campaigns, and their descriptions contain much realistic detail.

    The most important work of Sanskrit historical writing, Kalhaṇa’s Rājataraṅgiṇī (“River of Kings”), a verse history of Kashmir completed in 1148–49, is unusual in its critical attitude to its sources and its frankness about the failings of kings. Its later books describe in detail the civil wars of Kashmir in the eleventh and twelfth centuries: the struggles of kings with the landholding ḍāmaras, the sieges of forts, the use of mercenaries, the plundering of temples, the role of women in politics and the sufferings of the people. It is one of the richest sources for medieval warfare anywhere in India.

    3.6 Dharmaśāstra and Nīti¶

    3.6.1 Manu and the legal tradition¶

    The seventh chapter of the Mānava Dharmaśāstra (Manusmṛti, c. 2nd century CE) sets out the duties of a king. It covers the choice of a capital and the kinds of fort (MDh 7.70), the six measures of foreign policy, the four means (upāya) of conciliation, gifts, dissension and force, the conduct of a campaign, and the rules of fair fighting (MDh 7.90–93), which are discussed in Chapter 12 (Olivelle 2005). Later legal texts, such as the Yājñavalkya Smṛti, repeat and adapt these rules. Their interest is less in military technique than in the legitimacy of war and the obligations of the warrior.

    3.6.2 Kāmandaka’s Nītisāra¶

    The Nītisāra (“Essence of Policy”) of Kāmandaka is a verse treatise on statecraft that acknowledges its debt to the Arthaśāstra and condenses much of its political doctrine. Its date is uncertain; most estimates place it between the fifth and eighth centuries CE. It treats the circle of states, the six measures, the qualities of ministers and envoys, espionage, the march and battle arrays. It was widely read, and it was translated into Old Javanese — a sign of the reach of Indian political literature in Southeast Asia.

    3.6.3 The Tamil tradition¶

    In Tamil, the Caṅkam war poems (Chapter 2) preserve a non-Sanskrit vocabulary and ethic of war, and the Tirukkuṟaḷ treats kingship and the army systematically (Section 3.7).

    3.7 The Tamil Kuṟaḷ on the Army¶

    The Tirukkuṟaḷ, a Tamil collection of 1,330 couplets probably composed around the fifth century CE, deserves a fuller word, because it is one of the few Indian texts outside Sanskrit to treat military matters systematically. Its second book, on poruḷ (wealth, polity), is devoted to kingship. Couplet 381 lists six things a king must possess — army, people, wealth, ministers, allies and fortress — and the book contains chapters on the fortress (araṇ), the excellence of the army (paṭaimāṭci) and the pride of the soldier (paṭaiccerukku). The couplets praise an army that does not flee, that is ancient and tested, and that is well led; they praise the soldier who counts as lost the days on which he received no wound, and who prefers death in battle to dishonour. The Kuṟaḷ combines these heroic values with a strong emphasis on justice, generosity and the avoidance of needless cruelty in kingship, and it remains one of the most widely read ethical works in Tamil.

    3.8 The Purāṇas and Dhanurveda Chapters¶

    3.8.1 The Agni Purāṇa¶

    The Purāṇas are encyclopaedic texts that grew over many centuries, and several of them include chapters on kingship and war. The most important for this subject is the Agni Purāṇa, which contains a short group of four chapters on dhanurveda (248–251 in Rājendralāla Mitra’s edition; the chapter numbers differ slightly in other printed editions), together with chapters on royal duties, omens, the consecration of weapons and battle arrays (Mitra 1870–1879; Gangadharan 1984–1987). The Agni Purāṇa is a compilation whose present form is generally dated to the later first millennium CE or the early second; estimates range from about the eighth to the eleventh century, and some of its material may be older (Rocher 1986). It cannot be used as evidence for Vedic or Mauryan practice.

    The Agni Purāṇa’s dhanurveda divides fighting into five kinds, by the way the weapon is used: with weapons released by a machine, released by the hand, released and then recovered, not released at all (the sword), and without weapons (wrestling). It then describes the archer’s stances, the drawing and loosing of the bow, target practice, and the handling of the noose, sword, mace and many other weapons. Chapter 4 sets out its teaching in detail (Section 4.7.2). The Viṣṇudharmottara Purāṇa, probably composed in Kashmir in the second half of the first millennium CE, also includes material on kingship, weapons and the training of princes.

    3.8.2 Later dhanurveda texts¶

    Several later texts call themselves dhanurveda or include sections on it:

    • The Dhanurveda attributed to Vasiṣṭha (Vasiṣṭha-Dhanurveda-Saṃhitā), printed in the early twentieth century, is a compilation of uncertain date. It shares material with the Agni Purāṇa and with medieval anthologies, and its attribution to the Vedic sage is traditional. It cannot be dated before the second millennium CE with any confidence.

    • The Śārṅgadhara-paddhati, a Sanskrit anthology compiled in 1363, includes a section of verses on archery.

    • The Kodaṇḍamaṇḍana, attributed to Maṇḍana, an architect at the court of Rāṇā Kumbhā of Mewar in the fifteenth century, is a treatise on archery.

    • The Nītiprakāśikā, published by Gustav Oppert in 1882, is attributed in its colophons to Vaiśampāyana, the narrator of the Mahābhārata (Oppert 1882). The attribution is traditional and the work is medieval; it lists weapons and arrays and was used by Oppert in his study of Indian weapons (Oppert 1880).

    Other titles that appear in popular lists — a “Viśvāmitra Dhanurveda”, a “Hastavidyārṇava” of hand-to-hand combat, a “Yuddhatantradīpikā” — are either unpublished, unlocated or misdescribed. One example deserves correction: the Hastividyārṇava (“Ocean of Elephant Lore”) is an illustrated Assamese treatise on elephants, completed in 1734 by Sukumār Barkaith for the Ahom king Śiva Siṃha and his queen (Choudhury 1976). It is a work on the care, classification and medicine of elephants, not on unarmed combat.

    The pattern is clear. A body of practical knowledge about arms certainly existed in early India; the epics and the Arthaśāstra presuppose it. But the written dhanurveda that survives is a relatively late and partial codification, and the idea of a complete ancient “Dhanurveda” text is a construction of later tradition.

    3.9 Technical and Encyclopaedic Works¶

    3.9.1 Elephants and horses¶

    Specialised literatures on elephants (gajaśāstra, hastyāyurveda) and horses (aśvaśāstra, often associated with the name of Śālihotra or of the Pāṇḍava Nakula) deal with the breeding, classification, training and veterinary care of the animals on which armies depended. The Hastyāyurveda attributed to the sage Pālakāpya and the Kerala Mātaṅgalīlā are among the elephant texts; their dates are uncertain. Trautmann uses this literature, together with the Arthaśāstra, in his history of the war elephant (Trautmann 2015). Chapter 6 discusses it.

    3.9.2 Machines¶

    The Samarāṅgaṇasūtradhāra, an architectural encyclopaedia attributed to King Bhoja of Mālwa (eleventh century), includes a chapter on mechanical devices (yantra) that mentions machines for the defence of forts alongside automata and water devices. The Yuktikalpataru, also attributed to Bhoja, discusses among other things ships and weapons; the attribution is uncertain and the text as transmitted may be later. Both works are better evidence for the learned imagination of courts than for the actual machines used in war, but they show that military engineering was considered a fit subject for Sanskrit learning.

    3.9.3 The Mānasollāsa¶

    The Mānasollāsa (also called Abhilaṣitārthacintāmaṇi), compiled for the Cāḷukya king Someśvara III of Kalyāṇa around 1129–1131, is an encyclopaedia of the things a king should know and enjoy (Shrigondekar 1925–1961). Its sections on royal recreation include detailed accounts of wrestling (mallavinoda), of elephant-fights and of horsemanship, and its sections on government include military matters. It is one of the most important sources for the martial sports of the medieval Deccan (Chapter 14).

    3.9.4 The Śukranīti¶

    The Śukranīti, attributed to Śukra, the preceptor of the demons, is often cited for its description of guns (nālika) and gunpowder. Lallanji Gopal showed in 1962 that the text in its present form is a nineteenth-century composition (Gopal 1962). It is therefore no evidence for ancient knowledge of firearms, a claim made in the nineteenth century by Oppert and repeated since.

    3.10 Persian Manuals and Chronicles¶

    From the thirteenth century, the courts of north India produced a large literature in Persian. Among the earliest relevant works is the Ādāb al-ḥarb wa’l-shajāʿa (“The Rules of War and Bravery”) of Fakhr-i Mudabbir, written at Delhi in the early thirteenth century, which discusses the duties of rulers and generals, the organisation of armies, arms, sieges and battle arrays in the tradition of Persian “mirrors for princes”. The chronicles of the Delhi Sultanate record campaigns and military reforms from the sultans’ point of view (Section 3.14) (Jackson 1999).

    For the Mughal period, the memoirs of Bābur (written in Chagatai Turkish and later translated into Persian) give a soldier’s account of the battles of Pānīpat (1526) and Khanua (1527) (Thackston 1996). Abū’l-Faẓl’s Āʾīn-i Akbarī (completed c. 1598) describes the imperial army, the ranks (manṣab) of nobles, the arsenal, and the imperial stables of horses and elephants in statistical detail (Blochmann 1873). These works were written to glorify the dynasty, but their administrative information is of great value (Gommans 2002; Irvine 1903).

    3.11 Regional Literatures¶

    Regional literatures add other perspectives:

    • Rajasthani heroic poetry (rāso, vāt, khyāt) celebrates the deeds of Rajput warriors, often long after the events; the Pṛthvīrāj Rāso, in its surviving forms much later than its hero’s twelfth-century lifetime, is the most famous example (Talbot 2016).

    • Marathi chronicles (bakhar), such as the Sabhāsad Bakhar (1690s) on Śivājī, and ballads (povāḍā) preserve memories of the Maratha wars. The Ājñāpatra (1716), a statement of royal policy attributed to Rāmacandrapant Amātya, contains important chapters on forts and on the navy (Gordon 1993).

    • Assamese buraṅjī chronicles record the wars of the Ahom kingdom against the Mughals and others with unusual precision (Gait 1906).

    • Punjabi and Braj writings associated with the Sikh Gurus and their followers, including the compositions collected in the Dasam Granth, contain martial poetry and descriptions of battles (Dhavan 2011).

    3.12 Inscriptions¶

    Inscriptions are the most reliable source for the political and military history of early and medieval India, because they are contemporary and can usually be dated. Several kinds are relevant.

    • Royal eulogies (praśasti) list a king’s conquests and those of his ancestors. Samudragupta’s Allahabad pillar inscription, the Aihoḷe inscription of Pulakeśin II, and the long historical introductions (meykkīrtti) of Cōḻa inscriptions are examples. They are boastful and selective, but they record the names of enemies and battles and can often be checked against the inscriptions of the other side.

    • Grants of land on copper plates or stone often name the officers present, among them military commanders, and sometimes record grants to soldiers or to the families of men who died in battle.

    • Hero-stones commemorate individual warriors who died fighting (Chapter 13). They are the most abundant evidence for small-scale warfare and for the participation of ordinary people.

    • Temple inscriptions record gifts by soldiers and commanders and, in the Cōḻa period, the endowments made for regiments.

    3.13 Foreign Observers¶

    Foreign writers provide an outside view, often valuable precisely because they noticed what Indian writers took for granted. The most important include:

    • Greek and Latin authors: Arrian’s Anabasis and Indica, both drawing on the memoirs of Alexander’s officers and on Megasthenes; Strabo, Diodorus and Pliny, who preserve Megasthenes’ account of Mauryan India (Chakravarti 1941).

    • Chinese pilgrims: Faxian (early fifth century) and Xuanzang (seventh century), who describe the kingdoms they visited, including Harṣa’s army and court.

    • Arab and Persian geographers and scholars: the ninth- and tenth-century authors who described the Indian kingdoms of their time, and al-Bīrūnī, whose Kitāb al-Hind (c. 1030) is a remarkable account of Indian society and learning (Eaton 2019).

    • Travellers: Marco Polo, who visited south India in the 1290s; Ibn Baṭṭūṭa, who served Muḥammad bin Tughluq in the 1330s and 1340s; the Portuguese Domingo Paes and Fernão Nuniz, who described Vijayanagara and its army in the sixteenth century; and seventeenth-century European visitors to the Mughal court such as François Bernier and Niccolao Manucci, who described the Mughal army on the march.

    • European company records: from the seventeenth century, the letters and records of the Portuguese, Dutch, English and French companies contain a vast body of information on Indian armies, wars and military markets.

    Each of these sources has its biases — Greek writers saw India through the lens of their own culture, travellers repeated what they were told, company servants judged Indian states by their usefulness to trade — but together they provide a check on Indian court literature and fill many of its gaps.

    3.14 Persian Historical Writing¶

    The Persian chronicles of the Delhi Sultanate and the Mughal empire are the main narrative sources for the military history of north India from the thirteenth to the eighteenth century. Among the most important are Minhāj al-Sirāj Jūzjānī’s Ṭabaqāt-i Nāṣirī (1259–60), which covers the Ghurid conquest and the early sultanate; Ziyāʾ al-Dīn Baranī’s Tārīkh-i Fīrūz Shāhī (1357), with its account of ʿAlāʾ al-Dīn Khaljī’s reforms; Bābur’s memoirs; Abū’l-Faẓl’s Akbarnāma and Āʾīn-i Akbarī; and the many histories of the later Mughal period (Jackson 1999; Gommans 2002). Their authors were usually courtiers, and they wrote to glorify their patrons and to offer moral lessons; their accounts of battles follow literary conventions, and their figures for armies and casualties are often inflated. But they provide detailed chronologies, names, and much administrative information, and historians have learned to read them critically, comparing them with inscriptions, coins, regional sources and the accounts of foreign observers.

    3.15 The Vocabulary of War¶

    The languages of Indian warfare reflect its history. Sanskrit supplied a rich vocabulary of war, much of which passed into the regional languages. From the thirteenth century, Persian became the language of administration and of military affairs at the courts of the Delhi sultans, the Deccan sultanates and the Mughals, and many Persian and Turkic military terms entered Hindi, Urdu, Marathi, Bengali, Punjabi and other languages; some of them, such as sipāhī (sepoy), passed into English. Table 3.1 lists some common terms. It is not a list of exact equivalents — the institutions behind the words differed — but it shows how the two vocabularies existed side by side, and how readers moving between Sanskrit and Persian sources must translate not only words but concepts.

Military language, like military practice, was a meeting ground of traditions.

    3.16 Manuscripts and Editions¶

    Many of the texts discussed in this chapter survive in a small number of manuscripts, and several have never been critically edited. The Arthaśāstra itself was recovered only in the early twentieth century, when R. Shamasastry identified a palm-leaf manuscript of it at Mysore in 1905, and further manuscripts were found later that allowed Kangle to prepare his critical edition (Kangle 1960–1965). The dhanurveda texts are known mostly from late manuscripts and from printed editions of the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, some of them based on single manuscripts and of uncertain reliability. The Mānasollāsa was edited in three volumes between 1925 and 1961 (Shrigondekar 1925–1961); the Hastividyārṇava was published with its illustrations and a translation in 1976 (Choudhury 1976).

    Manuscript libraries in India — among them the Sarasvati Mahal Library at Thanjavur, the Oriental Research Institute at Mysore, the Bhandarkar Oriental Research Institute at Pune, and the Government Oriental Manuscripts Library at Chennai — and in Europe hold other works on arms, elephants, horses and statecraft, many of them unstudied. Their cataloguing, editing and translation would add considerably to our knowledge. Claims about the contents of unpublished manuscripts, however, should be treated with caution until the texts are edited and their dates established; several “ancient military treatises” cited in popular literature cannot be located at all.

    3.17 Translation and Exchange¶

    Texts on war, like other texts, moved between languages. At Akbar’s court the Mahābhārata was translated into Persian as the Razmnāma (“Book of War”) in the 1580s, and the Rāmāyaṇa was also translated; illustrated copies were made for the emperor and his nobles, showing the epic battles in the dress and arms of the Mughal age (Chapter 13). Persian works on horses and elephants drew on Sanskrit treatises, and Sanskrit and vernacular authors in turn borrowed from Persian. Kāmandaka’s Nītisāra travelled to Java, and the epics, with their battles and divine weapons, were retold in the languages of Southeast Asia and carved on the temples of Java and Cambodia. In the colonial period European scholars translated the Arthaśāstra, the epics and the dharmaśāstras, and their interpretations shaped how Indians themselves came to understand these texts.

    The textual traditions of war were never closed. Military knowledge, like other forms of knowledge, circulated among courts, communities and languages, and was transformed in the process.

    3.18 Reading the Sources in Translation¶

    Most readers will meet the sources described in this chapter in translation, and the choice of translation matters. For the Arthaśāstra, R. P. Kangle’s critical edition and annotated translation remains the scholarly standard, and Patrick Olivelle’s translation adds a fresh study of the text’s composition and date; R. Shamasastry’s pioneering translation of 1915 is historically important but less reliable, and L. N. Rangarajan’s rearranged version is readable but should be checked against the others (Kangle 1960–1965; Olivelle 2013; Shamasastry 1915; Rangarajan 1992). For the Mahābhārata, K. M. Ganguli’s complete prose translation of 1883–96 is freely available, while the incomplete Chicago translation begun by J. A. B. van Buitenen and continued by James Fitzgerald is based on the critical edition and is more accurate (Ganguli 1883–1896; Buitenen 1973–1978; Fitzgerald 2004). The Mānava Dharmaśāstra is best read in Olivelle’s critical edition and translation (Olivelle 2005).

    For the Tamil war poems, George Hart and Hank Heifetz’s translation of the Puṟanāṉūṟu is reliable and well annotated (Hart and Heifetz 1999). For the Mughal period, W. M. Thackston’s translation of Bābur’s memoirs and H. Blochmann’s translation of the first volume of the Āʾīn-i Akbarī are standard (Thackston 1996; Blochmann 1873). Readers should be cautious with anthologies and websites that print “verses” on war without precise references to edition, chapter and verse: many such quotations cannot be traced to any text, and several that circulate widely were composed in modern times.

    3.19 Reading Battle Narratives¶

    Battle narratives, whether in epic poetry, court chronicles or ballads, pose particular problems for the historian. They follow literary conventions: the hero’s single combat, the dramatic reversal, the omen, the heroic death. They use numbers to impress rather than to inform. They often concentrate on the deeds of a few leaders and ignore the mass of soldiers. And they were written after the event, often long after, by authors with reasons to praise or blame. The modern historian reads them alongside other evidence — inscriptions, documents, the accounts of the other side, archaeology, the geography of the battlefield — and asks what kind of truth each source can offer. A Persian chronicle may be reliable on the sequence of events and the names of commanders while exaggerating the size of the enemy; a Rajput ballad may be unreliable on facts but invaluable as evidence for how a battle was remembered; an inscription may record the outcome of a battle while saying nothing of how it was fought. Learning to read these sources critically is the beginning of military history.

    3.20 How to Read the Texts¶

    Three principles guide the use of these sources in this volume.

    First, date the text, not the tradition. A text that claims to transmit the teaching of a Vedic sage must be dated by its language, its contents and its manuscripts, not by its claim. The Agni Purāṇa’s dhanurveda is evidence for what a learned compiler of the late first millennium thought about weapons, not for the Vedic period.

    Second, distinguish norm from practice. Treatises tell rulers what they ought to do; chronicles, inscriptions and the accounts of observers tell us what rulers did. The two sometimes coincide, sometimes do not.

    Third, beware of numbers and lists. Sanskrit technical literature loves enumeration — four arms, six measures, thirty-two arrays, sixty-four arts. Such lists organise knowledge and aid memory; they do not necessarily describe distinct practices that all existed. Troop and casualty figures call for the same caution (Section 1.8).
