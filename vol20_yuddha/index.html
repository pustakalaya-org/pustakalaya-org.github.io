    Preface¶

    This is Volume 20 of the series Bhāratīya Jñāna Paramparā: Yuddha and Dhanurveda: Military Traditions. It covers both the military history and military thought of the subcontinent and the warrior’s training, ethics and arts. Its subject is war in South Asia from the fortified towns of the Indus civilisation to the sepoy armies of the East India Company: how armies were raised, paid, armed and led; what the classical texts — the Arthaśāstra, the epics, the dharmaśāstras and the late manuals called dhanurveda — say about war; how forts were built and taken, how navies fought, how rulers gathered intelligence; how the ethics of war were argued over and how they were, and were not, observed; and what became of the warrior’s arts, from wrestling and archery to kaḷarippayaṟṟu, silambam and gatka.

    The book tries to be fair to every tradition that shaped warfare in the subcontinent. Vedic chariot-fighters, Mauryan and Gupta armies, the Cōḻa fleet, Rajput clans, Turkic and Afghan cavalry, the Delhi sultans, Vijayanagara, the Mughals, the Ahoms, the Marathas, the Sikhs, Mysore, the Gorkhas and Dogras of the Himalaya, the hired Purbiya and Afghan soldiers of the military labour market, and the sepoys of the Company each receive attention on their own terms. Military history in India has often been written to praise one community or blame another; the aim here is to describe what armies did, using the evidence and the work of historians such as P. C. Chakravarti, Sarva Daman Singh, Jos Gommans, Dirk Kolff, Kaushik Roy and Thomas Trautmann, whose books are cited in the text and collected in the select bibliography.

    Primary texts are quoted in the original only where both the wording and the reference can be confirmed; elsewhere they are paraphrased, with a reference to a standard edition or translation. Several popular claims are discussed only to be set aside: that the epics describe nuclear weapons or aircraft, that the Buddhist monk Bodhidharma carried kung fu from India to China, that any Indian martial art is “the mother of all martial arts”, that an unbroken written science of dhanurveda survives from Vedic times, and that the rockets of Tipu Sultan were the origin of modern rocketry. Dates are given as the range accepted in current scholarship, with traditional dates labelled as such; army sizes and casualty figures are given only where a source can be named, and with the caution that ancient and medieval numbers deserve. The series dedication speaks of an unbroken tradition of knowledge. For the arts of war the continuity this volume finds is of a different kind: not a single science handed down from the Vedic age, but practical knowledge that was learned, borrowed, written down late and rewritten many times.

    The chapters on the martial arts describe practices and the claims made for them; they are not manuals. Nothing in this volume should be used as instruction in striking vital points or in any other technique that can injure, and claims about the healing power of marma or varmam treatment are reported as traditional claims, not as established medicine. No survey of this size can be complete, and every quotation should be checked against a critical edition before scholarly use. Four maps locate the passes and battlefields, the forts, the naval actions and the battles of the Company’s wars; a glossary of the principal technical terms and a chronology are provided at the end.

    Nikhil Kumar Rajput
    Ramanujan College, University of Delhi
