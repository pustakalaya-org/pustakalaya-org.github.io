    Chapter 2: Warfare in Early India, from the Harappans to the Age of Harṣa¶

    2.1 Introduction¶

    This chapter surveys warfare in the subcontinent from the cities of the Indus civilisation to the early seventh century CE. It is a long period, and the evidence for it changes in kind as well as quantity. For the Harappan period we have only archaeology; for the Vedic period, hymns and ritual texts; for the age of the Buddha, religious literature composed and edited later; and from Alexander’s invasion onwards, Greek and Latin accounts, inscriptions, coins and an increasing body of Sanskrit and Tamil literature. Throughout, the reader should remember that warfare is easier to infer than to describe: we know far more about what weapons and arrays existed in principle than about how any particular battle was fought.

    2.2 The Indus Civilisation¶

    The mature Harappan civilisation (c. 2600–1900 BCE) built large planned towns. Many of them — Harappa, Mohenjo-daro, Kalibangan, Dholavira, Lothal — had massive walls of mud-brick or stone around a raised “citadel” and sometimes around the lower town as well. Whether these walls were built mainly for defence is debated. They would certainly have impeded attackers, but they also protected against floods, controlled access to the town and expressed civic power. Archaeologists have found copper and bronze spearheads, arrowheads, knives and daggers, as well as sling-stones and clay balls; but they have found few weapons designed only for war, no clear evidence of destruction by large armies, and no depictions of battles. Gregory Possehl, summarising the evidence, concluded that the Indus civilisation shows remarkably little sign of organised warfare compared with contemporary Mesopotamia or Egypt (Possehl 2002). This does not mean that there was no violence, only that it is not visible in the record.

    Two popular claims need correction. First, there is no convincing evidence of the domesticated horse or of the spoked-wheel chariot in the mature Harappan cities. Claims of “Harappan horse bits” or of horse figures on seals rest on misidentifications. Second, the discovery at Sinauli in western Uttar Pradesh (c. 2000–1800 BCE) of burials with solid-wheeled vehicles, swords and shields has been described by its excavators as evidence of “chariots”. The vehicles had solid wheels and there is no evidence of the horses that drew them; whether they were war chariots in the later sense is uncertain and remains under study.

    2.3 The Vedic Period¶

    2.3.1 War in the Ṛgveda¶

    The hymns of the Ṛgveda (core c. 1500–1200 BCE) were composed by a society for which cattle were the chief form of wealth and raids for cattle were a normal form of conflict; one of the words for battle, gaviṣṭi, literally means “seeking cows”. The principal war-god is Indra, praised as the breaker of forts (purandara) and the winner of cattle, water and light. The most important military technology was the light, horse-drawn chariot (ratha) with spoked wheels, carrying a driver and a warrior armed with a bow (Singh 1965).

    Sarva Daman Singh’s study of the Vedic evidence describes the chariot, the bow (dhanus, possibly composite), arrows tipped with horn or metal and sometimes poisoned, the quiver, body armour (varman), helmets (śiprā), spears and axes. The hymns refer to purs, usually translated “forts”; these were probably earthworks or palisaded enclosures rather than stone cities (Singh 1965). Battles were fought between tribes (jana) and between the Āryas and peoples they called Dāsa or Dasyu.

    2.3.2 The Battle of the Ten Kings¶

    The best-known Vedic battle is the “battle of the ten kings” (dāśarājña), celebrated in hymns of the seventh book of the Ṛgveda, especially RV 7.18, 7.33 and 7.83 (Jamison and Brereton 2014). Sudās, king of the Bharatas, with the help of Indra and of his priest Vasiṣṭha, defeated a coalition of ten kings beside the river Paruṣṇī, identified with the Ravi in Punjab. The hymns say that the enemies tried to divert or cross the river and were drowned. The episode is one of the earliest named battles in the literature of the subcontinent, but it is known only from praise-poems composed by the victors’ priests, and its details — numbers, tactics, even the identity of all ten kings — cannot be reconstructed with confidence.

    2.3.3 Later Vedic kingship¶

    In the later Vedic period (c. 1200–500 BCE) chiefdoms became kingdoms, settlement moved eastward into the Ganges valley, and iron came into general use, first for weapons and later for tools. The great royal rituals described in the Brāhmaṇas — the royal consecration (rājasūya) and the horse sacrifice (aśvamedha) — contain martial elements: a mock cattle raid, a chariot drive, the year-long wandering of the sacrificial horse guarded by armed men. Among the king’s “jewels” (ratnin), the officials honoured during the consecration, are the army commander (senānī) and the charioteer (sūta) (Singh 1965; Thapar 2002).

    2.4 Iron, Cities and the Rise of States¶

    The emergence of the first large states in the Ganges valley in the first millennium BCE — the period sometimes called the “second urbanisation” — was connected with changes in technology and economy that also transformed warfare. Iron, which came into widespread use in the early first millennium BCE, allowed the production of more and cheaper weapons as well as tools for clearing forest and cultivating the heavy soils of the middle Ganges plain. Agricultural surpluses supported growing towns, rulers and armies. Many towns of the period were surrounded by earthen ramparts and moats, and some, like Rājagṛha and Kauśāmbī, by massive walls; the fortified city became the centre of the state. Coinage, in the form of punch-marked silver coins, made it easier to pay soldiers and officials. The rise of Magadha, which possessed rich agricultural land, iron ores in the hills to the south, and forests full of elephants, illustrates how these factors combined (Thapar 2002; Trautmann 2015). The armies of the Mauryas and their successors were built on this economic foundation.

    2.5 The Age of the Mahājanapadas and the Rise of Magadha¶

    By the sixth and fifth centuries BCE Buddhist and Jain texts list sixteen great states (mahājanapada) across north India, some ruled by kings and some by assemblies of clans (gaṇa or saṅgha). Over the following two centuries Magadha, in south Bihar, absorbed its rivals. Its rise owed much to its position on the Ganges, its iron ores, its rich rice lands, and the forests to the south that supplied elephants (Thapar 2002; Trautmann 2015).

    Jain canonical tradition describes the war of King Kūṇika (Ajātaśatru) of Magadha against the Vajjian confederacy and credits him with two remarkable devices: the mahāśilākaṇṭaka, apparently a machine for throwing large stones, and the rathamusala, a chariot fitted with a mace or blades. Whether these descriptions record real machines or exaggerate simpler devices is uncertain, but they show that siege and battlefield engines were imagined, and probably used, before the Mauryas.

    Greek writers who accompanied or followed Alexander describe the army of the Nanda kings of Magadha, beyond the Beas, as very large; the figures they give (in one version 200,000 infantry, 20,000 cavalry, 2,000 chariots and 3,000 or more elephants) differ between authors and probably reflect rumour reaching Alexander’s camp rather than any count (McCrindle 1893). They are best treated as evidence that Magadha was known to field an army much larger than those of the Punjab kingdoms.

    2.6 Alexander in the Punjab¶

    Alexander of Macedon crossed the Hindu Kush in 327 BCE and campaigned in the north-west and Punjab until 325 BCE. The Greek and Latin historians, especially Arrian, writing in the second century CE from earlier memoirs, are the first detailed external accounts of Indian armies in battle (Chakravarti 1941; Roy 2004).

    The best documented battle is that against Porus, ruler of the Paurava kingdom between the Jhelum (Hydaspes) and the Chenab, in 326 BCE (Figure 1.1). Porus held the far bank of the Jhelum with an army including infantry, cavalry, chariots and war elephants. Alexander made repeated feints along the river and then crossed by night upstream in a rainstorm. In the battle that followed, Alexander’s cavalry defeated Porus’s cavalry on the flank, while the Macedonian phalanx, armed with long pikes, held the elephants. Arrian and Curtius report that rain had made the ground slippery and muddy, hampering the Indian chariots and the archers, whose long bows had to be braced against the ground. The elephants, wounded and leaderless, eventually trampled friend and foe alike. Porus was wounded and captured, but Alexander restored him as ruler, now as an ally.

    Several points emerge from the Greek accounts. Indian armies of the north-west fought as combined forces of four arms. The elephants were formidable against horses unused to them, but were vulnerable to disciplined infantry and to panic. Indian archers used long bows that were powerful but slow. And Indian commanders did not yet have a cavalry able to match Alexander’s veteran horsemen. Modern attempts to identify Porus’s deployment with a named vyūha from the Sanskrit texts are speculation; no Indian account of the battle survives. Alexander’s army refused to advance beyond the Beas in 326 BCE, and he withdrew down the Indus.

    2.7 The Mauryan Empire¶

    2.7.1 Candragupta and Seleucus¶

    Within a few years of Alexander’s departure, Candragupta Maurya (r. c. 321–297 BCE) overthrew the Nandas and established an empire that eventually extended from Afghanistan to Bengal and into the Deccan. Around 303 BCE he fought Seleucus, Alexander’s successor in the east. The outcome was a treaty by which Seleucus ceded territories in the north-west and received five hundred war elephants, which he later used in the wars of the successors in western Asia. Trautmann has shown that this transfer was a turning point in the spread of the war elephant beyond India (Trautmann 2015).

    2.7.2 Megasthenes on the Mauryan army¶

    Megasthenes, Seleucus’s envoy to Candragupta’s capital Pāṭaliputra, wrote an account of India that survives only in quotations by later authors, chiefly Strabo, Diodorus and Arrian. According to Strabo’s summary, the war office was administered by thirty officials divided into six boards of five, responsible respectively for co-operation with the navy, for transport and supply, for the infantry, the cavalry, the chariots and the elephants. Soldiers were paid from the royal treasury and their weapons kept in royal arsenals. Megasthenes also reported that farmers worked their fields undisturbed while battles were fought nearby, a statement often cited as evidence of restraint in Indian warfare, though it is an idealised generalisation (Chakravarti 1941). Pliny gives the strength of the Mauryan army as 600,000 infantry, 30,000 cavalry and 9,000 elephants; like other ancient figures, these numbers cannot be verified.

    Megasthenes also divided the people of India into seven classes, a scheme that does not correspond exactly to the Indian varṇas. His fifth class was the soldiers: second in number only to the farmers, maintained with their horses and elephants at the king’s expense, and idle in peacetime while others made their weapons, tended their animals and drove their chariots (Chakravarti 1941; Thapar 1961). The picture of a professional, state-paid army agrees with the Arthaśāstra, though Megasthenes generalised from what he saw at the capital.

    2.7.3 Aśoka and the Kaliṅga war¶

    Candragupta’s grandson Aśoka (r. c. 268–232 BCE) conquered Kaliṅga (coastal Odisha) in about the eighth year of his reign, c. 261 BCE. His Thirteenth Rock Edict, inscribed in several versions across the empire (though, significantly, not in Kaliṅga itself), records the cost in his own words: 150,000 people were deported, 100,000 killed, and many times that number died. Aśoka says that he felt remorse and that thereafter he regarded “conquest by dhamma” as the best conquest (Hultzsch 1925; Thapar 1961). The edict is a rare example of a ruler publicly regretting a war, but it should be read carefully: Aśoka did not disband his army, and in the same edict he warns the forest peoples on his borders that he has power as well as compassion. The figures, though given by the victor himself, are round numbers and may not be precise.

    2.8 Between the Mauryas and the Guptas¶

    2.8.1 Invaders and the cavalry revolution¶

    After the Mauryan empire broke up in the second century BCE, north-western India was ruled successively by Indo-Greek kings, Śakas (Scythians), Parthians and Kuṣāṇas, all of whom came from or through the steppe and Iranian world. They brought with them a style of warfare in which cavalry was decisive: horse-archers using powerful composite bows, and heavily armoured horsemen with long lances (Section 2.9). These centuries probably saw the final decline of the war chariot in north India, although chariots remained in literary convention and ceremony long after they had disappeared from the battlefield (Roy 2004).

    2.8.2 Kaliṅga, the Deccan and the far south¶

    The Hāthīgumphā inscription of Khāravela of Kaliṅga (probably first century BCE) is one of the earliest royal records of campaigns: it claims victories against Magadha and a confederacy of Tamil states, records an army sent westward in defiance of the Sātavāhana king Sātakarṇi, and describes the recovery of a Jina image taken from Kaliṅga by a Nanda king. In the Deccan the Sātavāhana kings fought the Śaka Kṣatrapas of western India for control of the ports and trade routes (Section 2.10); their coins show ships.

    In the far south, the Tamil poems of the Caṅkam anthologies (core c. 1st–3rd centuries CE) describe warfare between the Cēra, Cōḻa and Pāṇṭiya kings and many smaller chiefs (Section 2.11) (Hart and Heifetz 1999). The battle of Veṇṇi, in which the Cōḻa king Karikāla defeated a coalition including the Cēra and Pāṇṭiya kings, is celebrated in several poems. The Tamil grammatical tradition classified war poetry by situation: veṭci (cattle raid), karantai (recovery of cattle), vañci (invasion), kāñci (resistance), uḻiñai (siege), nocci (defence of a fort), tumpai (pitched battle) and vākai (victory) (Hart and Heifetz 1999).

    2.9 The Kuṣāṇa Empire¶

    The Kuṣāṇas, a branch of the Yuezhi people who had migrated from the borders of China to Bactria, built in the first and second centuries CE an empire that stretched from Central Asia across Afghanistan into the Ganges valley as far as Mathura and beyond. Under Kaniṣka (probably early second century CE), it was one of the great powers of Eurasia, alongside Rome, Parthia and Han China, and it controlled important stretches of the Silk Roads. Kuṣāṇa coins and sculpture show kings in the dress of steppe horsemen — long belted coats, trousers and boots — and some carry swords and maces. Their armies, built around cavalry, brought the military practices of the steppe deep into north India. The Kuṣāṇa period also saw intense cultural exchange, visible in the Gandhāra and Mathurā schools of art and in the spread of Buddhism to Central Asia and China (Thapar 2002).

    2.10 The Western Deccan and the Śaka Kṣatrapas¶

    The wars between the Sātavāhana kings of the Deccan and the Śaka rulers of western India, known by the title Kṣatrapa (“satrap”), are among the best documented of the early centuries CE, because both sides left inscriptions. The prize was the rich trade of the western coast, whose ports, such as Bharukaccha (Broach) and Kalyāṇa, exported Indian goods to the Roman world, and the routes that linked them to the interior.

    An inscription of the queen mother Gautamī Balaśrī at the Buddhist caves of Nasik praises her son Gautamīputra Sātakarṇi (early second century CE) as the destroyer of Śakas, Yavanas and Pahlavas and the uprooter of the line of the Kṣaharāta ruler Nahapāna; coins of Nahapāna restruck by Gautamīputra confirm his victory. A generation later, the great rock inscription of the Śaka ruler Rudradāman at Junagadh in Gujarat (c. 150 CE) claims that he twice defeated Sātakarṇi, “lord of the Deccan”, but spared him because of a family relationship. The Junagadh inscription is also notable for being written in Sanskrit, and for recording the repair of a great reservoir damaged by a storm; the kings who fought these wars were also builders and administrators (Thapar 2002).

    These inscriptions show the language of military glory that would become standard in Indian royal inscriptions: the king as conqueror of named enemies, protector of the social order, and merciful victor who restores defeated rivals.

    2.11 Warfare in the Tamil Country¶

    The Tamil poems of the Caṅkam anthologies give a picture of warfare unlike anything in Sanskrit literature: concrete, local, and seen from the viewpoint of the participants and their families (Hart and Heifetz 1999). The kings of the Cēra, Cōḻa and Pāṇṭiya lines, and many smaller chiefs (vēḷir), fought constantly. Wars often began with cattle raids, in which bands of warriors seized the herds of an enemy village and the owners pursued them to recover them; men who died in such fights were commemorated with hero-stones (naṭukal), which the poems describe being set up, decorated with peacock feathers and worshipped. Larger wars involved the siege of forts, protected by ditches, walls and gates studded with spikes, and battles fought with elephants, chariots, cavalry and infantry armed with spears, swords, bows and shields.

    The poems describe the war drum (muracu), kept in its own shrine and worshipped, whose capture meant defeat; the garlands of flowers that identified the warriors of each side; the bards (pāṇar, poruṉar) who followed kings to war, praised their victories and received rich gifts; and the grief of mothers and wives. One celebrated theme is the king’s generosity: a king’s fame depended as much on his gifts to poets as on his victories. Another is the honour of death in battle: one poem refers to the custom of cutting with a sword the body of a king who had died of illness, so that he should not lose the heaven of heroes.

    These poems are the most important early evidence for warfare in south India, and they preserve a tradition that remained distinct from the Sanskrit one for many centuries, though the two gradually merged.

    2.12 Sri Lanka¶

    The Pali chronicles of Sri Lanka, the Dīpavaṃsa and the Mahāvaṃsa, compiled in the fourth to sixth centuries CE from earlier traditions, tell of wars between the Sinhalese kings of Anurādhapura and rulers from south India. The most famous is the war of King Duṭṭhagāmaṇī (second century BCE according to the chronicle) against the Tamil king Eḷāra, who had ruled Anurādhapura for many years. The chronicle describes Duṭṭhagāmaṇī’s champions, his war elephant Kaṇḍula, the siege of a series of forts, and the final single combat in which Eḷāra was killed; it also records that Duṭṭhagāmaṇī ordered Eḷāra to be honoured with a monument (Geiger 1912). The chronicle is a religious history written to glorify Buddhist kings, and its accounts of these wars are shaped by that purpose; its treatment of the ethics of the war is discussed in Chapter 12.

    Sri Lanka’s later wars lie outside the period of this chapter, and the rest of the volume returns to the island only in passing, so they are summarised here. The Cōḻas held the north of the island from the late tenth century until Vijayabāhu I drove them out around 1070 (Chapter 11); Parākramabāhu I of Polonnaruva (r. 1153–1186) sent expeditions to Burma and intervened in the civil wars of the Pāṇḍya kingdom. From the sixteenth century the European companies seized the coasts, and the hill kingdom of Kandy became the centre of resistance. Kandyan armies, fighting in forest and mountain country, destroyed Portuguese columns at Danture in 1594 and Randeniwela in 1630; Rājasiṃha II then allied with the Dutch, who expelled the Portuguese by 1658 and kept the coasts for themselves. The Dutch took and lost Kandy in the war of 1760–66, and the British garrison left in Kandy after its occupation in 1803 was destroyed. The kingdom fell only in 1815, when the British advanced with the support of disaffected Kandyan chiefs; its last king was exiled to Vellore (Section 10.17), and the rising of 1817–18 in Uva was suppressed with great severity (Silva 1981). These wars, like the hill wars of the Indian mainland, show how far terrain and seasons could offset the advantages of European arms.

    2.13 The Gupta Age¶

    The Gupta dynasty, founded in the early fourth century CE, built the largest north Indian empire since the Mauryas. Samudragupta’s long praise-inscription on the Allahabad pillar lists the kings he “uprooted” in the Ganges valley, those of the south whom he captured and released, and the frontier peoples and distant rulers who paid him homage (Thapar 2002). Gupta gold coins show the kings as archers, as horsemen, and in the act of killing lions, tigers and rhinoceroses: martial and hunting prowess was part of the royal image. Candragupta II (r. c. 375–415) defeated the Śaka rulers of western India and gained the ports of Gujarat.

    In the mid-fifth century the Hūṇas (Huns) attacked the north-west, and Skandagupta claims in his Bhitari inscription to have defeated them. Their later invasions, described in the next section, accelerated the break-up of Gupta power. Like the Śakas before them, the Hūṇas fought as mounted archers.

    2.14 The Hūṇas and Yaśodharman¶

    The Hūṇa invasions of the late fifth and early sixth centuries were among the most destructive episodes of early Indian history. The Hūṇas who entered India were probably a branch of the Hephthalites or related groups who had established a power in Bactria and Afghanistan. Their kings Toramāṇa and Mihirakula ruled the north-west and parts of central India in the early sixth century; Mihirakula is remembered in Buddhist sources, including the account of Xuanzang, as a persecutor of Buddhism and destroyer of monasteries, and in Kalhaṇa’s history of Kashmir as a cruel king.

    The Hūṇas were defeated by a coalition of Indian rulers. An inscription on a pillar at Sondhni near Mandasor in Malwa records that the Aulikara king Yaśodharman forced Mihirakula to pay him homage; the later Guptas also claimed victories. The Hūṇa episode hastened the fragmentation of north India into regional kingdoms, and some Hūṇa groups settled in India and were absorbed into the population, some of their descendants later claiming Rajput status. The episode illustrates both the vulnerability of north India to mounted invaders from the north-west and its capacity to absorb them.

    2.15 The Early Seventh Century: Harṣa and Pulakeśin II¶

    By about 600 CE the political map of India was a pattern of regional kingdoms. Two of their rulers are particularly well documented. Harṣavardhana (r. 606–647) of Thanesar and Kanauj is the subject of a prose biography, the Harṣacarita of his court poet Bāṇa, and is described by the Chinese pilgrim Xuanzang. Xuanzang’s account credits Harṣa with a very large army of elephants and cavalry built up in years of continuous campaigning; the figures he reports are those he was told at court. The Cāḷukya king Pulakeśin II of Bādāmi stopped Harṣa’s advance into the Deccan, probably on the Narmada, an event recorded in the Aihoḷe inscription of 634 (Thapar 2002). The Aihoḷe inscription says that Harṣa’s “joy” (harṣa) melted away when his elephants fell in battle. The pun works only because elephants were the decisive and the most prized arm of the age.

    2.16 The Battle Books of the Mahābhārata as Cultural History¶

    Even if the battle books of the Mahābhārata cannot be read as an account of a historical war, they are rich evidence for the ideals and imagination of warfare in the centuries of their composition (Brockington 1998; Hopkins 1889). They imagine war as a contest between heroes, each with his chariot, charioteer, banner, conch and named bow, fighting duels in which they exchange arrows and divine weapons while the common soldiers die in their thousands in the background. They describe the formal challenges and boasts that preceded combat, the honour paid to a worthy opponent, the shame of retreat, and the grief of the victors. They reveal anxieties about loyalty, as in Karṇa’s divided allegiance, and about the obligations of teachers and kinsmen on opposing sides, as in the cases of Bhīṣma and Droṇa. And they dramatise, again and again, the conflict between the rules of fair fighting and the will to win.

    In this sense the epic is a source for the history of warrior values rather than of military operations. Its influence on later Indian ideas of war was enormous: it supplied the models of heroism, the vocabulary of arrays and weapons, and the moral dilemmas through which later generations thought about war, from medieval court poets to modern nationalists and filmmakers.

    2.17 Military Administration in the Mauryan and Gupta States¶

    2.17.1 The Arthaśāstra’s army¶

    Whatever its precise date, the Arthaśāstra provides a picture of the military administration that a large Indian state of the early centuries was thought to need (Kangle 1960–1965; Olivelle 2013). The army was under a commander-in-chief (senāpati), with superintendents (adhyakṣa) for each arm — infantry, cavalry, chariots and elephants — and for the armoury, the stables and the elephant forests. Soldiers were paid from the treasury, and the text gives a scale of salaries for officials and soldiers, from the highest ministers and commanders to the trained foot-soldier. Weapons and armour were made in state workshops and kept in the royal armoury, to be issued when needed. Elephants, horses and their keepers, trainers and physicians were all under state control.

    The text also recognises that armies were not only made up of the king’s regular troops; its classification of troops by source and reliability is discussed in Section 7.5. It also discusses the army’s camp-followers and supporting personnel: engineers to clear roads and build bridges, labourers to dig ditches and build ramparts, and physicians with surgical instruments, medicines, oils and bandages, accompanied by women with food and drink, to stand behind the battle line to care for the wounded and encourage the fighting men (AŚ 10.3) (Olivelle 2013).

    2.17.2 Gupta officers¶

    Gupta inscriptions and seals name military and diplomatic officers whose titles suggest a developed military administration. The mahābalādhikṛta was a commander of forces; the mahādaṇḍanāyaka a high officer, apparently with military and judicial functions; the sāndhivigrahika was the minister of “peace and war”, responsible for relations with other states. Hariṣeṇa, who composed Samudragupta’s Allahabad inscription, held this last title, together with the offices of kumārāmātya and mahādaṇḍanāyaka (Thapar 2002). The offices of the sāndhivigrahika and of military commanders continue in the inscriptions of many later dynasties.

    2.18 Military Medicine in Early India¶

    Indian medicine developed a substantial body of knowledge about wounds, which must have been informed by the experience of war. The surgical treatise attributed to Suśruta, the Suśruta Saṃhitā, which reached its present form by c. 500 CE, devotes considerable attention to the extraction of foreign bodies (śalya), including arrowheads, from the body; it describes the signs by which a lodged arrowhead could be located, the instruments used to extract it, and the treatment of the wound afterwards. It also describes the marmas, the vital points where injury is especially dangerous (Chapter 14), the dressing of wounds, the setting of fractures and the stopping of bleeding. Although the Suśruta Saṃhitā is a medical, not a military, text, its surgery of wounds was relevant to soldiers, and the Arthaśāstra’s prescription that physicians should accompany the army shows that the connection was recognised.

    The epics, too, describe the care of wounded warriors: physicians extracting arrows, and the wounded Bhīṣma, lying on his bed of arrows, refusing the surgeons who come to treat him. In later centuries, Mughal and Maratha armies employed surgeons, and the care of wounded soldiers is mentioned in chronicles; but systematic military medicine, with field hospitals and medical services, developed only in the colonial army of the nineteenth century.

    2.19 The Fourfold Army and the Akṣauhiṇī¶

    2.19.1 The four limbs¶

    Throughout the period covered in this chapter the standard Sanskrit description of an army is caturaṅga, “having four limbs”: chariots (ratha), elephants (hastin, gaja), cavalry (aśva) and infantry (patti, padāti). The formula appears in the epics, the Arthaśāstra and the Buddhist and Jain canons, and it gave its name to the board game caturaṅga, the ancestor of chess (Section 2.20). The relative importance of the four arms changed over time: the chariot was the elite arm of the Vedic and early epic warrior, the elephant became the decisive arm from the later first millennium BCE, and cavalry rose in importance from the period of the Śakas and Kuṣāṇas onwards. In addition to the four arms, real armies included engineers, labourers, scouts, porters, camp followers, physicians, cooks and the boatmen who managed river crossings, as the Arthaśāstra recognises.

    2.19.2 The epic arithmetic¶

    The Mahābhārata gives a scheme of military units in which each unit is three times the size of the one below it, beginning with the patti of one chariot, one elephant, three horsemen and five foot-soldiers (MBh 1.2.15–23) (Buitenen 1973–1978). Ten anīkinīs make an akṣauhiṇī. Table 2.1 sets out the arithmetic.

    Table 2.1. The epic scheme of army units (Mahābhārata 1.2)
    Unit Chariots Elephants Horse Foot
    patti 1 1 3 5
    senāmukha (3 patti) 3 3 9 15
    gulma (3 senāmukha) 9 9 27 45
    gaṇa (3 gulma) 27 27 81 135
    vāhinī (3 gaṇa) 81 81 243 405
    pṛtanā (3 vāhinī) 243 243 729 1,215
    camū (3 pṛtanā) 729 729 2,187 3,645
    anīkinī (3 camū) 2,187 2,187 6,561 10,935
    akṣauhiṇī (10 anīkinī) 21,870 21,870 65,610 109,350

    The epic says that eighteen akṣauhiṇīs fought at Kurukṣetra, eleven for the Kauravas and seven for the Pāṇḍavas. By the epic’s own arithmetic this means some two million foot-soldiers, more than a million horsemen, and nearly four hundred thousand chariots and as many elephants: numbers far beyond what any pre-modern state could have assembled or fed. The scheme is an exercise in idealised symmetry, a way of expressing vastness, not a record of real organisation. Other texts, including the Arthaśāstra, give quite different unit schemes (Kangle 1960–1965), and real armies were never composed in fixed ratios of chariots to elephants to horses.

    2.20 From Caturaṅga to Chess¶

    The four-limbed army gave its name to India’s most widespread military-themed contribution to world culture. Chess is descended from an Indian board game called caturaṅga, whose pieces were the arms of the army. H. J. R. Murray’s A History of Chess (1913), which remains the fundamental study, concluded that the game originated in north-western India, probably in the sixth or early seventh century CE (Murray 1913). The earliest clear literary references are Indian and Persian. In the Harṣacarita, Bāṇa, praising the peace of Harṣa’s reign, plays on the double meaning of words for war and for the game: in that kingdom, he says, the only “armies” arrayed on squares were those of the board. A Middle Persian text of the Sasanian period, the Wizārišn ī čatrang (“The Explanation of Chess”), tells how an Indian king sent the game to the court of Khosrow I (r. 531–579) as a challenge, and how the minister Wuzurgmihr deciphered it and invented backgammon in reply. The story is legendary, but it shows that Persians believed the game had come from India.

    2.20.1 The pieces¶

    The names of the pieces trace the game’s journey (Table 2.2). The Sanskrit rājan (king) became the Persian shāh; the king’s counsellor or general (mantrin, senāpati) became the Persian farzīn and Arabic firz, which in Europe became the queen; the elephant (gaja, Persian pīl, Arabic al-fīl) became the Spanish alfil and the English bishop; the horse became the knight; the foot-soldier became the pawn; and the chariot became the Persian rukh, the rook, though the derivation of this word is uncertain. The word “chess” comes, through Old French, from shāh, and “checkmate” from the Persian shāh māt, “the king is helpless” (Murray 1913).

    Table 2.2. The pieces of caturaṅga and their descendants
    Sanskrit Persian Arabic English
    rājan (king) shāh shāh king
    mantrin, senāpati (counsellor, general) farzīn firz queen
    gaja, hastin (elephant) pīl fīl bishop
    aśva (horse) asp faras knight
    ratha (chariot) rukh rukh rook
    padāti (foot-soldier) piyāda baidaq pawn

    2.20.2 From Persia to the world, and back¶

    After the Arab conquest of Iran, chess (shaṭranj) became a favourite game of the Islamic world, whose players and writers developed its theory and composed problems. It reached Europe through Muslim Spain and Sicily by the tenth and eleventh centuries, and spread eastward to Central Asia, China, Korea, Japan and Southeast Asia, where local variants developed. In late fifteenth-century Europe the queen and bishop acquired their long-range moves, producing the faster modern game. Al-Bīrūnī in the eleventh century described a four-handed form played with dice in India; Murray rejected the view that this was the original form (Murray 1913).

    Chess remained popular in India in its own forms, played at the Mughal and other courts, and Indian rules differed from European ones until the nineteenth century, for example in the promotion of pawns and the king’s first move. In the 1850s the Bengali player Moheschunder Bannerjee played many games in Calcutta with the British player John Cochrane, and his style is remembered in the name “Indian Defence” given to a family of openings in which Black develops the bishop on the long diagonal. Mir Sultan Khan, a Punjabi player brought to England by his patron, won the British championship in 1929, 1932 and 1933. After independence chess became an organised sport; Viswanathan Anand became world champion in 2000 and held the undisputed title from 2007 to 2013, and D. Gukesh won the world championship in 2024. The game of the four-limbed army has returned to its homeland as a modern sport.

    2.21 Greek and Latin Writers on Indian Warfare¶

    Greek and Latin writers are among the earliest datable witnesses to warfare in India, and they are often quoted, sometimes uncritically. Their value and their limits differ. Herodotus wrote at second hand, about Indians from the Persian province in the north-west serving in the army of Xerxes. The historians of Alexander wrote centuries after the events, from the memoirs of his officers, which were themselves partisan; they magnify the difficulty of his victories and repeat the figures his officers gave. Megasthenes, who visited the Mauryan court around 300 BCE, survives only in quotation and paraphrase by later writers, who selected what interested them. All of them saw India through Greek categories, as when Megasthenes divided Indian society into seven “classes”, one of them the warriors (McCrindle 1877, 1893; Thapar 2002). Table 2.3 lists the principal writers.

    2.22 Assessment¶

    The wars and campaigns discussed in this chapter, with their main sources, are listed in Table 2.4. Two features of that list matter for the rest of the book. The first is the character of the sources. Before the Mauryas almost every war is known from the poetry or the religious literature of the victors, and even after them the best evidence comes from royal inscriptions written to praise a conqueror and from foreigners who never saw a battle; no Indian account of a single battle of this period describes how it was fought. The second is the importance of contact. The horse and chariot, the mounted archer of the steppe and the Hellenistic and Kuṣāṇa styles of cavalry each came from outside, while the war elephant, perfected in the Ganges valley, went out from India to the Mediterranean; iron, by contrast, was probably worked in India from local beginnings. The texts examined in the next chapter were composed against this background, and they should be read in its light.
