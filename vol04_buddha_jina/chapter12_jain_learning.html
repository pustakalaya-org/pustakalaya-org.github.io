    Chapter 12: Jain Learning: Language, History and Literature¶

    12.1 Learning in a Mendicant Tradition¶

    The preceding chapters have treated Jain philosophy, mathematics, cosmology and ethics. They do not exhaust what Jain authors wrote. Over some fifteen centuries Jain mendicants, and later Jain laymen, produced grammars and dictionaries, treatises on poetics and metre, works on astronomy and coinage, histories of their own lineages and of the kings who patronised them, and a large narrative and poetic literature in Prakrit, Sanskrit, Apabhraṃśa and the regional languages. Much of this writing was addressed to a readership far wider than the Jain community, and some of it became standard in Indian learning as a whole (Winternitz 1933; Dundas 2002; Jacobsen et al. 2020).

    Several features of Jain religious life explain this breadth. Jain mendicants were the teachers of lay communities, and from the early medieval period those communities were dominated by merchants and royal servants who needed literacy, arithmetic and a command of the courtly languages. Mendicants moved between towns and stayed for the rainy season wherever a lay community invited them (Chapter 13), and many of them spent long periods at royal courts — of the Gaṅgas and Rāṣṭrakūṭas in the Deccan, of the Caulukyas in Gujarat, and later of the Delhi sultans and the Mughals. The copying and gift of manuscripts was a religious act, and the libraries (bhaṇḍāras) that resulted preserved Brahmanical and Buddhist works as well as Jain ones (Chapter 8, Section 8.5.2). The Digambaras, finally, gave secular learning a place in their classification of scripture. Of their four “expositions” (anuyoga), one, the prathamānuyoga, comprises narrative and universal history, and another, the karaṇānuyoga, cosmography and calculation; the others treat conduct (caraṇānuyoga) and the categories of reality (dravyānuyoga).

    12.2 Grammar and Lexicography¶

    Jain grammarians wrote within the Pāṇinian tradition rather than against it, but they produced independent systems that were shorter, easier to teach and free of the Vedic rules that Pāṇini had included (Scharfe 1977). The earliest is the Jainendra-vyākaraṇa of the Digambara Devanandin, better known as Pūjyapāda, who probably wrote in the fifth or sixth century and is also the author of the Sarvārthasiddhi, the standard Digambara commentary on the Tattvārthasūtra. His grammar survives in two recensions, a shorter one that is probably original and an expanded one. In the ninth century the Yāpanīya monk Pālyakīrti, known as Śākaṭāyana, composed a new grammar with his own commentary, the Amoghavṛtti, named after his patron, the Rāṣṭrakūṭa king Amoghavarṣa I.

    The best-known Jain grammar is Hemacandra’s Siddhahemaśabdānuśāsana, written in the 1130s or 1140s for the Caulukya king Jayasiṃha Siddharāja of Gujarat; according to the later Prabhāvakacarita, the king commissioned it after his conquest of Mālava so that Gujarat would have a grammar to match the one associated with King Bhoja of Dhārā. Its first seven chapters, with Hemacandra’s own commentaries, cover Sanskrit. The eighth is a grammar of the Prakrits — Māhārāṣṭrī, Śaurasenī, Māgadhī, the Paiśācī dialects and Apabhraṃśa — and remains a basic source for the history of the Middle Indo-Aryan languages, not least because its rules on Apabhraṃśa are illustrated with verses that survive nowhere else. Hemacandra also wrote a long poem, the Dvyāśraya (“having two supports”), whose Sanskrit cantos narrate the history of the Caulukya kings while illustrating, in order, the rules of his grammar; its Prakrit continuation, the Kumārapālacarita, does the same for the Prakrit rules (Dundas 2002; Winternitz 1933).

    Lexicography was a Jain speciality. The poet Dhanapāla of Dhārā compiled the Pāiyalacchī Nāmamālā, a Prakrit dictionary of synonyms, in 972 or 973, one of the earliest Prakrit lexicons. Hemacandra again produced the most complete set of tools: the Abhidhānacintāmaṇi, a dictionary of Sanskrit synonyms arranged by subject; the Anekārthasaṅgraha, on words with several meanings; the Nighaṇṭuśeṣa, on the names of plants; and the Deśīnāmamālā, a list with his own commentary of “regional” (deśī) words that could not be derived from Sanskrit. The last is one of the few pre-modern Indian works to treat non-Sanskritic vocabulary as worth recording for its own sake. Hemacandra’s Kāvyānuśāsana and Chandonuśāsana, on poetics and metre, completed a curriculum through which a student could learn the whole apparatus of literary composition from Jain textbooks; Indian poetics more generally is the subject of Volume 8 (Kāvya Kalpalatā), and grammar of Volume 10 (Śabda Vidyā).

    12.3 Astronomy, Calculation and the Sciences of Trade¶

    Jain astronomy began as part of cosmography. The canonical Sūryaprajñapti and Candraprajñapti and the later Jyotiṣkaraṇḍaka describe a Jambūdvīpa lit by two suns and two moons, which take turns to cross the sky as they circle Mount Meru, and they reckon time in a five-year cycle (yuga) of lunar and solar months that closely resembles the calendar of the Vedic Jyotiṣa (Plofker 2009). The geometry needed for this cosmos, and the mathematics of Mahāvīra’s Gaṇitasārasaṅgraha, are described in Chapter 10.

    Later Jain authors took part in the mathematical astronomy of their time. Two cases from the fourteenth century show Jain expertise put at the service of new rulers. Ṭhakkura Pherū, a Śvetāmbara layman who served in the mint of the Khaljī sultans of Delhi, wrote short Prakrit handbooks on arithmetic (Gaṇitasārakaumudī), astrology, architecture, gemstones and — unusually — on the coins that passed through the mint, with their weights and silver content (Dravyaparīkṣā). The Śvetāmbara monk Mahendra Sūri, at the court of Fīrūz Shāh Tughluq, wrote the Yantrarāja (1370), the first Sanskrit treatise on the astrolabe, an instrument he learned of from the Islamic astronomers of the court (Plofker 2009). Astronomy as a whole is treated in Volume 12 (Khagola Jyotiṣa).

    12.4 Jain Historical Writing¶

    Jain authors kept careful records of their own lineages. The list of elders (sthavirāvalī) at the end of the Kalpasūtra names the teachers and branches of the early community, and the Mathurā inscriptions show that some of its names were real (Chapter 8, Section 8.8). The later gacchas kept lists of their successive heads (paṭṭāvalī), which give names and dates of initiation and death. These are sectarian documents, but they amount to a continuous chronology of Śvetāmbara monastic history from the early medieval period onwards.

    Hemacandra’s Pariśiṣṭaparvan (“Appendix”), written as a supplement to his universal history of the sixty-three illustrious persons, tells the lives of the Jain elders after Mahāvīra. It includes the stories of Bhadrabāhu and of the Maurya emperor Candragupta and his minister Cāṇakya, and is a principal source for the Jain tradition of Candragupta’s conversion (Hemacandra 1998). From the thirteenth century Śvetāmbara monks in Gujarat also developed a distinctive genre of historical anecdote, the prabandha. Prabhācandra’s Prabhāvakacarita (1277/78) tells the lives of twenty-two great teachers who “illuminated” the faith. Merutuṅga’s Prabandhacintāmaṇi (“Wishing-Stone of Narratives”), completed at Vardhamāna (Wadhwan) in 1305, gathers stories of the kings of Gujarat and Mālava — Bhoja of Dhārā, Jayasiṃha Siddharāja, Kumārapāla — and of their poets and ministers (Merutuṅga 1901). Rājaśekhara’s Prabandhakośa (1349) continues the genre. Jinaprabha Sūri’s Vividhatīrthakalpa, completed about 1333, is a different kind of work: a guide to more than fifty pilgrimage sites, from Śatruñjaya and Girnār to Mathurā and Pāṭaliputra, recording local traditions, the history of their temples and their destruction and restoration.

    Historians treat these works with caution. The prabandhas were written to instruct and to honour the faith; their stories are often anecdotal, sometimes legendary, and are usually set down a century or more after the events. Merutuṅga himself says that he collected stories that were current in his time. Yet for the history of Caulukya Gujarat and Paramāra Mālava they are among the few narrative sources that survive, and much of what is told about Bhoja’s court, or about the rivalries of poets and ministers, comes from them (Dundas 2002).

    12.5 Jain Scholars under the Delhi Sultanate¶

    The Turkish conquests of the late twelfth and thirteenth centuries, which ended the great Buddhist monasteries of the east (Chapter 7), also brought destruction to Jain temples in the west; Jain sources record, for example, the sack of the temples on Śatruñjaya in 1313. Jain communities nevertheless adapted to the new order, and Jain sources describe a series of negotiated relationships with the sultans of Delhi. Jain accounts relate that Samarā Śāh, an Osvāl merchant in the service of the governor of Gujarat, obtained permission to restore the main temple at Śatruñjaya in 1315. Jinaprabha Sūri, of the Kharatara Gaccha, is said in Jain sources, including sections of his own Vividhatīrthakalpa, to have been received with honour at the court of Muḥammad bin Tughluq (r. 1325–1351), to have obtained an order (farmān) protecting Jain pilgrims and temples, and to have recovered a sacred image that had been carried off to Delhi (Dundas 2002). The details come from Jain writers and cannot all be checked; but together with the careers of Ṭhakkura Pherū and Mahendra Sūri they show Jain merchants, officials and monks finding a place in the Sultanate state more than two centuries before Hīravijaya Sūri’s better-known meetings with Akbar (Chapter 14, Section 14.7).

    12.6 Narrative Literature in Prakrit and Sanskrit¶

    Jain authors built up one of the largest narrative literatures of pre-modern India, much of it intended for the sermons in which mendicants taught the laity. Its organising frame is a universal history of the sixty-three “illustrious persons” (śalākāpuruṣa) of each half-cycle of time: the twenty-four Tīrthaṅkaras, twelve universal emperors (cakravartin), and nine each of the Baladevas, the Vāsudevas and their adversaries the Prativāsudevas. The scheme is told most fully in the Digambara Mahāpurāṇa of Jinasena and Guṇabhadra (9th century) and in Hemacandra’s Triṣaṣṭiśalākāpuruṣacarita (Jaini 1979; Dundas 2002).

    Within this frame Jain poets retold the epics. Vimalasūri’s Prakrit Paümacariya, of disputed date but perhaps from the early centuries CE, is the earliest Jain Rāmāyaṇa, and the Harivaṃśapurāṇa of a Jinasena of the Punnāṭa lineage (783 CE, not the author of the Mahāpurāṇa) gives a Jain version of the Kṛṣṇa story. These versions rationalise the epic narratives — Rāvaṇa, for example, is a learned vidyādhara rather than a demon, and it is Lakṣmaṇa, the Vāsudeva, not Rāma, who kills him — and recast them in terms of Jain ethics, so that Rāma ends his life as a Jain mendicant and attains liberation. The epics and Purāṇas are treated in Volume 7 (Itihāsa–Purāṇa).

    Alongside universal history stands a wide range of prose and verse fiction. Saṅghadāsa’s Prakrit Vasudevahiṇḍī, composed before the seventh century, is a Jain adaptation of the lost Bṛhatkathā. Haribhadra’s Samarāiccakahā follows two souls, one virtuous and one consumed by hatred, through nine successive births, and his Dhūrtākhyāna (“Tales of the Rogues”) satirises the improbable stories of the Purāṇas. Uddyotana Sūri’s Kuvalayamālā, completed at Jābālipura (Jalor) in 779 CE, is a Prakrit romance celebrated also for a scene in which merchants from eighteen regions of India speak, and are characterised by, their own languages. Siddharṣi’s Sanskrit Upamitibhavaprapañcā Kathā (906 CE) is an allegory in which the passions and virtues appear as persons, and Dhanapāla’s Tilakamañjarī, written at the court of Bhoja, is a Sanskrit prose romance in the manner of Bāṇa. Story collections (kathākośa) supplied preachers with exempla for every vow and virtue, and many Indian folk tales are first recorded in them (Granoff 1990; Winternitz 1933).

    12.7 Apabhraṃśa and the Regional Languages¶

    From the late first millennium Jain poets wrote extensively in Apabhraṃśa, the literary form of the late Middle Indo-Aryan vernaculars, and they are the chief reason why an Apabhraṃśa literature survives at all. Svayambhū’s Paümacariu, a Rāma epic of perhaps the ninth century, and the Mahāpurāṇa that Puṣpadanta composed at the Rāṣṭrakūṭa capital Mānyakheṭa in the 960s, are its major works; the Bhavisattakaha of another Dhanapāla is a popular romance of a merchant’s son (Winternitz 1933).

    Jain writers also stand at the beginning of several regional literatures. In Kannada the treatise on poetics Kavirājamārga (c. 850), associated with the court of Amoghavarṣa I, is the earliest extant literary work, and the first great poets were Jains: Pampa (Ādipurāṇa and Vikramārjunavijaya, a retelling of the Mahābhārata, both 941), Ponna (Śāntipurāṇa, c. 950) and Ranna (Ajitapurāṇa, 993, and the Gadāyuddha), remembered together as the “three gems” (ratnatraya) of Kannada literature. The Cāmuṇḍarāyapurāṇa (978) of Cāmuṇḍarāya, the minister who commissioned the Bāhubali colossus at Śravaṇabeḷagoḷa, is the earliest surviving work of Kannada prose, and Nāgacandra’s Jain Rāmāyaṇa (c. 1100) continued the epic tradition. These poets wrote in courtly as well as religious genres, and their works are still read as classics of the language (Desai 1957; Dundas 2002).

    In Tamil, Jain authors produced the Cīvakacintāmaṇi of Tiruttakkatēvar (9th–10th century), a long epic of a prince who ends as a Jain ascetic; the Nālaṭiyār, an anthology of four hundred moral quatrains traditionally ascribed to Jain monks; the Nīlakēci, a polemical poem in which a Jain heroine refutes rival schools; the treatises on prosody of Amitacākarar (11th century); and Pavaṇanti’s grammar Naṉṉūl (c. 13th century), still the standard traditional grammar of Tamil. In western India, Jain monks composed the earliest substantial works in Old Gujarati, the narrative poems called rāsa, among which Śālibhadra Sūri’s Bharateśvara-Bāhubali-rāsa (1185) is often counted the first; and in north India the merchant Banārasīdās wrote the first autobiography in Hindi (Chapter 13, Section 13.6.6). The Buddhist share in Indian literature, and the two traditions’ contributions side by side, are discussed in Chapter 14 (Section 14.6).

    12.8 Jain Learning in Perspective¶

    It would be wrong to treat this literature as a separate “Jain science”. Jain grammarians worked within the Pāṇinian tradition, Jain lexicographers and poeticians drew on Brahmanical predecessors, and Jain astronomers learned from Islamic colleagues. What distinguishes the Jain contribution is its continuity and its social base. Because learning was carried by wandering mendicants and by lay merchant communities rather than by a few great monasteries, it did not depend on the survival of any single institution, and it was preserved in libraries that the community itself maintained. That pattern, explored for religious practice in Chapter 13, is one reason why so much of the history of medieval western and southern India is known from Jain texts, and why the Jain share in Indian knowledge is larger than the size of the community would suggest.
