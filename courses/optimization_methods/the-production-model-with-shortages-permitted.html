    Lesson 34: The Production Model with Shortages Permitted¶

    34.1 Learning Outcomes¶

    By the end of this hour a student will be able to:

    1. Draw the four-phase inventory profile of the general production–backorder model.
    2. Derive \(Q^{*}\), \(I_{\max}\) and \(B^{*}\) for the combined model.
    3. Show that the general model reduces to each of Lessons 31–33 in the appropriate limit.
    4. Assemble the complete family of deterministic inventory models in one table.
    5. Select the right member of the family for a given situation.

    34.2 The General Deterministic Model¶

    This lesson combines the two extensions studied so far:

    • Finite replenishment (Lesson 33): production at rate \(P>D\), not instantaneous delivery.
    • Planned backorders (Lesson 32): shortages are permitted and cost \(C_s\) per unit per unit time.

    The result is the most general deterministic EOQ-family model, with four parameters \((C_o, C_h, C_s, P)\) and two decision variables \((Q, I_{\max})\). Every earlier model is a special case.

    The four-phase profile¶

     stock
       |        /\                    /\
  Imax |      /   \                 /   \
       |    /       \             /       \
     0 |---/----------\----------/----------\-------  time
       |  /            \        /            \
    -B | /              \______/              \____
       |<t1><--t2-->|<t3>|<-t4->|
       |<---------- t ----------->|
    Phase What is happening Rate Duration
    \(t_1\) Production on; backlog \(B\) being cleared \(+(P-D)\) \(B/(P-D)\)
    \(t_2\) Production on; stock building to \(I_{\max}\) \(+(P-D)\) \(I_{\max}/(P-D)\)
    \(t_3\) Production off; stock depleting to zero \(-D\) \(I_{\max}/D\)
    \(t_4\) Production off; backlog building to \(B\) \(-D\) \(B/D\)

    Production runs for \(t_1+t_2 = Q/P\) and the whole cycle is \(t=Q/D\).

    The average levels¶

    As in Lesson 32, both averages are triangle areas divided by the full cycle. Writing \(f=1-D/P\) and noting that the peak-to-trough range is \(Qf\) (the same total build-up as in Lesson 33):

    \[\text{average stock}=\frac{I_{\max}^{2}}{2Qf},\qquad \text{average shortage}=\frac{B^{2}}{2Qf},\qquad I_{\max}+B=Qf. \tag{1.1}\]

    Setting \(f=1\) recovers Lesson 32; setting \(B=0\) recovers Lesson 33.

    34.3 Deriving the Optimum¶

    \[TRC(Q,I_{\max})=\frac{C_oD}{Q}+\frac{C_hI_{\max}^{2}}{2Qf}+\frac{C_s(Qf-I_{\max})^{2}}{2Qf}\tag{2.1}\]

    Optimising over I_max¶

    \[\frac{\partial TRC}{\partial I_{\max}}=\frac{C_hI_{\max}}{Qf}-\frac{C_s(Qf-I_{\max})}{Qf}=0\]

    Result 1. $\(\boxed{\ I_{\max}^{*}=\frac{C_s}{C_h+C_s}\,Qf,\qquad B^{*}=\frac{C_h}{C_h+C_s}\,Qf\ }\tag{2.2}\)$

    The same critical ratio \(\dfrac{C_s}{C_h+C_s}\) as in Lesson 32, now applied to \(Qf\) instead of \(Q\).

    Optimising over Q¶

    Substituting (2.2) into (2.1) reduces the holding-plus-shortage term to \(\dfrac{Qf}{2}\cdot\dfrac{C_hC_s}{C_h+C_s}\), giving an EOQ in disguise:

    Result 2 (general production–backorder model). With $\(f=1-\frac{D}{P},\qquad k=\sqrt{\frac{C_h+C_s}{C_s}},\)$ $\(\boxed{\ Q^{*}=\sqrt{\frac{2C_oD}{C_hf}}\cdot k=\frac{Q^{*}_{\text{EOQ}}\cdot k}{\sqrt f}\ }\tag{2.3}\)$ $\(\boxed{\ I^{*}_{\max}=\frac{Q^{*}f}{k^{2}}=\frac{Q^{*}_{\text{EOQ}}\sqrt f}{k},\qquad B^{*}=Q^{*}f-I^{*}_{\max}\ }\tag{2.4}\)$ $\(\boxed{\ TRC^{*}=\frac{\sqrt{2C_oDC_h\,f}}{k}=\frac{TRC^{*}_{\text{EOQ}}\sqrt f}{k}\ }\tag{2.5}\)$

    Both corrections multiply cleanly: \(\sqrt f\) from finite replenishment and \(1/k\) from backordering.

    34.4 The Complete Family¶

    Model \(P\) \(C_s\) \(Q^{*}\) \(TRC^{*}\) Lesson
    Classical EOQ \(\infty\) \(\infty\) \(\sqrt{\dfrac{2C_oD}{C_h}}\) \(\sqrt{2C_oDC_h}\) 31
    EOQ with backorders \(\infty\) finite \(\sqrt{\dfrac{2C_oD}{C_h}}\,k\) \(\dfrac{\sqrt{2C_oDC_h}}{k}\) 32
    EPQ, no shortage finite \(\infty\) \(\sqrt{\dfrac{2C_oD}{C_hf}}\) \(\sqrt{2C_oDC_h f}\) 33
    General finite finite \(\sqrt{\dfrac{2C_oD}{C_hf}}\,k\) \(\dfrac{\sqrt{2C_oDC_hf}}{k}\) 34

    with \(f=1-D/P\) and \(k=\sqrt{(C_h+C_s)/C_s}\).

    One formula to remember. $\(Q^{*}=\sqrt{\frac{2C_oD}{C_h}}\times\underbrace{\frac{1}{\sqrt f}}_{\text{finite }P}\times\underbrace{k}_{\text{backorders}}, \qquad TRC^{*}=\sqrt{2C_oDC_h}\times\sqrt f\times\frac1k\)$ Both extensions increase \(Q\) and decrease \(TRC\), and they act independently.

    34.5 Solved Problems¶

    Problem 1 — The general model¶

    \(D=10{,}000\) units/yr, \(P=25{,}000\) units/yr, \(C_o=₹150\), \(C_h=₹8\)/unit/yr, \(C_s=₹25\)/unit/yr. Find the complete policy and compare with all three earlier models.

    Solution.

    Step 1 — the two factors.

    \[f=1-\frac{10000}{25000}=0.6,\qquad k=\sqrt{\frac{8+25}{25}}=\sqrt{1.32}=1.14891\]

    Step 2 — the batch.

    \[Q^{*}=\sqrt{\frac{2(150)(10000)}{8(0.6)}}\times1.14891=790.57\times1.14891=\mathbf{908.30\text{ units}}\]

    Step 3 — the levels. First \(Q^*f = 908.30(0.6)=544.98\) — the peak-to-trough range.

    \[I^{*}_{\max}=\frac{25}{33}(544.98)=\mathbf{412.86\text{ units}}\]
    \[B^{*}=\frac{8}{33}(544.98)=\mathbf{132.12\text{ units}}\]

    Step 4 — the cost.

    \[TRC^{*}=\frac{\sqrt{2(150)(10000)(8)(0.6)}}{1.14891}=\frac{3794.73}{1.14891}=\mathbf{₹3302.89\text{ per year}}\]

    Step 5 — verification by components.

    \[\text{setup}=\frac{150(10000)}{908.30}=₹1651.45\]
    \[\text{holding}=\frac{8(412.86)^2}{2(908.30)(0.6)}=\frac{8(170{,}453)}{1089.96}=₹1251.10\]
    \[\text{shortage}=\frac{25(132.12)^2}{2(908.30)(0.6)}=\frac{25(17{,}455)}{1089.96}=₹400.35\]
    \[\text{TOTAL}=1651.45+1251.10+400.35=₹3302.90\ \checkmark\]

    Again setup cost \(=\) holding \(+\) shortage.

    Step 6 — the whole family, same data.

    Model \(f\) \(k\) \(Q^{*}\) Peak stock \(TRC^{*}\) Saving vs EOQ
    EOQ (L31) 1.0 1.0000 612.37 612.37 ₹4898.98 —
    EOQ + backorders (L32) 1.0 1.1489 703.56 533.00 ₹4264.01 13.0 %
    EPQ (L33) 0.6 1.0000 790.57 474.34 ₹3794.73 22.5 %
    General (L34) 0.6 1.1489 908.30 412.86 ₹3302.89 32.6 %

    Interpretation. Each relaxation makes the batch larger and the cost lower, and the two effects compose multiplicatively: \(0.775\times0.870=0.674\), and indeed \(3302.89/4898.98=0.674\) ✓. Peak stock falls monotonically down the table — from \(612\) to \(413\) units, a \(33\,\%\) reduction in the warehouse space required, achieved while increasing the batch size by \(48\,\%\).

    Problem 2 — Checking the limits¶

    Verify that Result 2 reduces correctly in each of the three limiting cases, using Problem 1's data.

    Solution.

    (a) \(P\to\infty\) (instantaneous delivery). Then \(f\to1\):

    \[Q^{*}\to 612.37(1.14891)=703.56,\quad TRC^{*}\to\frac{4898.98}{1.14891}=₹4264.01\]

    — exactly Lesson 32's backorder model ✓.

    (b) \(C_s\to\infty\) (no shortages tolerated). Then \(k\to1\) and \(I^*_{\max}\to Q^*f\), \(B^*\to0\):

    \[Q^{*}\to790.57,\quad TRC^{*}\to₹3794.73\]

    — exactly Lesson 33's EPQ ✓.

    (c) Both. \(f\to1\) and \(k\to1\):

    \[Q^{*}\to612.37,\quad TRC^{*}\to₹4898.98\]

    — classical EOQ ✓.

    Interpretation. Always test a general formula against its special cases. It costs two minutes, catches almost every algebraic slip, and is the technique by which the correctness of (2.3)–(2.5) can be confirmed without re-deriving them. A formula that fails a limit check is wrong, whatever the derivation looked like.

    Problem 3 — Reading the cycle structure¶

    For Problem 1's policy, find the four phase durations and state how long the firm is out of stock.

    Solution. \(Q^*=908.30\), \(I^*_{\max}=412.86\), \(B^*=132.12\), \(P-D=15{,}000\)/yr.

    \[t=\frac{Q^{*}}{D}=\frac{908.30}{10000}=0.09083\text{ yr}=\mathbf{33.2\text{ days}}\ (365\text{-day year})\]
    Phase Formula Value (yr) Days
    \(t_1\) clearing backlog \(B/(P-D)=132.12/15000\) 0.00881 3.2
    \(t_2\) building stock \(I_{\max}/(P-D)=412.86/15000\) 0.02752 10.0
    \(t_3\) depleting stock \(I_{\max}/D=412.86/10000\) 0.04129 15.1
    \(t_4\) building backlog \(B/D=132.12/10000\) 0.01321 4.8
    Total 0.09083 33.2

    Checks. Production time \(t_1+t_2=0.03632\) yr \(=Q^*/P=908.30/25000=0.03632\) ✓. Time in stock \(t_2+t_3 = 0.06881\); time short \(t_1+t_4=0.02202\).

    \[\text{Fraction of time short}=\frac{0.02202}{0.09083}=\mathbf{24.24\,\%}\]

    which equals \(\dfrac{C_h}{C_h+C_s}=\dfrac{8}{33}=0.2424\) ✓ — the critical ratio again, exactly as in Lesson 32.

    Interpretation. The firm operates a \(33\)-day cycle in which the machine runs for \(13.2\) days and customers wait for \(8.1\) days. Both are deliberate. Notice that the fraction of time short depends only on \(C_h\) and \(C_s\) — not on \(D\), \(C_o\) or \(P\). That makes it the single most robust number in the whole model and the natural thing to negotiate with a customer: "we will be out of stock about a quarter of the time; is that acceptable, and if not, what is it worth to you?"

    Problem 4 — Selecting the right model¶

    For each case, name the model and give the formula for \(Q^{*}\).

    Case Situation Model \(Q^{*}\)
    (a) Buy screws from a supplier; delivery next day; no stock-outs allowed EOQ \(\sqrt{2C_oD/C_h}\)
    (b) Buy custom furniture; customers accept a 2-week wait EOQ + backorders \(\sqrt{2C_oD/C_h}\ k\)
    (c) Make brackets on a press at 400/day, use 150/day, no stock-outs EPQ \(\sqrt{2C_oD/(C_hf)}\)
    (d) Make machine tools at 8/week, demand 5/week, dealers accept backorders General \(\sqrt{2C_oD/(C_hf)}\ k\)
    (e) Make ice cream at 500 l/day, demand 300 l/day, cannot be stored over a week None of these —

    Case (e) explained. Perishability imposes a maximum cycle length, which none of these models contains. If the unconstrained \(Q^{*}\) gives \(t=Q^{*}/D\) longer than the shelf life \(T_{\max}\), the answer is simply \(Q=DT_{\max}\) — the constraint binds and the optimisation is irrelevant. Because the cost curve is flat (Lesson 31), the penalty for this forced choice is usually small, which is a comforting result: a binding practical constraint rarely costs much in an EOQ-family model.

    Problem 5 — Sensitivity to C_s in the general model¶

    For Problem 1's data, tabulate the general policy as \(C_s\) varies from \(₹2\) to \(\infty\).

    Solution. \(f=0.6\) throughout, so \(Q^{*}_{\text{EPQ}}=790.57\) and \(TRC^{*}_{\text{EPQ}}=₹3794.73\) are the reference values.

    \(C_s\) \(k\) \(Q^{*}\) \(Q^{*}f\) \(I^{*}_{\max}\) \(B^{*}\) % of cycle short \(TRC^{*}\)
    2 2.2361 1767.77 1060.66 212.13 848.53 80.0 % ₹1697.06
    8 1.4142 1118.03 670.82 335.41 335.41 50.0 % ₹2683.28
    25 1.1489 908.30 544.98 412.86 132.12 24.2 % ₹3302.89
    72 1.0541 833.33 500.00 450.00 50.00 10.0 % ₹3600.00
    792 1.0050 794.55 476.73 471.96 4.77 1.0 % ₹3775.71
    \(\infty\) 1.0000 790.57 474.34 474.34 0 0 % ₹3794.73

    Interpretation.

    • \(I^{*}_{\max}\) rises and \(B^{*}\) falls as \(C_s\) increases, always summing to \(Q^{*}f\).
    • The percentage of the cycle spent short is exactly \(C_h/(C_h+C_s)\) — check the \(C_s=8\) row: with \(C_h=C_s=8\) the system is short exactly half the time, regardless of everything else.
    • As in Lesson 32, once \(C_s>10C_h\) (i.e. \(C_s>80\) here) the backorder refinement buys under \(5\,\%\) and is not worth the complexity. The general model earns its keep only when shortages are genuinely cheap.

    34.6 Practice Problems¶

    1. \(D=6000\)/yr, \(P=15{,}000\)/yr, \(C_o=₹300\), \(C_h=₹10\), \(C_s=₹40\). Find \(Q^{*}\), \(I^{*}_{\max}\), \(B^{*}\) and \(TRC^{*}\). (Answer: \(f=0.6\), \(k=1.1180\); \(Q^{*}=866.03\), \(I^{*}_{\max}=415.69\), \(B^{*}=103.92\), \(TRC^{*}=₹4156.92\))
    2. Show that \(I^{*}_{\max}+B^{*}=Q^{*}f\) always, and explain what that quantity means physically.
    3. In Problem 1 of §4, what would \(C_s\) have to be for the firm to be out of stock only \(5\,\%\) of the time? (Answer: \(C_h/(C_h+C_s)=0.05\) gives \(C_s=₹152\))
    4. Prove that \(TRC^{*}\) for the general model is never greater than \(TRC^{*}\) for any of the three special cases with the same \(D\), \(C_o\) and \(C_h\).
    5. A firm using the general model finds \(Q^{*}=1000\), \(I^{*}_{\max}=480\) and \(B^{*}=120\). Deduce \(f\) and \(C_s/C_h\). (Answer: \(Q^*f = 600\) so \(f=0.6\), i.e. \(D/P=0.4\); \(B/(I_{\max}+B)=0.2=C_h/(C_h+C_s)\) so \(C_s=4C_h\))

    34.7 Summary¶

    • The general model has a four-phase cycle: clear backlog, build stock, deplete stock, build backlog.
    • The peak-to-trough range is \(Qf\), and both averages divide by the full cycle: average stock \(=I_{\max}^2/(2Qf)\), average shortage \(=B^2/(2Qf)\).
    • Critical ratio unchanged: \(I^{*}_{\max}=\dfrac{C_s}{C_h+C_s}Qf\), and the fraction of the cycle spent short is \(\dfrac{C_h}{C_h+C_s}\) — independent of \(D\), \(C_o\) and \(P\).
    • \(\boxed{Q^{*}=\sqrt{\dfrac{2C_oD}{C_h}}\cdot\dfrac{k}{\sqrt f}}\) and \(\boxed{TRC^{*}=\sqrt{2C_oDC_h}\cdot\dfrac{\sqrt f}{k}}\) with \(f=1-D/P\), \(k=\sqrt{(C_h+C_s)/C_s}\).
    • The two corrections compose multiplicatively and each one raises \(Q\) while lowering \(TRC\).
    • Always check the limits \(P\to\infty\) and \(C_s\to\infty\): a general formula that does not collapse to its special cases is wrong.
    • The backorder refinement is worth having only when \(C_s\) is within about an order of magnitude of \(C_h\); beyond that, use the simpler model.
    • When a practical constraint (shelf life, storage space, minimum order) binds, take the constrained value — the flat cost curve makes the penalty small.
