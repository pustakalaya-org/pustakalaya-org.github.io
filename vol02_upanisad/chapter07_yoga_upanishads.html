    Chapter 7: The Yoga Upaniṣads¶

    7.1 Introduction: The Yoga Upaniṣads in Context¶

    Among the 108 Upaniṣads enumerated in the Muktikā canon, twenty are classified as Yoga Upaniṣads. While the mukhya Upaniṣads focus primarily on the metaphysical identity of Ātman and Brahman, the Yoga Upaniṣads give practical instructions on how that identity is to be realised through psycho-physical disciplines: posture, breath control, the subtle channels and centres of the body, inner sound and light, and meditative absorption. They thus represent the sādhana (practice) dimension of the Vedāntic teaching.

    7.1.1 Date and Composition¶

    It is essential to be clear about the date of these texts, because they are often presented as ancient sources of yoga. They are not. Apart from a few short and comparatively early works (such as the Amṛtabindu, Amṛtanāda, Kṣurikā and Tejobindu, which may go back to the first millennium CE), the Yoga Upaniṣads are medieval and early modern compositions. Their vocabulary — kuṇḍalinī, a system of cakras, the three bandhas, khecarī and vajrolī mudrā — is that of the Haṭha Yoga literature of the eleventh to fifteenth centuries, and of the Nātha tradition associated with Gorakṣanātha.

    The decisive study is Christian Bouy’s Les Nātha-yogin et les Upaniṣads (Bouy 1994). Bouy showed that the Yoga Upaniṣads survive in two forms: shorter versions, current in North India, and much longer versions transmitted in South India, which are the versions found in the 108-Upaniṣad collection and commented on by Upaniṣadbrahmayogin in the eighteenth century. The longer South Indian versions were expanded, probably in the seventeenth and early eighteenth centuries, by incorporating verses taken wholesale from Haṭha Yoga works such as the Haṭhayogapradīpikā (15th century), the Yogabīja, the Vivekamārtaṇḍa and the Khecarīvidyā; the second chapter of the Yogakuṇḍalī Upaniṣad, for example, is drawn from the Khecarīvidyā (Mallinson 2007). In other words, the direction of borrowing generally runs from the Haṭha texts to the Yoga Upaniṣads, not the reverse. Giving these teachings the name “Upaniṣad” brought Haṭha Yoga within the authority of the Veda and reconciled it with Advaita Vedānta, which is the doctrinal frame of most of these texts.

    None of this lessens the interest of the texts. They document how yogic practice and Vedāntic theology were brought together, and they preserve an important stage in the history of Indian contemplative techniques (Mallinson and Singleton 2017). But they should be read as late witnesses to the yoga traditions, not as their source, and as later than both Patañjali’s Yogasūtra (c. 325–425 CE) and the earliest Haṭha texts (Table 7.1).

    The practices that the Yoga Upaniṣads share with Haṭha Yoga — prāṇāyāma, the nāḍīs and cakras, Kuṇḍalinī, mudrās and bandhas — and Patañjali’s Yogasūtra are treated in detail in Volume 5 of this series, Yoga Taraṅgiṇī. This chapter summarises them only so far as is needed to show what the Yoga Upaniṣads themselves contribute and how they relate to the older Upaniṣads and to the yoga traditions.

    7.2 Key Themes of the Yoga Upaniṣads¶

    The Yoga Upaniṣads share a small set of themes, each taken up in the sections that follow: breath control (prāṇāyāma), the subtle channels and centres (nāḍīs and cakras), the coiled power (kuṇḍalinī), the inner sound (nāda), the bodily seals and locks (mudrās and bandhas), and meditation (dhyāna) leading to absorption (samādhi).

    7.3 The Aṣṭāṅga Yoga in the Yoga Upaniṣads¶

    Several Yoga Upaniṣads present their own versions of the eight-limbed (aṣṭāṅga) Yoga path — yama, niyama, āsana, prāṇāyāma, pratyāhāra, dhāraṇā, dhyāna and samādhi — which they take over from Patañjali’s formulation, often reinterpreting the limbs in Vedāntic terms (the Śāṇḍilya and Darśana, for example, list ten yamas and ten niyamas). Others give different numbers of limbs: the Amṛtanāda has six, the Tejobindu fifteen (Section 7.8).

    7.4 Prāṇāyāma, Nāḍīs and Cakras¶

    The Yoga Upaniṣads give extensive attention to prāṇāyāma (breath control), which they regard as the pivotal practice linking the gross body to the subtle mind. The Yoga-Tattva, Śāṇḍilya and Triśikhi-Brāhmaṇa Upaniṣads give the most detailed instructions. Each round has three phases — inhalation (pūraka), retention (kumbhaka), regarded as the most important, and exhalation (recaka) — and in the purification of the channels (nāḍī-śodhana) the breath is drawn in through one nostril and let out through the other, alternately. The texts prescribe proportions such as 1:4:2 for the three phases and grade practice by its bodily signs: perspiration at the lowest level, trembling at the middle, and at the highest a “rising” (utthāna) of the body, which is a traditional claim.

    Health and Safety Note

    This chapter describes practices as the texts present them; it is not a manual. Prolonged breath retention (kumbhaka), forceful breathing, inverted postures and techniques such as khecarī (which traditionally involves cutting the frenulum of the tongue) and vajrolī can cause injury and are unsuitable for people with heart disease, high blood pressure, epilepsy, glaucoma, respiratory illness or pregnancy. The Haṭha texts themselves warn that breath control practised wrongly produces disease (Haṭhayogapradīpikā 2.16). The texts’ claims that these practices cure illness, conquer ageing or produce levitation are traditional claims, not established facts. Anyone wishing to practise should seek qualified instruction and medical advice.

    Modern Relevance: Breath Control and Modern Research

    Laboratory studies of slow, regular breathing report measurable effects on heart-rate variability, autonomic balance and subjective calm; a systematic review is given by Zaccaro et al. (2018). Such findings are consistent with the texts’ general claim that breath control “steadies the mind”, but they do not validate the specific ratios, the subtle-body physiology, or the claims of longevity and supernatural powers, and many of the underlying studies are small.

    7.4.1 The Nāḍīs¶

    The Yoga Upaniṣads describe a system of subtle channels (nāḍīs) and centres (cakras) within the body. This is a map of the body as experienced in meditation and ritual (the “subtle body”), not an anatomical description, and the individual texts do not agree with one another on the number, location or names of the centres. The figure of 72,000 channels goes back to the Bṛhadāraṇyaka (2.1.19), where they are the paths of the self in sleep. The Yoga Upaniṣads adopt it and single out three channels as of paramount importance (Table 7.2).

    Table 7.2. The Three Principal Nāḍīs
    Nāḍī Position Quality Function
    Iḍā Left side Lunar, cooling Associated with the left nostril and the moon
    Piṅgalā Right side Solar, heating Associated with the right nostril and the sun
    Suṣumṇā Central — The channel through which breath and Kuṇḍalinī rise in yogic practice

    7.4.2 The Cakras¶

    The scheme of six cakras along the central channel — mūlādhāra, svādhiṣṭhāna, maṇipūra, anāhata, viśuddha and ājñā, with the thousand-petalled sahasrāra above — that is familiar today is only one of several. It was popularised by Pūrṇānanda’s Ṣaṭcakranirūpaṇa (1577) and, in the West, by Arthur Avalon’s The Serpent Power (1919); the Yoga Upaniṣads themselves give varying lists.

    7.5 Kuṇḍalinī: The Coiled Power¶

    The concept of Kuṇḍalinī — the dormant power coiled at the base of the spine — entered yoga from the Śaiva and Śākta Tantras and the Haṭha texts, and is a central theme in several Yoga Upaniṣads, particularly the Yoga-Kuṇḍalī, the Yoga-Cūḍāmaṇi, the Yoga-Śikhā, the Yoga-Tattva and the Darśana. It does not occur in the principal Upaniṣads. As these texts describe it, the process of awakening involves:

    1. Purification of the nāḍīs through prāṇāyāma

    2. Arousal of the dormant energy through specific practices (bandhas, mudrās, mantras)

    3. Gradual ascent of the energy through the suṣumṇā nāḍī, piercing each cakra in succession

    4. Union with Śiva at the sahasrāra

    Several texts add that the rising power must pierce three “knots” (granthis) named after Brahmā, Viṣṇu and Rudra. Their locations differ from text to text (the Haṭhayogapradīpikā, 4.69–77, connects them with stages of inner sound at the heart, the throat and between the eyebrows); modern teachers often explain them psychologically as attachment to the body, to persons and to the sense of doership, but that interpretation is modern.

    The Yoga Upaniṣads insist that these practices must be learned from a qualified teacher and not from textual study alone, and they warn of physical and mental disturbance when they are practised prematurely or wrongly. In this they continue the older Upaniṣadic view that the highest teaching is transmitted personally, from teacher to pupil (Section 1.15).

    7.6 Nāda Yoga: The Yoga of Inner Sound¶

    The Nāda-Bindu Upaniṣad and the Dhyāna-Bindu Upaniṣad describe a distinctive meditative practice based on the perception of inner sounds (nāda). The practitioner, seated in a specific posture with the ears closed (using the ṣaṇmukhī mudrā), progressively hears subtler and subtler sounds — from the roaring of an ocean to the sound of a bell, a flute, a lute, the buzzing of a bee, and finally the subtlest sound of all, which leads to absorption in the Absolute (Table 7.3).

    Table 7.3. Progressive Stages of Nāda Perception (a composite summary; the texts differ in the number and order of the sounds)
    Stage Sound Perceived Significance
    1 Ocean / Thunder Gross stage; initial practice
    2 Drum / Waterfall Attention begins to stabilise
    3 Bell / Conch Intermediate stage; mind becomes absorbed
    4 Flute / Vīṇā Subtle stage; deep concentration
    5 Bee / Humming Very subtle; approaching samādhi
    6 Silence / Unstruck Sound The anāhata nāda; absorption in Brahman

    7.7 The Haṃsa Upaniṣad: The Swan of the Self¶

    The Haṃsa Upaniṣad presents one of the most elegant teachings in the Yoga Upaniṣadic corpus. The word haṃsa (swan, or more precisely, the bar-headed goose) is traditionally explained as the natural sound of breathing: ha on exhalation and sa on inhalation. Reversed, it gives so ’ham, “I am He” (saḥ aham). Texts such as the Yogacūḍāmaṇi add that every being thus repeats this mantra 21,600 times in a day and a night — a traditional figure, not a physiological measurement.

    This is called the Ajapā Gāyatrī — the “un-chanted” Gāyatrī — because it occurs naturally with every breath, whether one is aware of it or not. The practice of Haṃsa Yoga consists in becoming conscious of this natural mantra and thereby transforming every breath into an act of Self-remembrance.

    7.8 The Tejo-Bindu Upaniṣad: The Fifteen-limbed Yoga¶

    The Tejo-Bindu Upaniṣad presents a fifteen-limbed Yoga, going far beyond the standard eight limbs. The additional limbs include subtle practices aimed at the dissolution of the mind (mano-nāśa) and the exhaustion of latent tendencies (vāsanā-kṣaya). The fifteen are: (1) yama, (2) niyama, (3) tyāga (renunciation), (4) mauna (silence), (5) deśa (place), (6) kāla (time), (7) āsana, (8) mūlabandha, (9) dehasāmya (bodily equipoise), (10) dṛksthiti (steadiness of gaze), (11) prāṇasaṃyamana (breath control), (12) pratyāhāra, (13) dhāraṇā, (14) ātma-dhyāna (meditation on the Self) and (15) samādhi.

    7.9 Mudrās and Bandhas¶

    The Yoga Upaniṣads describe a number of mudrās (seals) and bandhas (locks) that are considered essential for the arousal and control of the breath and the subtle energies. They are the same as those of the Haṭha texts: khecarī mudrā, in which the tongue, lengthened by gradual cutting of the frenulum, is turned back into the cavity above the palate; mahāmudrā; the inverted viparītakaraṇī, said to reverse the downward flow of the nectar (amṛta); vajrolī, a technique of urethral suction intended to retain bindu (semen); and the three locks — mūla bandha (contraction of the perineum), uḍḍīyāna bandha (drawing the abdomen in and up) and jālandhara bandha (chin pressed to the chest). The Yoga-Cūḍāmaṇi Upaniṣad states that when the three bandhas are applied together the breath is forced into the suṣumṇā, where it begins to ascend and awakens Kuṇḍalinī. The claims made for these practices (conquest of ageing, longevity) are traditional; see the safety note above, and Volume 5 for the techniques.

    7.10 The Advaya-Tāraka Upaniṣad: Tāraka Yoga¶

    The Advaya-Tāraka Upaniṣad presents a distinctive form of yoga called Tāraka Yoga — the “Yoga of the Deliverer” — which centres on the perception of inner light phenomena (jyotis). The text describes three types of inner vision:

    1. Antar-lakṣya (Internal Vision): Perception of coloured lights, geometric forms, and luminous patterns within the body during meditation with closed eyes

    2. Bahir-lakṣya (External Vision): Perception of subtle lights in the space before the eyes — blue, golden, and white radiances perceived with partially open eyes

    3. Madhya-lakṣya (Intermediate Vision): The vision of the bhrūmadhya (inter-brow centre), where the concentrated gaze reveals progressively subtler luminous phenomena

    The text declares that through sustained practice of Tāraka Yoga, the practitioner perceives the advaya (non-dual) nature of reality and attains liberation even while living (jīvanmukti).

    Tāraka as “That Which Delivers”

    The word tāraka derives from the root tṛ (to cross over, to deliver). Tāraka Yoga is thus the “yoga that delivers” the practitioner across the ocean of saṃsāra. The inner light perceived in this practice is understood not as a physiological phenomenon but as the self-luminous radiance of the Ātman itself, becoming progressively visible as the veils of ignorance are thinned through practice.

    7.11 The Varāha Upaniṣad: Tattva-Jñāna through Yoga¶

    The Varāha Upaniṣad presents a comprehensive synthesis of Yoga and Vedānta. It describes the nature of the five elements (pañca-bhūtas), the nature of the jīva (individual soul), and the method of liberation through combined practice of Haṭha Yoga and Jñāna Yoga. Unusually for a Yoga Upaniṣad, the Varāha gives an extended treatment of the tattvas (principles of reality) and their transcendence through meditative insight.

    The text describes the person who has attained tattva-jñāna (knowledge of reality) through yogic practice in terms familiar from the Gītā’s portrait of the person of steady wisdom; in summary:

    • Equanimity in pleasure and pain, honour and dishonour

    • Freedom from attachment to the fruits of action

    • Spontaneous compassion for all beings

    • Absence of ego-identification with the body

    • Natural abidance in the awareness “I am the Ātman”

    • Indifference to worldly acquisitions and conventional religious observances

    jñātvā devaṃ sarvapāśāpāhāniḥ kṣīṇaiḥ kleśair janmamṛtyuprahāṇiḥ
    Translation: “Knowing the Lord, all fetters fall away, afflictions are destroyed, and birth and death cease.”
    — Śvetāśvatara Upaniṣad 1.11, an older verse expressing the ideal the Yoga Upaniṣads take up

    7.12 The Relationship Between Yoga Upaniṣads and Patañjali¶

    The Yoga Upaniṣads are later than Patañjali’s Yogasūtra and borrow its eight limbs, but they differ from it significantly (Table 7.4).

    Table 7.4. Yoga Upaniṣads vs. Patañjali’s Yoga Sūtras
    Aspect Yoga Upaniṣads Patañjali’s Yoga Sūtras
    Date Mostly medieval to early modern (expanded versions c. 17th–18th century) c. 325–425 CE
    Philosophical base Vedāntic (Brahman = Ātman) Sāṅkhya-Yoga (Puruṣa \(\neq\) Prakṛti)
    Goal Realisation of Brahman Kaivalya (isolation of Puruṣa)
    Kuṇḍalinī Central concept Not mentioned
    Nāda Yoga Extensively described Not mentioned
    Cakras Detailed description Not mentioned
    Emphasis Haṭha practice combined with meditation and Vedāntic knowledge Eight-limbed meditative discipline (later called Rāja Yoga)

    7.13 Samādhi: The Goal of Yogic Practice¶

    The Yoga Upaniṣads describe samādhi (complete absorption) as the culmination of all yogic practice. Following Advaita usage (rather than Patañjali’s distinction between samprajñāta and asamprajñāta samādhi), they distinguish two types (Table 7.5).

    Table 7.5. Two Types of Samādhi in the Yoga Upaniṣads
    Aspect Savikalpa Samādhi Nirvikalpa Samādhi
    Nature Absorption with a subtle trace of subject-object distinction Complete absorption without any distinction whatsoever
    Content Awareness of “I am meditating on Brahman” No awareness of meditation; only Brahman
    Duration Temporary; one returns to normal consciousness May be temporary or permanent (sahaja)
    Result Purification; strengthening of conviction Direct knowledge (aparokṣa jñāna); liberation

    The Maṇḍala-Brāhmaṇa Upaniṣad describes the highest samādhi in terms that echo the Māṇḍūkya’s description of Turīya: it is beyond waking, dream, and deep sleep; it is self-luminous, non-dual, and blissful. The practitioner who attains this state is described as jīvanmukta — liberated while still living in the body.

    7.14 The Yoga Upaniṣads and Śaṅkara’s Advaita¶

    The relationship between the Yoga Upaniṣads and Śaṅkara’s Advaita Vedānta is complex. Śaṅkara himself emphasised jñāna (knowledge) as the sole direct means of liberation and treated Yoga as a preparatory discipline (sādhana) for purifying the mind. The Yoga Upaniṣads, while not contradicting this position, give far greater independent weight to Yogic practice, sometimes suggesting that Samādhi itself is the means of liberation rather than merely a preparatory stage.

    Key Insight

    The Yoga Upaniṣads serve as a bridge between the philosophical Upaniṣads and the practical yoga traditions of India. They present the Upaniṣadic vision of Ātman-Brahman identity not as a theoretical proposition but as a state to be realised through āsana, prāṇāyāma, pratyāhāra, dhāraṇā, dhyāna and samādhi — a synthesis that, as Section 7.1.1 showed, was achieved largely in the medieval and early modern periods.

    7.15 The Haṭha Yoga Tradition and Its Upaniṣadic Roots¶

    7.15.1 From Upaniṣadic Yoga to Haṭha Yoga¶

    The relationship between the Yoga Upaniṣads and the Haṭha Yoga tradition is one of close textual continuity — but, as explained in Section 7.1.1, the Haṭha texts generally come first. The Haṭhayogapradīpikā of Svātmārāma (15th century) compiles material from earlier Haṭha works; the expanded South Indian Yoga Upaniṣads in turn quote the Pradīpikā and its sources, often verbatim. The Gheraṇḍa Saṃhitā (c. 17th–18th century) and the Śiva Saṃhitā belong to the same late period.

    The parallels listed below are therefore best read as evidence of Haṭha material being absorbed into Upaniṣadic form. Together, these texts show that later Indian traditions came to treat the trained and purified body as an instrument of liberation rather than simply an obstacle to it (Table 7.6).

    Table 7.6. Parallels Between the Yoga Upaniṣads and the Haṭhayogapradīpikā
    Practice Yoga Upaniṣad Parallel in the Haṭhayogapradīpikā
    Khecarī Mudrā Yoga-Cūḍāmaṇi, Yoga-Kuṇḍalī Haṭha-Yoga-Pradīpikā 3.32–54
    Nāḍī Śodhana Śāṇḍilya, Yoga-Tattva Haṭha-Yoga-Pradīpikā 2.7–10
    Kuṇḍalinī awakening Yoga-Kuṇḍalī, Yoga-Śikhā Haṭha-Yoga-Pradīpikā 3.1–5
    Tri-bandha Yoga-Cūḍāmaṇi Haṭha-Yoga-Pradīpikā 3.55–76
    Nāda meditation Nāda-Bindu, Dhyāna-Bindu Haṭha-Yoga-Pradīpikā 4.64–102
    Samādhi descriptions Maṇḍala-Brāhmaṇa Haṭha-Yoga-Pradīpikā 4.1–16
