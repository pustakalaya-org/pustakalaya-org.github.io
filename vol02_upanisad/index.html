    Preface¶

    This is Volume 2 of the series Bhāratīya Jñāna Paramparā: Upaniṣad Dīpikā: The Upaniṣads. It introduces the Upaniṣads — the speculative texts that close the Vedic corpus — and the traditions of thought that grew from them.

    The first part of the book (Chapters 1–4) introduces the corpus and the evidence for its relative chronology, and then reads the principal Upaniṣads one by one, from the Īśā to the Śvetāśvatara, with the Kauṣītaki and the Maitrī, with the ritual meditations that fill much of the oldest texts, and with the kings, brahmins and patrons among whom they were first taught. Chapters 5, 6 and 10 treat the great sayings (mahāvākyas), the central doctrines of Brahman, Ātman, creation, māyā, karma and liberation (with the Buddhist and Jain critiques of the Upaniṣadic self), and the analysis of waking, dream, deep sleep and the “fourth” state. Chapters 7–9 survey the later Yoga, Sannyāsa, sectarian and general Vedāntic Upaniṣads. Chapter 11 begins with the Brahmasūtra and the Vedānta before Śaṅkara, introduces the great commentators — Śaṅkara, Rāmānuja, Madhva and others — and follows the Upaniṣads into Tamil, where the Śrīvaiṣṇavas called Nammāḻvār’s hymns a “Tamil Upaniṣad” and the Śaiva Siddhānta read the Upaniṣads through the Āgamas, and into the regional languages. Chapter 12 treats the modern reception of the Upaniṣads in India and the world, beginning with their passage to Europe through Persian and Latin.

    Three principles have guided this edition. First, dates: the mainstream academic dating is given first (for example, c. 7th–6th century BCE for the oldest prose Upaniṣads), and traditional dates are labelled as such; the reader is told plainly that most of the 108 Upaniṣads of the Muktikā list, including the Yoga Upaniṣads, are medieval or early modern. Second, the texts and their interpreters are kept apart: much that is popularly presented as “the teaching of the Upaniṣads” is in fact the systematisation of a later school, usually Advaita Vedānta, and the theistic schools read the same texts differently. Third, honesty about claims and contexts: Sanskrit quotations have been checked and corrected or removed, claims that the Upaniṣads anticipated modern science have been withdrawn, and the social limits of the tradition — including the restriction of Vedic learning by caste and gender — are stated rather than passed over.

    The Upaniṣads need no exaggeration. Read carefully, they remain among the most searching enquiries ever made into the self, reality and freedom. This volume is offered as a lamp (dīpikā) for that reading. A note on texts and references follows; a glossary and a chronology close the volume.

    Nikhil Kumar Rajput, Ramanujan College, University of Delhi
