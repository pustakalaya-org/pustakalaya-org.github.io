    Preface¶

    This is Volume 3 of the series Bhāratīya Jñāna Paramparā: Darśana Darpaṇa: Schools of Indian Philosophy. It introduces the classical schools of Indian philosophy — Nyāya, Vaiśeṣika, Sāṃkhya, Yoga, Pūrva Mīmāṃsā and Vedānta, together with the philosophy of the Grammarians — and the traditions that Brahmanical authors called nāstika: the materialist Cārvāka, and Buddhist and Jain philosophy. A chapter on Śaiva philosophy — the dualist Śaiva Siddhānta and the non-dualist school of Recognition (pratyabhijñā) — follows, with an account of its Tamil form, and a final chapter compares the schools’ theories of knowledge (pramāṇa), error and debate and draws the volume’s conclusions.

    The book tries to present each school on its own terms and in its own best arguments, and to give the reader a sense of the long debate among them. Dates are given according to mainstream academic scholarship, with traditional dates identified as such. Quotations from primary texts are limited to passages whose wording and reference could be checked; claims that ancient texts anticipated modern science have been avoided, and comparisons with modern philosophy are offered as analogies. The social setting of philosophy, including restrictions of learning by caste and gender, is noted where it bears on the subject.

    This edition has been revised to correct errors found in an earlier draft, and the Buddhist and Jain chapters in particular have been expanded. Later additions are the chapter on Śaiva philosophy and accounts of Advaita after Śaṅkara, of the method of Navya-Nyāya and its early-modern successors, and of the theory of debate; in the latest revision, sections that treated the same topic twice were merged, the arguments of the Cārvākas and the Vaiśeṣikas were set out more fully, and the volume’s omissions — above all Indo-Islamic thought and much regional-language philosophy — are stated in the Introduction. It remains an introduction: specialists will find simplifications, and every primary-text quotation should be checked against a critical edition; the editions used are listed at the end, with a chronology and a guide to further reading.

    Transliteration and abbreviations. Sanskrit, Pali and Prakrit are given in the International Alphabet of Sanskrit Transliteration (IAST): long vowels are marked with a macron (ā, ī, ū), retroflex consonants with a dot below (ṭ, ḍ, ṇ, ṣ), the palatal sibilant as ś, anusvāra as ṃ and visarga as ḥ; vocalic r is ṛ. Tamil follows the transliteration of the Tamil Lexicon (so ḻ, ṉ, ṟ). Names of modern authors and places are given in their usual English spelling. References to primary texts are by chapter and verse or sūtra; in a few tables NS, VS, MS and ṚV stand for the Nyāya Sūtra, Vaiśeṣika Sūtra, Mīmāṃsā Sūtra and Ṛgveda. Dates are CE unless marked BCE.

    Nikhil Kumar Rajput
    Ramanujan College, University of Delhi
