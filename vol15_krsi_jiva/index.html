    Preface¶

    This is Volume 15 of the series Bhāratīya Jñāna Paramparā: Kṛṣi and Jīva Vijñāna: Agriculture, Plants and Animals. It is about the knowledge with which the people of the subcontinent fed themselves and lived alongside plants and animals: how fields were prepared, watered and manured, how rain was forecast, which crops were grown and where they came from, how trees and gardens were tended, how plants were named and classified, how cattle, buffaloes, sheep, goats, camels, elephants and horses were bred, kept and treated, and how birds, insects and fishes were observed and used.

    Two kinds of evidence run through the book. The first is textual: Vedic hymns, the Arthaśāstra, the Bṛhatsaṃhitā of Varāhamihira, the medical saṃhitās, and a small group of specialised treatises — the Kṛṣi-Parāśara, Surapāla’s Vṛkṣāyurveda, the Upavanavinoda, the Kāśyapīya Kṛṣisūkti, Pālakāpya’s Hastyāyurveda, the horse books ascribed to Śālihotra, Someśvara’s Mānasollāsa — together with Tamil poetry, Persian chronicles and inscriptions. The second is the evidence of practice: archaeobotany and zooarchaeology, surviving irrigation works, farmers’ and herders’ vocabularies, and the observations of travellers and colonial officials. The texts are precious but partial. Most were written by and for elites, many are late, and none of them records the full knowledge of the cultivators, labourers, pastoralists and fisherfolk who did the work; the book tries to keep both in view, and to be honest about caste, labour and gender in Indian agriculture.

    This volume combines and corrects two earlier drafts, one centred on the Kṛṣi-Parāśara and agriculture, the other on plant and animal science. In the course of revision the editors removed a great deal: verses that could not be found in the texts they were ascribed to, invented tables and “modern validations”, statistics without a source, and claims that ancient texts anticipated modern science. Where a quotation is given, it is one the editors believe to be genuine; where a traditional claim is reported, it is reported as a claim. Dates are given as the range accepted in current scholarship, with traditional dates labelled as such. Several texts discussed here — among them the Kṛṣi-Parāśara, which is not the work of the Vedic sage, and the Mṛgapakṣiśāstra — are later or less secure than popular accounts suggest, and the book says so.

    Nothing in this volume is advice on the treatment of people or animals. Medicinal plants are treated here as plants; their use in medicine is the subject of Volume 13 (Āyurveda), and readers are referred there and to qualified practitioners. References to editions, translations and studies are given in the text and collected in the select bibliography; a glossary and a chronology of the principal texts are provided at the end.

    Nikhil Kumar Rajput
    Ramanujan College, University of Delhi
    Guru Pūrṇimā, 2026
