    Chapter 4: Vṛkṣāyurveda: The Science of the Life of Trees¶

    Vṛkṣāyurveda, “the science of the life of trees”, is the Sanskrit name for a body of knowledge about growing and tending trees, shrubs, creepers and garden plants. Its name and much of its method come from medicine (āyurveda). Plants are treated as living bodies that must be nourished, can fall ill, and can be cured. The tradition is represented by a single chapter in an encyclopaedia, a few chapters in the Purāṇas, one surviving specialised treatise, sections of medieval encyclopaedias and anthologies, and a late compilation. Its literature is therefore small. This chapter describes the texts, the ideas about plant life on which they rest, and the main topics they treat. It deals separately with the fermented liquid manure called kuṇapajala, with the literary and horticultural notion of plant “cravings” (dohada), and with the marvels the texts claim to produce. What the gardens produced, and what Indian culture made of their flowers, scents and betel, is the subject of Chapter 5. Plant classification is treated more fully in Chapter 6, and medicinal plants in Chapter 12 and in Volume 13, Āyurveda.

    The word itself is old. The Arthaśāstra (2.24.1) requires the superintendent of agriculture to know the science of agriculture and the science of plant life (vṛkṣāyurveda), or to be assisted by people who do (Section 2.11.1). Nothing survives of any treatise of that period, but the term shows that a recognised body of knowledge about the care of plants existed by the early centuries CE.

    4.1 The Texts¶

    The principal sources are the Bṛhatsaṃhitā (ch. 55), the Agni Purāṇa, Surapāla, the Lokopakāra, the Mānasollāsa, the Upavanavinoda and the Viśvavallabha; their approximate dates are given in Tables 1.1 and 1.2 (Chapter 1). For several of them the dates are disputed.

    4.1.1 Bṛhatsaṃhitā, chapter 55¶

    The oldest surviving systematic account is chapter 55 of Varāhamihira’s Bṛhatsaṃhitā (6th century), titled Vṛkṣāyurveda (Bhat 1981). It is short, but most of the topics of the later texts are already present in it. It recommends planting trees by houses, gardens and water bodies, and describes the preparation of the soil. It lists trees that can be raised from cuttings, gives seasons for transplanting, and prescribes the spacing of trees and a watering schedule. It then sets out the symptoms of disease and their treatment, ways of making a barren tree bear, and treatments of seeds before sowing (Section 4.3). Varāhamihira’s chapter is the model on which several later texts drew, directly or indirectly. For the Bṛhatsaṃhitā more generally, and for its rain-forecasting chapters, see Chapter 3; for chapter 54 on finding groundwater, see Chapter 8.

    4.1.2 The Agni Purāṇa and other Purāṇas¶

    The Agni Purāṇa, an encyclopaedic Purāṇa of uncertain and composite date, contains a short chapter on the care of trees. Much of it parallels the Bṛhatsaṃhitā. Chapter numbers differ between editions and are not given here. Several Purāṇas also praise the planting of trees as a meritorious act and prescribe rites for consecrating new gardens and trees. A widely quoted verse of the Matsya Purāṇa (chapter 154 in the usual numbering, within the story of Pārvatī and Śiva), spoken by the goddess Pārvatī when she adopts an aśoka sapling as her son, ranks a single tree as the equal of ten sons. Such passages tell us more about the religious value placed on tree-planting than about techniques. They help explain why wealthy patrons founded gardens and groves, and why treatises on their care were written (see also Chapter 11).

    4.1.3 Planting as merit and as policy¶

    The planting of useful trees was also an act of public policy long before any surviving treatise: Aśoka’s second Rock Edict and seventh Pillar Edict record the planting of medicinal herbs, roadside banyans and mango groves (Chapter 11). Later kings, queens, merchants and temple authorities continued the practice, and inscriptions of many periods record the gift of gardens, groves and roadside trees together with tanks and wells. The vṛkṣāyurveda texts served people who undertook such works. Their praise of tree-planting as a source of religious merit, which can seem conventional, reflects a real social institution.

    Folk practice carried the same values in other forms. In many parts of India a pīpal (aśvattha, Ficus religiosa) and a neem (Azadirachta indica) are planted side by side and their union is celebrated as a “marriage” of the two trees. The tulasī plant is ceremonially married to Viṣṇu in the annual tulasī-vivāha. Such customs express the idea that trees are persons with kinship and life-cycles of their own.

    4.1.4 Surapāla’s Vṛkṣāyurveda¶

    The only surviving independent treatise of any length is the Vṛkṣāyurveda of Surapāla, edited and translated by Nalini Sadhale as the first of the Agri-History Bulletins of the Asian Agri-History Foundation (Sadhale 1996). Surapāla describes himself as a physician (vaidya) in the service of a king named Bhīmapāla. This king has not been securely identified, and the text is usually dated, with some uncertainty, to the tenth or eleventh century CE. A Pāla connection in eastern India has been suggested but is not proven. The text is a verse work of a few hundred stanzas. Its standpoint is a physician’s. It covers the praise of trees and the merit of planting them, the choice of land, the classification of plants, seeds and their treatment, planting, watering, nourishment, the protection of plants from external harm, the diagnosis and cure of their diseases, a series of “marvels”, and the layout of gardens.

    Two features make Surapāla’s text especially important. First, it shows how thoroughly the categories of human medicine were carried over to plants, as discussed below. Second, it contains the classic account of kuṇapajala (Section 4.3.6). What it does not contain is listed in Section 1.5; for its contents the reader should rely on Sadhale’s translation.

    4.1.5 The Lokopakāra and the Mānasollāsa¶

    Two encyclopaedic works of the eleventh and twelfth centuries, described in Section 3.8, include plant-care sections in the vṛkṣāyurveda manner. The Kannada Lokopakāra of Cāvuṇḍarāya (c. 1025 CE) gives one of its chapters to the care of trees and garden plants, which its English translation makes accessible (Ayangarya 2006). The chapter follows the order already familiar from Varāhamihira: the choice and preparation of the ground, the trees auspicious near a house, the treatment of seeds and cuttings, planting, watering by season, nourishing applications of dung, oil-cake, milk and similar substances, the protection of trees against insects and disease by pastes and fumigation, and procedures for making trees flower or fruit out of season or bear fruit of unusual size, colour or taste. Much of this is shared with Surapāla, and it is not known whether either author drew on the other or both on a common source. The Lokopakāra is the earliest surviving treatment of the subject in a Dravidian language, and it carries the knowledge of the Sanskrit garden texts to readers who did not read Sanskrit. Its terms for plants and operations are partly Kannada, which makes it a valuable witness to the vocabulary of gardeners in the Deccan. The Mānasollāsa of Someśvara III (1131 CE) treats the laying out and tending of a garden among the king’s enjoyments (Shrigondekar 1925).

    4.1.6 The Upavanavinoda¶

    The Upavanavinoda, “Delight of the garden”, is a section on arbori-horticulture in the Śārṅgadharapaddhati, an anthology of Sanskrit verse on many subjects compiled by Śārṅgadhara in 1363 CE. This Śārṅgadhara is not the author of the medical Śārṅgadhara-saṃhitā. G. P. Majumdar edited and translated the section in 1935, with notes on the plants named (Majumdar 1935). The Upavanavinoda covers much the same ground as Surapāla, from the praise of trees and the choice of soil to planting, watering, nourishment, diseases and marvels. The two texts share a good deal of material, including a description of kuṇapajala. As an anthology section, it gathers older verses rather than presenting the views of a single author.

    4.1.7 The Viśvavallabha¶

    The Viśvavallabha of Cakrapāṇi Miśra, composed in Mewar in the later sixteenth century, is a late compilation; its author is associated with the court of Mahārāṇā Pratāp, and Nalini Sadhale has translated it (Sadhale 2004). It is a short verse work in a sequence of chapters that moves from water to plants. It begins, as Varāhamihira’s dakārgala does, with the signs of underground water — trees, termite mounds, soils and stones — and with the digging of wells and tanks (Section 8.10). It then turns to the garden: the kinds of land, the choice of trees for particular places, the treatment of seeds before sowing, planting and the spacing of trees, watering, manures and nourishing liquids, the protection of plants from pests and disease, and a closing set of marvels like those of Surapāla. Much of the content closely parallels the Bṛhatsaṃhitā and the older vṛkṣāyurveda material, so that the work is most useful as evidence of what a scholar at a Rajput court in the sixteenth century thought worth keeping from that tradition, and of its continued practical interest in an arid kingdom where wells and orchards mattered greatly. Its author should not be confused with Cakrapāṇidatta, the eleventh-century Bengali commentator on the Caraka-saṃhitā, and no separate vṛkṣāyurveda by a “Cakrapāṇi” is known.

    4.1.8 The disputed “Vṛkṣāyurveda of Parāśara”¶

    A work entitled Vṛkṣāyurveda and attributed to Parāśara has been published in modern editions. It is often presented as the foundational text of Indian botany, dated to the early centuries CE or earlier, and credited with an advanced classification of plants and with descriptions of the internal structure of leaves. These claims cannot be accepted as they stand.

    Debate: The “Parāśara” Vṛkṣāyurveda

    The claim: an ancient sage Parāśara composed a botanical treatise that classified plants by their flowers and described the internal anatomy of leaves, anticipating modern botany.

    The difficulties: the manuscript basis of the published text has not been set out in a critical edition that other scholars can check; its date is unknown; its technical vocabulary and some of its descriptions have no parallel in the securely dated texts; and the name Parāśara was attached to several works of very different periods, including the medieval Kṛṣi-Parāśara (Chapter 3).

    Assessment: until a proper critical study is available, the work should be treated as of uncertain authenticity and date. It should not be cited as evidence for ancient Indian botanical knowledge, and statements that it “anticipated Linnaeus” or described cells are unsupported.

    4.2 Plants as Living Beings¶

    The vṛkṣāyurveda texts rest on a premise widely shared in Indian thought: plants are living beings (jīva) with some form of awareness. This premise appears in several traditions with different emphases.

    4.2.1 Brahmanical sources¶

    The Manusmṛti, in its account of creation, lists the kinds of plants and then says of them:

    Manusmṛti 1.49

    tamasā bahurūpeṇa veṣṭitāḥ karmahetunā
    antaḥsaṃjñā bhavanty ete sukhaduḥkhasamanvitāḥ
    “Enveloped by darkness of many forms, the result of their past actions, these beings have inner awareness and experience pleasure and pain.”

    The verse places plants at the bottom of a hierarchy of rebirth. They are beings born into a dull, “dark” form as a result of past deeds, yet still conscious and capable of pleasure and pain (Olivelle 2005). The surrounding verses (1.46–48) give one of the early Sanskrit classifications of plants, discussed in Chapter 6.

    The Mahābhārata’s dialogue between Bhṛgu and Bharadvāja (12.177 in the critical edition), in which Bhṛgu infers the five senses of trees from their behaviour, makes the same point in argument. Jain thought classes plants as one-sensed living beings (ekendriya) (Tatia 1994), and the Buddhist monastic code forbids monks to destroy plant life because lay people held plants to be alive. These views are discussed in Chapter 6.

    4.2.2 Āyurveda and the vṛkṣāyurveda texts¶

    The medical texts classify plants by form, habitat and use, and they treat plants as bodies made of the five elements (pañcabhūta), with tastes (rasa) and qualities that affect the human body (see Chapter 6 and Volume 13). The vṛkṣāyurveda authors went a step further and treated the plant itself as a patient. Its body is governed, like a human body, by the three doṣas, vāta, pitta and kapha, and it can be diagnosed and treated accordingly. In Surapāla this is a practical framework, not a metaphysical claim (Sadhale 1996).

    4.2.3 Modern plant physiology: J. C. Bose¶

    In the early twentieth century the physicist Jagadis Chunder Bose (1858–1937) turned from research on electromagnetic waves to plant physiology. Using sensitive instruments of his own design, he recorded electrical responses of plant tissues to mechanical, electrical, thermal and chemical stimuli. He argued that these responses resembled those of animal nerve and muscle. His Plant Response as a Means of Physiological Investigation (1906) set out these results (Bose 1906), and later works described further instruments such as the crescograph for recording growth. Bose was open about his conviction that living and non-living matter, plants and animals, form a continuum. That conviction drew on his own cultural background as well as on his experiments.

    Bose’s work was controversial in his lifetime, and some of his interpretations were not accepted. His central observation, that plants generate and propagate electrical signals, is now part of mainstream plant physiology. Action potentials in sensitive plants and in the Venus fly-trap are well documented, and electrical and chemical signalling within plants is an active field of research. None of this shows that plants have minds or feelings in the human sense. Nor does it vindicate the claims, popular since the 1970s, that plants respond to human emotions, prayers, mantras or music. Those claims have not been established by reliable experiment. The vṛkṣāyurveda texts should be read as belonging to their own philosophical world, not as anticipations of modern plant physiology.

    4.3 The Topics of the Texts¶

    The following account draws chiefly on Bṛhatsaṃhitā 55 (Bhat 1981), Surapāla (Sadhale 1996) and the Upavanavinoda (Majumdar 1935). Details are given only where they are secure. The texts are not identical, and a statement made in one should not be assumed to hold for all.

    4.3.1 Classes of plants¶

    Surapāla, following earlier usage, divides plants into four classes. Vanaspati are trees that bear fruit without visible flowers, such as the figs. Druma are trees with flowers and fruit. Latā are creepers, and gulma are shrubs (Sadhale 1996). The scheme is practical: it groups plants by habit, which determines how they are planted and tended. The fig family’s lack of visible flowers is a correct observation, since fig flowers are enclosed within the fruit-like syconium. But there is nothing in the text to suggest that its authors understood fig pollination, and the classification should not be read as an anticipation of modern taxonomy. The history of plant classification in India, from the Vedic and dharmaśāstra lists to the medical lexicons, is treated in Chapter 6.

    4.3.2 Choosing the land and the site¶

    The texts begin practical instruction with the land. They use the classical division into jāṅgala (dry, open country), ānūpa (wet, marshy country) and sādhāraṇa (intermediate) land, familiar from the medical texts (Zimmermann 1987). They describe good and bad soils by colour, texture and other qualities, and they recommend preparing the ground before planting (Sadhale 1996; Majumdar 1935). Varāhamihira advises softening the soil and enriching it before planting (Bhat 1981). The broader history of Indian soil concepts is treated in Chapter 7.

    The choice of site was not only agronomic. The texts discuss which trees should be planted near the house and in which directions. Following the vāstu literature, they regard certain trees, for instance thorny ones and those with milky sap, as unsuitable near a dwelling. Auspicious trees were to be planted first in a new garden. Such rules combine considerations of shade, safety and convenience with omen lore, and they cannot be neatly separated.

    4.3.3 Seeds, cuttings and propagation¶

    The texts describe propagation from seed, from cuttings and from bulbs or rootstocks. Seeds were selected and treated before sowing. The treatments described include soaking in milk, smearing with ghee or cow-dung, and fumigating with the smoke of various substances; the details differ between the texts (Bhat 1981; Sadhale 1996). Some of these treatments are intended to hasten germination, others to protect the seed.

    Varāhamihira lists trees that can be raised from cuttings smeared with cow-dung. Among them are the jackfruit, the aśoka, the banana, the rose-apple (jambū), the pomegranate, the grape-vine and the citron (Bhat 1981). Propagation by cuttings and by suckers or divisions of the rootstock is well within the experience of any gardener, and the lists are plausible.

    Scholarly Note: Did the texts describe grafting?

    It is often said that the vṛkṣāyurveda texts describe cleft grafting, budding, approach grafting and air-layering in the modern sense, but no such systematic account is found in the editions. A few passages have been read as referring to the insertion of a cutting into another plant, or to the joining of plants. But the wording is brief and open to more than one interpretation, and the translators do not present a systematic account of grafting. Grafting was certainly practised in India at later periods, notably for the mango; its history in the earlier period remains to be established. This volume does not credit the Sanskrit texts with grafting techniques that they do not clearly describe.

    4.3.4 Planting and spacing¶

    Pits are to be dug, prepared and filled with good soil before planting, and young plants protected from sun, wind and animals until established (Sadhale 1996; Majumdar 1935). Varāhamihira gives seasons for transplanting. He also prescribes a spacing between trees: twenty hastas is best, sixteen middling, and twelve the minimum. Trees planted too close, so that their roots touch and intertwine, are said to bear poorly (Bhat 1981). The hasta or cubit was roughly the length of a forearm. These are the distances of a tree garden or orchard, not of a field.

    4.3.5 Watering through the seasons¶

    Varāhamihira’s watering rule is simple. Newly planted trees are to be watered morning and evening in the hot season, on alternate days in the cold season, and in the rains whenever the soil has dried (Bhat 1981). Surapāla and the Upavanavinoda elaborate on this, adjusting watering to the season, the age of the plant and its kind, and warning against both excess and deficiency (Sadhale 1996; Majumdar 1935). The rule that water should be matched to the season and to the plant is sound horticultural advice, though the texts do not quantify it.

    4.3.6 Nourishment and kuṇapajala¶

    The texts recommend a range of nourishing applications. Varāhamihira prescribes watering a tree that fails to fruit with milk, cooled after being boiled with horse gram, black gram, green gram, sesame and barley, to bring abundant flowers and fruit (Bhat 1981). The later texts add manures and liquid preparations of various kinds.

    The best known of these is kuṇapajala. The name means “carrion water” (kuṇapa, corpse or carrion; jala, water). In the description given by Surapāla, and in similar terms by the Upavanavinoda, the remains of animals, flesh, fat and marrow, are boiled in water. The liquid is then mixed with further ingredients such as oil-cake, milk and pulses, and left to ferment for some days before being applied to plants (Sadhale 1996; Majumdar 1935). Versions differ in their ingredients, and no single standard recipe can be extracted from the texts; none is given here. The texts recommend the preparation for nourishing trees and promoting flowering and fruiting.

    Kuṇapajala is a horticultural preparation of the tree and garden texts. It does not appear in the Kṛṣi-Parāśara, and there is no evidence that it was a standard practice of field agriculture. Its essential ingredient is animal remains, which distinguishes it from modern preparations made only from cow dung and urine, such as the jīvāmṛta of natural farming (Section 20.5). Fermented animal and plant residues are a recognised class of organic manure, and it is unsurprising that such a liquid would feed plants; the effects of any particular modern version are a matter for field trials. Rotting animal matter also carries a risk of pathogens and attracts flies and scavengers, and anyone experimenting with such preparations today should follow modern guidance on handling animal by-products.

    4.3.7 Diseases and their treatment¶

    Here the medical model is most visible. Varāhamihira notes that trees exposed to cold, wind and hot sun fall ill. The signs are pale or yellow leaves, the failure of new shoots to grow, the drying of branches and the oozing of sap. The treatment he gives is first to cut away the affected part with a knife, then to apply a paste of viḍaṅga (Embelia ribes), ghee and mud, and to water the tree with milk mixed with water (Bhat 1981).

    Surapāla develops this into a fuller scheme (Sadhale 1996). He distinguishes harm from outside, such as insects, frost, wind, fire, lightning, injury by animals and people, from internal disorders arising from the three doṣas. Each doṣa disorder is recognised by its signs, and the treatment follows the logic of human therapeutics. Oily and nourishing applications counter vāta; cool, sweet substances counter pitta; and pungent, bitter or drying preparations counter kapha. Pastes, washes, fumigation and special waterings are all used. The framework allowed the gardener to sort a wide range of symptoms into a small number of types and to choose a treatment by analogy.

    Some modern writers have argued that the three doṣa categories correspond to water stress, heat stress and fungal disease. The correspondence is loose at best. The doṣa scheme is a classification of symptoms in the language of humoral medicine, not a theory of causes in the sense of modern plant pathology. The texts knew nothing of fungi, bacteria or viruses as agents of disease. Their treatments are best read as what they are: the empirical and theoretical practice of medieval gardeners, worth studying in its own terms.

    4.3.8 Dohada: the cravings of plants¶

    Dohada originally meant the cravings of a pregnant woman, which in Indian custom were to be satisfied for the sake of the child. In Sanskrit poetry the term was transferred to trees. Poetic convention held that certain trees would burst into flower only when their particular “craving” was satisfied, usually by a beautiful woman. The aśoka blossoms when kicked by a young woman’s foot, and the bakula when sprinkled with wine from her mouth. Other trees respond to an embrace, a glance, a song or a laugh. Kālidāsa’s play Mālavikāgnimitra turns on the ritual of the aśoka’s dohada, which the heroine performs. In the Meghadūta the exiled yakṣa describes the aśoka and the bakula in his garden as longing for his wife’s foot and mouth. These are literary conventions (kavisamaya). They were part of courtly culture and may have been enacted in garden festivities, but they are not horticultural observations.

    In the horticultural texts the word acquires a more technical sense. Dohada there refers to special nourishing treatments applied to a tree to induce or increase flowering and fruiting, using substances such as milk, honey, ghee, flesh, fish and various plant products (Sadhale 1996; Majumdar 1935). The two senses coexist. Gardeners of royal and wealthy households worked within a culture in which the poetic notion of the tree’s longing was well known, and their manuals recast it as a matter of feeding the tree what it “desired”.

    4.3.9 Marvels¶

    Both Surapāla and the Upavanavinoda include a section on marvels (adbhuta), procedures said to produce extraordinary effects (Sadhale 1996; Majumdar 1935). They include making a tree flower or fruit out of season, changing the colour of flowers or the taste and fragrance of fruits, making seeds sprout and grow with unusual speed, and producing unusual forms of growth. These are the claims of the texts, and they are reported here as such. Some may reflect real effects of nutrition, pruning or watering on the timing of flowering. Others, such as changing the colour of a flower by watering it with dyed water, are not borne out by plant physiology. There is no reason to suppose that the authors tested them systematically. Such sections were also entertainment for patrons, akin to the marvels of other medieval garden literatures.

    4.3.10 Gardens¶

    The vṛkṣāyurveda texts were written for the owners of gardens: kings, nobles, temples, monasteries and wealthy householders. Sanskrit literature distinguishes several kinds, among them the udyāna or upavana (pleasure garden or park), the ārāma (grove or orchard, also used of monastic parks) and the garden attached to the royal women’s quarters, so prominent in court drama. Such gardens combined fruit trees, flowering trees and shrubs, creepers trained on bowers, ponds and tanks with lotuses, and pavilions. The texts’ advice on spacing, on the placing of trees around the house and the water, and on fragrant and ornamental plants serves this kind of garden. Trees that Āyurveda values as medicines, such as neem, bilva, āmalakī and harītakī, appear among those to be planted and tended, and several of them, notably the aśoka, the bilva and the aśvattha, were valued as auspicious garden and temple trees; but the texts give them no special cultivation rules, and their medicinal uses belong to Volume 13 and Chapter 12. The Mānasollāsa describes a royal garden as one of the king’s enjoyments (Shrigondekar 1925). The Persianate garden tradition, with its walled, quartered chārbāgh, arrived later and is discussed in Chapter 18 (Moynihan 1979).

    The people who actually did the work rarely appear in these texts. Gardening and the supply of flowers and garlands were the hereditary occupation of particular communities, known in Sanskrit as mālākāra (“garland-makers”) and in the modern languages by names such as Mālī. Their practical knowledge was transmitted within families and is largely unrecorded. The treatises represent the learned, written side of a craft whose main bearers were not authors (see Section 5.2).

    4.4 Reading Vṛkṣāyurveda Today¶

    The vṛkṣāyurveda texts are valuable historical documents. They show that by the late first millennium CE there was in India a written tradition of horticulture that drew on long practical experience. They record sensible practices such as soil preparation, propagation by cuttings, adequate spacing, seasonal watering, manuring and the cutting out of diseased wood. They also show how the conceptual tools of medicine were used to organise knowledge about plants. The same texts contain ritual prescriptions, omen lore and marvels. For their authors these were not separate from the practical advice, and a modern reader should not pick out the practical parts and present them as the whole.

    Several cautions follow from the state of the sources. The number of texts is small, and only Surapāla’s is a substantial independent treatise. The identification of many plant names is uncertain, and different translators identify the same Sanskrit name with different species. Where modern experiments on traditional preparations have been carried out, they must be judged on their own merits, by the ordinary standards of agricultural research. Their results tell us about the modern preparation, not about what a medieval author knew.

    The revival of interest in these texts since the 1990s, led in large part by the editions of the Asian Agri-History Foundation, has made them accessible to farmers and gardeners as well as historians. Some organic growers have adopted preparations inspired by them. That is a legitimate modern development, discussed in Chapter 20. But it should not be confused with the historical question of what the texts say, which can be answered only by reading them in critical editions and translations.

    Summary¶

    Vṛkṣāyurveda, the science of the life of trees, is attested as a term in the Arthaśāstra and survives in a small group of texts, from chapter 55 of the Bṛhatsaṃhitā (6th century) and Surapāla (c. 10th–11th century) to the Upavanavinoda (1363) and the Viśvavallabha (16th century); the so-called Vṛkṣāyurveda of Parāśara is of disputed authenticity. Resting on the widely shared view that plants are living beings, the texts treat the plant as a patient and deal with land, propagation, spacing, watering, nourishment (including the animal-based kuṇapajala), diseases classified by the doṣas, dohada, marvels and gardens. Bose’s experiments belong to modern plant physiology and do not confirm the texts’ metaphysics. Read critically, the texts document a real horticultural tradition written for garden owners and resting on the unrecorded skill of gardeners.
