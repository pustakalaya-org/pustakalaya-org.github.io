    Chapter 10: Agroecology: Pests, Livestock, Trees and Fallows¶

    “Agroecology” is a modern word for the study of farms as ecological systems: how crops, weeds, pests, livestock, trees, soil and water interact, and how farming practices shape those interactions. Applied to the Indian past, it is a useful lens, but it is also easily misused. Pre-modern farming in India, as everywhere, used no synthetic fertilisers or pesticides because none existed; that does not make it “organic farming” in the modern sense, nor does it make it sustainable or benign by definition. This chapter examines what the texts and the historical record actually show about the protection of crops and trees from pests, the integration of crops and livestock, the growing of trees on farms, shifting cultivation, the farming systems of the Himalaya and the North-East, and the making of manure. It ends with the ethical ideals often invoked in discussions of Indian ecological thought, and with their limits. Soils are discussed in Chapter 7, water in Chapter 8, forests and sacred groves in Chapter 11, and modern natural-farming movements in Chapter 20.

    10.1 Protecting Crops and Trees in the Texts¶

    10.1.1 Tree medicine¶

    The fullest classical discussions of plant protection concern trees and garden plants rather than field crops, and are found in the Vṛkṣāyurveda tradition (Chapter 4). Varāhamihira’s chapter on trees in the Bṛhatsaṃhitā (chapter 55, 6th century CE) describes the signs of disease in trees and prescribes a simple course of treatment: the diseased part is cut away, the wound is dressed with a paste of viḍaṅga (Embelia ribes), ghee and mud, and the tree is sprinkled with milk and water (Bhat 1981). Surapāla’s Vṛkṣāyurveda (c. 10th–11th century CE) develops this into a fuller system. Following the logic of human medicine, it classifies the disorders of trees by the three doṣas and by external causes such as insects, frost, wind, fire and lightning, and its remedies include pastes applied to wounds, fumigation (dhūpana) with burning plant and animal substances, and sprinkling or watering with various liquids, among them the fermented preparation kuṇapajala (Sadhale 1996). The Upavanavinoda, a section of Śārṅgadhara’s anthology of 1363, follows the same pattern for the trees and flowering plants of the pleasure garden (Majumdar 1935).

    These texts are valuable evidence of how learned gardeners thought about plant health: by analogy with the human body, through the categories of Āyurveda, and with close attention to symptoms such as yellowing leaves, failure to fruit and insect attack. They are not field manuals of crop protection, and the Kṛṣi-Parāśara as edited gives no recipes against pests.

    10.1.2 Pests as calamities¶

    The Arthaśāstra treats rats as one of the great calamities against which a king must guard, alongside fire, flood, disease, famine, wild animals and snakes, and among the measures it lists is the release of cats and mongooses (Kangle 1965). Locust swarms, birds and wild animals in standing crops were a constant concern of farmers, and literature preserves the human side of crop protection. In the fourth canto of the Raghuvaṃśa, Kālidāsa pictures the women who guard the rice fields, sitting in the shade of the sugarcane and singing of the king’s fame; the Tamil poems of the hill country repeatedly describe girls on raised platforms in the millet fields, driving off parrots with rattles and slings. Field-watching from platforms, by day against birds and by night against deer, wild pigs and elephants, was work for women, children and the poor, and it remained a major part of the farming year in forest-edge villages into modern times.

    10.2 Farmers’ Pest Practices¶

    Most pest management in Indian agriculture was never written down in Sanskrit. It was carried in farmers’ practice and has been recorded, unevenly, in colonial reports and modern surveys. The commonly described practices include:

    • Neem. Leaves of the neem tree (Azadirachta indica, nimba) mixed with stored grain and seed; neem cake, the residue of oil pressing, worked into the soil; and, in more recent practice, water extracts of leaves or seed kernels sprayed on crops.

    • Ash and dust. Wood ash dusted on vegetable crops, especially cucurbits, against leaf-eating beetles and other insects; ash or fine sand mixed with stored pulses.

    • Smoke and fire. Smoke from smouldering heaps at the edge of fields, and fires lit at night, to drive off or attract and kill flying insects.

    • Birds as allies. Poles or branches set up in fields as perches for insect-eating birds such as drongos, and the retention of trees and hedges that shelter birds. Bird perches are now also recommended in integrated pest management programmes.

    • Border and trap crops. Rows of a different crop sown around or through the main crop, which may attract pests away from it or harbour their enemies; mixed cropping in general (Chapter 9).

    • Timing and choice of variety. Adjusting sowing dates to avoid periods of heavy pest attack, and keeping landraces known to withstand local pests and diseases.

    • Field sanitation and physical removal. Hand-picking of caterpillars and egg masses, removal of infested plants, and the burning or burial of crop residues.

    Of these, neem has been studied most thoroughly. The seed kernel contains limonoids, of which azadirachtin is the most important, that act on many insects as feeding deterrents, as disrupters of moulting and growth, and as deterrents to egg-laying; their toxicity to mammals is low, though not nil, and they break down quickly in sunlight (Schmutterer 1995). Neem preparations are therefore genuinely useful, but their effect varies with the insect, the crop, the part of the tree used and the preparation; they act more slowly than synthetic insecticides; and they are not harmless to all beneficial insects. The disputes over patents on neem-based products are discussed in Chapter 20.

    For the other practices, systematic evidence of effectiveness is patchy. Some, such as perches for insectivorous birds, rest on sound ecological reasoning and have experimental support in particular settings; others are untested, and each has to be judged on the evidence for it.

    Health and Safety Note

    Several plants used as traditional pest repellents are toxic to people and animals, among them tobacco, the latex of Calotropis (arka) and the seeds of Datura. Neem oil taken by mouth has caused serious poisoning in infants. Nothing in this chapter is a recipe; preparations of this kind should be handled only in line with current agricultural safety advice.

    10.3 Crops and Livestock Together¶

    Indian farming was, for most of its history, mixed farming: crops and animals depended on each other. Cattle and, in wet-rice areas, buffaloes pulled the plough, the cart, the water-lift and the cane-crusher. Their dung was the main manure of the fields, and also a fuel and a building material. In return, the fields fed them: straw of rice and wheat, the stalks (stover) of sorghum and pearl millet, the haulms of pulses and groundnut, and oil cake from the press. In dry regions sorghum stover could be as important a product as the grain, and farmers chose varieties with an eye to fodder as well as food. Trees on farms were lopped for leaf fodder in the dry season (Section 10.4).

    The integration was never complete or frictionless. Much of the dung that might have manured the fields was dried into cakes and burnt for want of fuel wood, a shortage Voelcker regarded as a central constraint on Indian agriculture (Chapter 7). Grazing land and fallows were contested between cultivators and herders; and the care of animals followed lines of caste and gender. Women did much of the work of collecting dung, making dung cakes and cutting fodder. The removal of dead cattle and the working of hides fell to communities treated as untouchable, whose labour was essential to the agrarian economy and whose status was degraded by it (Chapter 13) (Ambedkar 1948; Wiser 1936).

    Relations between settled farmers and mobile pastoralists also had a cooperative side: herders were paid to pen their flocks overnight on harvested fields, an exchange of manure for grazing described in Chapter 13.

    In some wetland regions, fish were part of the same system. The Apatani of the Ziro valley in Arunachal Pradesh raise fish in their irrigated rice terraces; in the brackish coastal fields of Kerala, pokkāḷi rice grown in the monsoon season alternates with prawn and fish culture in the saline season; and in the khazan lands of Goa, reclaimed estuarine fields are managed with sluice gates for both salt-tolerant rice and fish. These are cases where a farming system makes use of seasonal changes in water and salinity rather than fighting them.

    10.4 Trees on Farms: Agroforestry¶

    Growing trees and crops on the same land, now studied under the name of agroforestry, is very widespread in India (Nair 1993). The classical texts encourage the planting of trees, especially by roads, wells and tanks, as a meritorious act (Chapter 4), but the farming systems described below are known mainly from historical observation and modern study.

    10.4.1 Homegardens¶

    The homegardens of Kerala are the best-studied example. Around each house, on plots that are often small, are grown together coconut and areca palms, jackfruit, mango, tamarind, banana, black pepper trained on trees, tubers such as yams and colocasia, vegetables, spices and medicinal plants, frequently with a cow, goats or poultry. The garden supplies food, fodder, fuel, timber and cash crops in a layered structure that resembles, in some respects, a forest (Kumar and Nair 2004). The homegarden is not a fixed “traditional” form: cassava, cocoa, rubber and other introductions of the nineteenth and twentieth centuries have been absorbed into it, and many gardens have been simplified as land has been subdivided or turned over to a single cash crop. Homestead gardens of other kinds are found across India, from the bamboo- and fruit-tree groves around Bengal villages to the kitchen gardens of north Indian households.

    10.4.2 Trees in fields¶

    In the arid west of Rajasthan, the khejri tree (Prosopis cineraria) is deliberately kept in farmers’ fields. Crops such as pearl millet, moth bean and cluster bean are grown beneath it; its leaves are lopped for fodder, its pods (sāṅgarī) are eaten as a vegetable, and its wood is used for fuel. Khejri is a leguminous tree, and farmers in the region regard the soil under it as better for crops. It is protected with particular strictness by the Bishnoi community (Section 10.8 and Chapter 11). Elsewhere farmers retain or plant other useful trees in and around fields: the babul (Vachellia nilotica) on the black soils of the Deccan and Gujarat, valued for timber, pods and gum; the palmyra palm on field bunds in Tamil Nadu, tapped for sap and used for leaves and timber; the mahua (Madhuca longifolia) of central India, whose flowers are food and the source of a distilled liquor and whose seeds yield oil; and the tamarind, mango and neem in almost every region. Trees on bunds and boundaries mark property, provide shade for workers and animals, and break the wind; they also shade and compete with crops, and farmers manage them by lopping and spacing.

    10.4.3 Tree crops under shade¶

    In the Western Ghats, several plantation crops are grown under a canopy of shade trees. In Kodagu (Coorg) and Chikmagalur, coffee is grown beneath retained forest trees and planted shade trees, on which pepper vines are trained; cardamom is grown under forest cover in the hills of Kerala, Karnataka and Tamil Nadu. Coffee is not a traditional Indian crop: tradition credits the Sufi Baba Budan with bringing coffee seeds from Yemen in the seventeenth century, but plantations developed under British and Indian planters in the nineteenth century, often on land taken from forest. These shaded systems retain much more tree cover than open plantations do, and they have attracted the attention of conservationists, but their history is also one of forest clearance and of a plantation workforce recruited under harsh conditions.

    10.5 Shifting Cultivation¶

    Shifting cultivation (swidden, slash-and-burn) is practised in the hills of north-east India, where it is called jhūm, and has been practised in the Eastern Ghats (poḍu), in central India (bewar among the Baiga and other names elsewhere) and in the Western Ghats (kumri). A patch of forest or secondary growth is cut, the vegetation is dried and burnt, and the cleared land is cropped for one or two seasons, usually with a mixture of crops sown together: upland rice, millets, maize, pulses, vegetables, cotton, sesame and tubers. The plot is then left fallow so that the vegetation and the soil can recover, and the cultivators move on to another patch, returning after a cycle of years.

    The ecology of shifting cultivation depends on the length of that cycle. Burning releases nutrients from the vegetation into the soil as ash; the fallow allows trees and bamboo to regrow, rebuilds soil organic matter and suppresses weeds; and the mixture of crops spreads risk and provides a varied diet. P. S. Ramakrishnan’s long-term studies of jhūm in Meghalaya showed that when cycles are long the system can sustain itself, but that as cycles are shortened by growing population, loss of land and other pressures, soil fertility declines, weeds increase and yields fall (Ramakrishnan 1992). Shifting cultivation is thus neither inherently destructive nor inherently sustainable; its effects depend on the cycle, the terrain and the wider pressures on the land.

    Colonial forest departments were hostile to shifting cultivation, which they saw as a waste of timber and a challenge to state control of forests. From the late nineteenth century it was banned or restricted in many reserved forests; in the Central Provinces, the Baiga were confined to a reserved tract in which alone bewar was permitted (Gadgil and Guha 1992). These restrictions were part of the broader colonial transformation of forest rights (Chapter 11). Hostility continued after independence, when shifting cultivation was often described in official documents as primitive and wasteful and cultivators were encouraged to adopt settled terraces or plantations. More recent research has tended to stress the knowledge embodied in shifting cultivation, its crop diversity and the importance of secure land rights for those who practise it, while recognising that shortened cycles can cause real degradation (see also Volume 23, Jana–Jāti).

    10.6 Farming in the Hills: The Himalaya and the North-East¶

    Hills and mountains cover a large part of India, from Kashmir and Ladakh along the Himalaya to Arunachal Pradesh, and south through the hills of Nagaland, Manipur, Mizoram, Tripura and Meghalaya. Farming there contends with slope and erosion, short growing seasons at altitude, cold, heavy monsoon rain on the eastern ranges and, until recently, isolation from markets. Hill peoples answered these conditions with a range of systems that combine terraces, valley-floor rice, shifting cultivation, orchards, forest and livestock. Several have been described already: terraces in general (Section 7.6), the kuhls of Kangra, the zabo of the Chakhesang Nagas and the bamboo pipes of Meghalaya (Section 8.8), shifting cultivation (Section 10.5) and the transhumant herders of the western Himalaya (Chapter 13). The Apatani valley, the alder fallows of the Angami Nagas, the bamboo flowering and famines of Mizoram and the meltwater irrigation of Ladakh are treated in Volume 23 (Jana–Jāti). This section describes some of the other hill systems: the mixed farming of the central Himalaya, Kashmir, the orchards of the western Himalaya, Sikkim, and the rice valley of the Brahmaputra.

    10.6.1 Terraces, forest and livestock in the central Himalaya¶

    In Garhwal and Kumaon (Uttarakhand) and the adjoining hills of Himachal Pradesh, villages farm narrow bench terraces cut into the slopes. Two kinds of land are distinguished. Low terraces in the valleys and on gentle lower slopes, irrigated by small channels (gūl) led from streams, carry rice in the monsoon and wheat in the winter. The much larger area of upland terraces depends on rain alone and carries millets, pulses and other monsoon crops, followed where moisture allows by wheat or barley. Local terminology, such as talāun for the irrigated and upraun for the rain-fed land, records the distinction.

    The fertility of these terraces has depended on the forest. Farmers lop oak (banj, Quercus leucotrichophora) and other trees for winter fodder, gather fallen leaves for bedding in the cattle sheds, and carry the mixture of dung and leaf litter down to the fields as manure, so that a village’s terraces are sustained by a much larger area of forest and grassland around them. Much of this work, from lopping and carrying fodder to spreading manure, is done by women. The link between forest, livestock and field explains why the reservation of Himalayan forests by the colonial forest department from the late nineteenth century, which restricted lopping, grazing and burning, was resented so strongly, and why protest in these hills, culminating in Chipko, took the form it did (Guha 1989); see Section 11.10.

    On the rain-fed terraces the characteristic practice is the mixed sowing known as bārahnājā (“twelve grains”; Section 9.13). A single field may carry finger millet (maṇḍuā), barnyard millet (jhaṅgorā), foxtail millet, amaranth (rāmdānā), horse gram (gahat), rice bean, black soybean (bhaṭ), cowpea and other pulses and vegetables, sown together or in close succession; at higher altitudes buckwheat and naked barley take their place. The crops ripen at different times, differ in their tolerance of drought and pests, provide grain, pulses, greens and fodder from the same land, and spread the risk of failure. From the late 1980s farmers in the Henwal valley of Tehri Garhwal, among them Vijay Jardhari, organised the Beej Bachao Andolan (“save the seeds movement”) to collect and exchange local seeds and to defend mixed cropping against single cash crops promoted by development agencies. Since the 1970s large-scale migration of men to the plains and to the army has left many outlying terraces abandoned, and crop raiding by monkeys and wild boar (Section 16.11) has hastened their abandonment.

    Grain from these terraces was traditionally ground in water mills (gharāṭ), in which a small stream led down a wooden chute turns a horizontal paddle wheel coupled directly to the upper millstone. Thousands of such mills once stood on the streams of Uttarakhand and Himachal Pradesh, usually owned by a family or a village and paid for with a share of the flour. Most were abandoned as diesel and electric mills spread, but since the 1990s improved designs with metal wheels, which can also drive small generators and other machines, have been promoted by government and voluntary agencies as a source of local, renewable power.

    10.6.2 Kashmir: rice, saffron and floating gardens¶

    The Kashmir valley, a flat alluvial basin drained by the Jhelum at about 1,600 metres, has been a land of irrigated rice since at least the period of Kalhaṇa’s chronicle, which records the drainage and irrigation works of Suyya in the ninth century (Section 8.6). Channels led from the side streams water terraced and level rice fields; maize, pulses and oilseeds were grown on the higher, unirrigated land. The fullest description of the agriculture of the valley before modern changes is The Valley of Kashmir (1895) by Walter Lawrence, who as settlement commissioner surveyed its land revenue. It records the cultivators’ varieties of rice, their calendar and their methods of irrigation and manuring, and is frank about the heavy exactions of the state and its officials under which they worked (Lawrence 1895).

    Kashmir’s most famous crop is saffron (Crocus sativus; Sanskrit kuṅkuma). It is grown on the karewa, the raised plateaus of old lake deposits, above all around Pampore south-east of Srinagar. The saffron crocus is sterile and does not set seed; it is propagated by its corms, which are planted in well-tilled beds and left in the ground for several years. The flowers appear in October and November, and the three red stigmas of each flower are picked by hand and dried, so that a kilogram of saffron represents the labour of picking very large numbers of flowers. Kalhaṇa already counts saffron among the characteristic products of the valley in the description of Kashmir that opens his chronicle (Stein 1900), and the Āʾīn-i Akbarī describes the saffron fields (Section 18.7). How and when the plant reached Kashmir is uncertain. In recent decades the saffron area has shrunk under the pressure of urban building on the karewa and of irregular rainfall.

    On the Dal and other lakes around Srinagar, cultivators grow vegetables on floating gardens (rādh): mats of reeds and water plants are cut, bound together, anchored with poles and covered with weed and mud dredged from the lake bed, and on them are grown cucumbers, melons, tomatoes and other vegetables. The gardens can be cut into sections and towed from place to place. They are one of the few Indian examples of a farming system built on the water itself, comparable in principle to the floating gardens of Myanmar’s Inle lake and of Bangladesh.

    10.6.3 Orchards of the western Himalaya¶

    Fruit growing in the western Himalaya is old — the walnuts, apricots and almonds of Kashmir and Ladakh, the grapes of Kinnaur — but the orchard economy that now dominates much of Himachal Pradesh is modern. Small apple orchards were planted by Europeans in Kullu and around Shimla in the late nineteenth century. In 1916 Samuel Evans Stokes, an American who had settled at Kotgarh near Shimla, introduced varieties of the American Delicious apple, which proved well suited to the hills; from the middle of the twentieth century, as roads reached the valleys, apples became the main cash crop of Shimla, Kullu and Kinnaur districts. The change brought prosperity to many villages, but also dependence on a single crop, on market prices and on heavy spraying, and pressure on forests for packing cases until cardboard replaced wood.

    10.6.4 Sikkim and the eastern Himalaya¶

    In Sikkim and the Darjeeling hills the characteristic cash crop is large cardamom (Amomum subulatum), a native plant of the eastern Himalaya, distinct from the small cardamom of the Western Ghats (Section 9.9). It is grown in moist ravines and on shaded slopes under a canopy of trees, especially the Himalayan alder (Alnus nepalensis), whose roots fix nitrogen and whose leaf litter enriches the soil. Ecologists have described the alder–cardamom system as an efficient form of agroforestry that combines a valuable crop with continuous tree cover on steep land. Mandarin oranges, ginger, maize and terraced rice complete the farming of the lower and middle hills. From the late twentieth century the cardamom gardens suffered heavily from virus diseases, known locally as chirke and foorkey, and from the ageing of plantations, and production in Sikkim declined; replanting with healthy suckers and seedlings has been the main remedy.

    Sikkim has also become a test case for organic farming policy. In 2003 the state government resolved to convert its agriculture to organic methods; chemical fertilisers and pesticides were progressively withdrawn, and in 2016 Sikkim was declared fully organic. Supporters point to the state’s small consumption of chemicals even before conversion, to the protection of its rivers and biodiversity and to the market value of an organic label; critics have pointed to lower yields of some crops, dependence on food from outside the state, and difficulties in certification and marketing. Independent, published assessments are still few, and the experiment is too recent for firm conclusions, and the general evidence on organic and natural farming is discussed in Chapter 20.

    10.6.5 The Brahmaputra valley¶

    The Brahmaputra valley of Assam is a rice country shaped by the river’s floods, which bring silt to the fields but can also destroy crops and villages. Assamese cultivators match their rice crops to the levels of the land and to the seasons of the flood. Āhu rice is sown in spring and harvested in summer, before or between floods. Sāli, the main crop, is transplanted during the monsoon and harvested in early winter. Bao, deep-water rice, is broadcast on low-lying land and grows with the rising water, its stems elongating as the flood deepens, to be harvested after the water falls. Boro rice is grown in winter on low wet land and harvested before the floods, now mostly with pumped water. The great Assam earthquake of 1950 raised the beds of many rivers with landslide debris and worsened the floods of the following years; from the mid-1950s the government built long earthen embankments along the Brahmaputra and its tributaries, which protected some land but, when breached, caused sudden and severe flooding and deprived the fields behind them of the silt that floods once brought. Among the many local varieties are glutinous bora rices used in festival foods, aromatic joha rices, and the komal sāul (“soft rice”) prepared from a glutinous variety, which can be eaten after soaking in cold water without cooking.

    Wet-rice cultivation in the valley was greatly extended under the Ahom kings, who entered the Brahmaputra valley from upper Burma in the thirteenth century (1228 in the reckoning of their chronicles) and ruled much of Assam until the 1820s. The Ahom state was organised through the paik system: adult men were registered as paiks, grouped into units that served the state in rotation as soldiers and labourers, and received in return allotments of rice land. Paik labour built the embankments, raised roads (ālī) and tanks of the Ahom kingdom, and Amalendu Guha has argued that the state’s organisation of labour and land was central to the spread of settled wet-rice farming in the valley (Guha 1991). The system, like other systems of compulsory labour, bore heavily on those who served under it, and it was in decline before the British annexation.

    Under colonial rule the valley acquired tea plantations on its higher ground (Section 18.8) and, from the early twentieth century, a large immigration of peasants from eastern Bengal who brought the low riverine and char lands under jute and rice. The valley is also the home of two of India’s native silks (Section 17.5): the golden muga silk of the moth Antheraea assamensis, reared outdoors by village households on som (Persea bombycina) and sualu (Litsea) trees, and eri silk from Samia ricini, reared indoors on castor leaves; because eri cocoons are usually spun after the moth has emerged, rather than reeled, the silk is produced without killing the pupa. In the Khasi hills of Meghalaya, potatoes introduced in the early nineteenth century became a major cash crop alongside the older areca, betel and orange gardens.

    10.6.6 Manipur, Tripura and Mizoram¶

    In the hills south of the Brahmaputra, the contrast between valley and hill is sharpest in Manipur. The Imphal valley, the heartland of the Meitei kingdom, is a basin of permanent wet-rice fields, among whose varieties is the black, aromatic, glutinous chak-hao used in festive dishes; the surrounding hills, home of Naga and Kuki peoples, were farmed largely by jhūm and terraces. The Meitei state, like the Ahom, drew on compulsory periodic labour from its male subjects (the lallup system) for public works, until the system was abolished under British supervision at the end of the nineteenth century. At the southern end of the valley lies Loktak lake, whose surface is partly covered by phumdi, floating mats of vegetation, soil and decaying organic matter. Fishing households build circular enclosures of phumdi (athaphum) in which fish gather and are harvested, and the largest continuous mass of floating vegetation forms the Keibul Lamjao National Park, the only home of the sangai, Manipur’s brow-antlered deer. Since a barrage at Ithai, built for the Loktak hydroelectric project in the 1980s, held the lake at a more constant level, the phumdi have thickened and spread, with consequences for fishing, farming on the lake margins and the sangai’s habitat that remain a matter of dispute.

    In Tripura and Mizoram, where shifting cultivation was the main form of farming, the state has for decades tried to settle shifting cultivators on permanent holdings. In Tripura, programmes from the 1960s onward resettled jhūm families on rubber plantations, and the state became the second-largest producer of natural rubber in India after Kerala. In Mizoram a New Land Use Policy, in successive versions from the 1980s onwards, offered households support to move from jhūm to horticulture, wet-rice terraces, plantations and livestock. Evaluations have been mixed: incomes rose for some households, but the policies were costly, benefits were unevenly distributed, and many families continued to cultivate jhūm alongside the new enterprises. These experiences bear out a conclusion that P. S. Ramakrishnan drew from his long research in the region (Ramakrishnan 1992): that hill farming is changed most durably by building on what cultivators already know and do, rather than by replacing it wholesale.

    Hill agriculture shows, perhaps more clearly than that of the plains, the interdependence of field, forest, water and livestock that runs through this chapter, and the value of crop diversity in uncertain environments. It also shows the limits of policies designed for the plains: the high-yielding packages of the 1960s (Chapter 19) did little for rain-fed hill terraces, and the hill states have increasingly turned instead to horticulture, spices and organic production.

    10.7 Manure, Compost and the Indore Method¶

    Manuring with dung, ash, oil cake, tank silt, leaf litter and the folding of animals is well attested in Indian agriculture. The Kṛṣi-Parāśara gives directions for collecting cow dung, letting it decompose and spreading it on the fields before sowing (Majumdar and Banerji 1960; Sadhale 1999), and the Vṛkṣāyurveda texts give recipes for nourishing trees, including kuṇapajala. These are discussed in Chapters 3, 4 and 7.

    A documented case in which colonial science learned from Indian practice is the work of Albert Howard (1873–1947). Howard went to India in 1905 as Imperial Economic Botanist at Pusa in Bihar, where he worked with his wife Gabrielle Howard, also a botanist, on wheat and other crops. From 1924 to 1931 he directed the Institute of Plant Industry at Indore. There, with Yeshwant D. Wad, he worked out the “Indore process” of composting: vegetable wastes, cattle dung, earth soaked with urine from the cattle shed, wood ash and water were built into heaps or pits and turned at intervals until they had decomposed into humus. The method was published in 1931 (Howard and Wad 1931) and set out at length in An Agricultural Testament (Howard 1940).

    Howard was explicit about his debts. He wrote that he had resolved to treat the cultivators of India as his teachers (Howard 1940), noted the mixed cropping and careful cultivation he observed in their fields, and drew also on the composting practices of East Asia. The Indore process was adopted on some plantations in India and elsewhere, and Howard’s writings became founding texts of the organic farming movement in Britain and beyond. His case also shows the limits of such learning. The Indore process was a systematisation by a colonial research station, not a traditional practice simply recorded; it demanded labour, water and organic material that many smallholders could not spare; and Howard’s insistence on humus was tied to a polemic against chemical fertilisers that later agronomists did not wholly accept. Contemporary natural-farming movements in India draw on a mixture of Howard’s ideas, older Indian practice and new formulations; they are discussed in Chapter 20.

    10.8 Ethical Ideals and Their Limits¶

    10.8.1 Ideals¶

    Indian texts and traditions offer several ideas that modern writers invoke in discussions of agriculture and the environment. The Vedic concept of ṛta, the order by which dawns return, seasons follow one another and sacrifices succeed, is often presented as an ecological principle. In the Vedic texts it is a cosmic and ritual order, closely linked with truth (satya); it is not a theory of ecosystems, though it does express a sense that human action must fit into a larger regularity. The Atharvaveda’s hymn to the Earth (12.1) addresses the earth as mother (12.1.12, quoted in Chapter 2) and includes a farmer’s or digger’s plea:

    Atharvaveda 12.1.35

    yat te bhūme vikhanāmi kṣipraṃ tad api rohatu
    mā te marma vimṛgvari mā te hṛdayam arpipam
    “What I dig out of you, O Earth, may that quickly grow back; O purifier, may I not strike your vital spot or your heart.”

    The opening verse of the Īśā Upaniṣad, with its instruction to enjoy the world through renunciation and not to covet, is also frequently cited in this context; in the text, its subject is the Lord’s pervasion of the world and the renunciation of possessiveness, not the management of nature (Olivelle 1998).

    The principle of non-injury (ahiṃsā) had direct consequences for attitudes to agriculture. Manu’s disapproval of the plough rests on the injury it does to the earth and its creatures (Section 2.9). The Buddhist monastic code forbids monks to dig the earth or destroy plant life, and later Jain lay codes list among trades to be avoided those that involve breaking the earth. These ideas did not stop anyone from farming. They expressed a hierarchy in which the people who actually ploughed were placed low, and in which the labour of cultivation was pushed onto śūdras and those below them (Sharma 1980).

    The Bishnoi community of western Rajasthan, whose rules protect green trees, especially the khejri, and wild animals, has shaped a distinctive farming landscape in which trees and wildlife are tolerated in and around fields (Chapter 11, Section 11.8).

    10.8.2 Limits¶

    These ideals must be set against the historical record.

    • Agricultural expansion meant clearing forests. The Śatapatha Brāhmaṇa’s legend of Videgha Māthava, in which the fire god burns his way eastward across the land, and the Mahābhārata’s burning of the Khāṇḍava forest to found Indraprastha both remember clearing by fire. Over three millennia, the Ganga plain and much of the peninsula were turned from forest and grassland to fields, a process that continued and accelerated under colonial rule (Gadgil and Guha 1992).

    • Farming systems did not prevent famine. Pre-modern and colonial India suffered recurrent famines when the monsoon failed. Mike Davis has argued that colonial revenue and market policies sharply worsened the impact of the droughts of the late nineteenth century (Davis 2001); economic historians such as Tirthankar Roy give more weight to the severity of the droughts, the low productivity of rain-fed farming and the slow growth of transport and credit, and note that famine mortality fell after 1900 as railways and relief improved (Roy 2011). On either view famine was not a colonial invention, and nothing in traditional practice guaranteed food security.

    • The labour was bound by caste. Much of the work of ploughing, transplanting, weeding, harvesting and herding was done by landless labourers, many of them from castes treated as untouchable, under relations of hereditary servitude or debt bondage that persisted into the twentieth century (Prakash 1990; Breman 1974). Women’s labour in the fields was essential and rarely matched by rights in land (Agarwal 1994). Any account of “sustainable” traditional agriculture must ask for whom it was sustained.

    • “Sustainability” is a modern frame. The idea of meeting present needs without compromising those of future generations was formulated in the international environmental debates of the late twentieth century. Pre-modern Indian practices can be studied for what they achieved and failed to achieve under that frame, but they were not designed to meet it, and texts should not be read as if they had anticipated it.

    Debate: Was pre-colonial resource use prudent?

    Madhav Gadgil and Ramachandra Guha argued that Indian caste society, by dividing occupations and resources among endogamous groups, reduced competition and encouraged the prudent use of local resources, and that colonial rule broke down these arrangements by asserting state control over forests and commons (Gadgil and Guha 1992). Their account of colonial forestry has been widely accepted; their picture of pre-colonial harmony has been criticised as idealised, since the same caste order rested on inequality and exclusion and did not prevent depletion of forests and soils. Studies of common-property institutions suggest that sustainable use of shared resources depends on specific rules, enforcement and local conditions rather than on culture in general (Agrawal 2001).

    Summary¶

    The classical texts discuss plant protection mainly for trees and gardens: Varāhamihira, Surapāla and the Upavanavinoda treat tree disorders by analogy with human medicine, with pastes, fumigation and sprinkling. Field crop protection lived in farmers’ practice: neem, ash, smoke, bird perches, border crops, timing, resistant landraces and watching the fields, of which neem has the strongest scientific support (Schmutterer 1995). Indian farms were mixed systems in which cattle, dung, fodder and crop residues linked fields and animals, sometimes with fish in wet-rice and coastal systems, though fuel scarcity, contested grazing and caste-bound labour shaped how they worked. Trees on farms are widespread; shifting cultivation is sustainable or degrading according to its cycle; and the hill systems from Garhwal and Kashmir to Sikkim, Assam and Manipur show farming fitted closely to its terrain. Howard’s Indore method shows colonial science learning from Indian cultivators, with real limits. Ideals of ṛta, reverence for the Earth, ahiṃsā and the Bishnoi ethic are genuine parts of the tradition, but they coexisted with forest clearance, famine and caste-bound labour.

    Further Reading¶

    Schmutterer (1995) is the standard scientific work on neem. Nair (1993) introduces agroforestry, and Kumar and Nair (2004) reviews research on tropical homegardens. Ramakrishnan (1992) is a detailed study of shifting cultivation in north-east India; Guha (1989) links Himalayan forests to village agriculture, Lawrence (1895) describes the agriculture of Kashmir, and Guha (1991) the Ahom state and its rice economy. Howard (1940) gives Howard’s own account of the Indore method, and Gadgil and Guha (1992) is a provocative ecological history of India.
