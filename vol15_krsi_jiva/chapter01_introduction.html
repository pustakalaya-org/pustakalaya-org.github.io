    Chapter 1: Introduction: Knowledge of Fields, Plants and Animals¶

    1.1 Scope of the Volume¶

    This volume is about how people in the Indian subcontinent grew crops, tended trees and gardens, kept and treated animals, and thought and wrote about living things. Its title, Kṛṣi and Jīva Vijñāna, joins three Sanskrit words. Kṛṣi, from the root kṛṣ, “to draw (a furrow), to plough”, is the ordinary word for agriculture. Jīva, “living being”, is the widest of the three: in Jain, Buddhist and Brahmanical thought alike it covers animals and, with qualifications discussed below, plants. Vijñāna means knowledge, especially knowledge of a discriminating, systematic kind, and is used here in the broad sense of “science”. Two further words recur throughout the book. Vṛkṣāyurveda, “the science of the life of trees”, is the name of a small group of texts on arboriculture and horticulture; and paśu, “domestic animal”, names the cattle, buffaloes, sheep, goats and other animals on which farming depended.

    The Arthaśāstra groups agriculture, animal husbandry and trade together under the name vārttā, “livelihood” or economics, one of the four fields of knowledge a king must master (Arthaśāstra 1.4.1; (Kangle 1965)). That grouping is a useful reminder that in South Asia, as elsewhere before the modern period, agriculture, gardening and animal-keeping were not separate sciences but parts of a single economy of field, pasture, forest and household. For that reason this volume treats them together. It brings into one book material that the series had previously divided between a volume on agriculture and a volume on plant and animal knowledge.

    Two kinds of knowledge run through the book. The first is practical knowledge: the skills of cultivators, herders, gardeners, fishers, mahouts, horse-keepers, cattle-healers and many others, learned by doing and passed on by example and word of mouth. Most of it was never written down by the people who held it. The second is textual knowledge: what Sanskrit, Tamil, Kannada, Persian and other authors chose to record about fields, plants and animals, usually for patrons and readers far removed from the plough. The two overlap, but they are not the same, and much of this book is concerned with the relation between them.

    The volume does not claim that this knowledge was uniquely profound, that it anticipated modern science, or that it offers ready-made solutions to present-day agricultural problems; the reasons are set out once, in Section 1.5. The Indian record is rich enough to be studied on its own terms: as the experience of farmers and herders in a monsoon environment over nine thousand years, and as a literate tradition that tried, in its own categories, to describe and regulate that experience.

    1.2 The Sources¶

    No single source, and no single kind of source, gives an adequate picture. The chapters that follow draw on the following groups.

    1.2.1 Vedic literature¶

    The Ṛgveda and Atharvaveda contain hymns addressed to the field, the furrow, the plough, the rain and the plants, as well as charms for the growth of crops and the protection of cattle. The later Vedic prose texts (Brāhmaṇas and Śrautasūtras) describe ritual ploughing and sowing, and the early Upaniṣads use agricultural and food imagery to make philosophical points. These texts were composed by and for a priestly elite and are primarily religious, but they are our earliest written evidence for the crops, implements and animals of northern India (Chapter 2).

    The Ṛgveda’s hymn to the plants (10.97), often quoted for its “hundred and seven domains” of the plants, is a healer’s praise of medicinal herbs, not a botanical survey; its opening verse is given and discussed in Chapter 6, and its medical setting in Volume 13.

    1.2.2 Dharmaśāstra and Arthaśāstra¶

    The legal literature (Dharmaśāstra), above all the Mānava-Dharmaśāstra or Manusmṛti (Olivelle 2005), sets out who may farm, who may own land and cattle, the liabilities of herdsmen for lost animals, the penalties for damage to crops and trees, and rules on food. The Arthaśāstra (Kangle 1965; Olivelle 2013) is a treatise on statecraft with chapters on crown farms, pastures, cattle, horses, elephants, forests, slaughterhouses and irrigation dues. Both are normative texts: they tell us what their authors thought ought to happen, which is not necessarily what did happen.

    1.2.3 Saṃhitās and Purāṇas¶

    The Bṛhatsaṃhitā of Varāhamihira (6th century CE) is an encyclopaedia of omens and natural knowledge. Its chapters on forecasting rain (21–28), on finding groundwater (54, dakārgala) and on the care of trees (55, vṛkṣāyurveda) are among the most important early sources for this volume (Bhat 1981). Several Purāṇas, notably the Agni Purāṇa, contain chapters on tree-planting and on the treatment of cattle, horses and elephants. These compilations borrowed freely from one another and from the specialised treatises, and the numbering of their chapters varies between editions.

    1.2.4 Āyurveda¶

    The medical compendia of Caraka and Suśruta (Sharma 1981; Bhishagratna 1907) classify plants and animals for the purposes of diet and therapy, describe the qualities of grains, pulses, vegetables, fruits, milk and meat, and distinguish types of land and the animals that live on them. They are therefore indispensable for the history of food and of natural history, even though their concern is the human patient (Wujastyk 2003; Meulenbeld 1999).

    1.2.5 The specialised treatises¶

    A small number of Sanskrit and regional-language works are devoted wholly or largely to agriculture, horticulture or the care of particular animals: the Kṛṣi-Parāśara and the Kāśyapīya Kṛṣisūkti on field agriculture; Surapāla’s Vṛkṣāyurveda, the Upavanavinoda and the relevant parts of the Lokopakāra and Viśvavallabha on trees and gardens; the Mānasollāsa, a royal encyclopaedia with sections on gardens, animals, hunting and fishing; the Hastyāyurveda and Mātaṅgalīlā on elephants; and the Śālihotra and related texts on horses. They are later than often supposed, mostly dating from the late first and the second millennium CE, and their dates and authorship are frequently uncertain. Table 1.2 summarises current scholarly opinion.

    1.2.6 Tamil and other regional literatures¶

    Classical Tamil poetry organises its themes by landscape (tiṇai), each with its own plants, animals, occupations and seasons, and so preserves a picture of farming, herding, hunting and fishing in early south India. The Tirukkuṟaḷ devotes a chapter to ploughing (Pope 1886). Kannada, Telugu, Marathi, Bengali, Hindi and other literatures contain agricultural lore, and the Kannada Lokopakāra is itself a regional-language treatise. Chapter 2 discusses the Tamil material.

    1.2.7 Persian sources¶

    From the thirteenth century, Persian chronicles, memoirs and administrative manuals add a new kind of evidence. Bābur’s memoirs describe the plants and animals of Hindustan as a newcomer saw them (Thackston 1996); Jahāngīr’s memoirs record close observations of birds and animals (Thackston 1999); and Abū’l-Faẓl’s Āʾīn-i Akbarī gives crop lists, revenue rates, prices, fruits, and the management of the imperial elephants, horses and cattle (Abū’l-Faẓl 1873). Persian translations and adaptations of Sanskrit veterinary works also circulated. These sources, and the revenue records of the Mughal state analysed by Habib (1999), are treated in Chapters 15 and 18; the Persian agricultural record as a whole is surveyed in Section 18.7.

    1.2.8 Inscriptions¶

    Stone and copper-plate inscriptions are the best evidence we have for actual, datable arrangements on the ground. Aśoka’s edicts record the planting of trees and medicinal herbs and the digging of wells; Rudradāman’s inscription at Junagadh (150 CE) records the history of the Sudarśana reservoir; thousands of later land grants name fields, crops, trees, tanks, pastures and the rights and dues attached to them. South Indian temple and village inscriptions document the management of tanks and the gifts of cattle and land that supported temples. See Chapters 2, 8 and 18.

    1.2.9 Archaeology¶

    Archaeobotany (the study of charred seeds, plant impressions and microscopic plant remains) and zooarchaeology (the study of animal bones) have transformed the early history of farming in South Asia since the 1970s. They show farming at Mehrgarh from about 7000 BCE, the local domestication of zebu cattle, several independent centres of plant domestication within the subcontinent, and the arrival of crops from south-west Asia, Africa and East Asia (Fuller 2006, 2011). Archaeology is the only source for the first five millennia of farming, and it remains an essential check on the texts thereafter.

    1.2.10 Folk sayings and practice¶

    Much agricultural knowledge survives in proverbs and weather sayings attributed to legendary figures — Khanā in Bengal, Ghāgh and Bhaḍḍarī in the Hindi-speaking north — and in the practice of farmers, herders and craftspeople recorded by ethnographers, colonial officials and modern researchers. Two colonial scientists in particular, the agricultural chemist J. A. Voelcker and the economic botanist Albert Howard, recorded Indian practice with respect (Sections 7.5.2 and 10.7). Such sources are invaluable, but the dates of oral sayings are almost always unknown, and observers’ reports reflect their own purposes.

    1.3 Dating the Texts¶

    Few Indian texts are dated by their authors, and many survive only in late manuscripts. Popular literature often assigns very early dates to agricultural and veterinary works on the strength of their attribution to ancient sages. This volume follows current academic opinion and hedges where the evidence is weak. Tables 1.1 and 1.2 give the dates used throughout.

    The pattern that emerges is clear. The Vedic and early historic sources speak of agriculture and animals in passing, within religious, legal or political works. The specialised agricultural and veterinary treatises belong overwhelmingly to the period after about 900 CE. This matters, because a practice described in a twelfth-century text cannot be assumed to have existed a thousand years earlier simply because the text is ascribed to an ancient sage.

    1.4 Reading Śāstra and Practice¶

    The Sanskrit word śāstra means both a body of systematic knowledge and an authoritative text that prescribes. Śāstric writing on agriculture and animals has several features that the reader should keep in mind.

    First, it is prescriptive. A text that tells the farmer to plough when the moon is in a particular nakṣatra, or a king to establish a superintendent of crown lands, records an ideal. Whether farmers or kings followed the prescription must be established from other evidence, and often cannot be.

    Second, it is written from above. With few exceptions, the authors were literate men from priestly, scribal or courtly backgrounds, writing for patrons who owned land and animals rather than for those who worked them. Some authors clearly had first-hand experience; others compiled from earlier works. Verses on rain forecasting, for example, circulate between the Bṛhatsaṃhitā, the Kṛṣi-Parāśara, the Purāṇas and later almanacs, so that agreement between texts may mean borrowing rather than independent observation.

    Third, it is selective. The texts say much about rice, barley and wheat, and about cattle, horses and elephants, which mattered to their patrons; they say little about the millets and pulses that fed most of the rural population in dry regions, about shifting cultivation, or about the knowledge of pastoral and forest communities. Archaeology, inscriptions, travellers’ accounts and ethnography are needed to fill these gaps.

    Fourth, it mixes what we would call empirical, ritual and astrological knowledge. Instructions on manuring, seed storage or the treatment of a lame horse stand beside rules on auspicious days and omens. It is anachronistic to separate the “scientific” parts and discard the rest; it is equally misleading to present the whole as an early form of modern science. This volume therefore describes what the texts say, in their own terms, and does not claim that they “anticipated” modern discoveries. Where a practice described in a text has been tested by modern research, the research is cited by name; where it has not, nothing is claimed.

    Finally, modern editions and translations differ greatly in reliability, a point taken up in the next section.

    1.5 Popular Claims and This Volume¶

    Much that is written about Indian agriculture and biology in popular books, websites and some textbooks cannot be traced to the sources it cites. Because the same claims recur from topic to topic, they are described here once, and the later chapters do not argue with them again. They fall into four kinds.

    The first is the invented or misattributed text: verses with chapter-and-verse numbers that are not in any edition of the work named, and tables built from them. Examples include classifications of twelve soils, earthworm tests and pesticide recipes credited to the Kṛṣi-Parāśara; tables of grafting methods, companion plantings, water requirements and special prescriptions for medicinal trees credited to Surapāla and the other vṛkṣāyurveda texts; and tables of well depths in modern units credited to Varāhamihira. The second is the late or doubtful text presented as ancient, such as the Vṛkṣāyurveda ascribed to Parāśara (Section 4.1) or the Mṛgapakṣiśāstra (Section 17.3). The third is the “modern validation”: yield increases, efficiency percentages and trials reported without a source that can be checked. The fourth is the claim of priority or descent, that a text anticipated a modern discovery, or that a modern preparation such as jīvāmṛta or a “homa” ritual descends from a classical one (Chapter 20).

    This volume does not repeat these claims or rebut them case by case. The later chapters say plainly what each text contains, on the authority of the editions named, and add a correction only where a specific misreading would otherwise mislead. A reader who meets a striking claim elsewhere should ask for the edition and verse number and check them. The practice of the present volume is to cite only verses that can be checked against an edition, and otherwise to paraphrase with the locus.

    1.6 Conceptual Frameworks¶

    Several ideas recur across the sources and are worth introducing here.

    1.6.1 The five elements¶

    Brahmanical, Buddhist and Jain thought all analysed the material world into elements (bhūta). The classical Sanskrit scheme has five: earth (pṛthivī), water (āp, jala), fire (tejas, agni), wind (vāyu) and space (ākāśa). The Taittirīya Upaniṣad (2.1) gives a famous cosmogony in which space, wind, fire, water and earth arise in turn, then plants from earth, food from plants, and the person from food (Olivelle 1998). Āyurveda uses the elements, and the three doṣas derived from them, to explain the qualities of foods and drugs; the vṛkṣāyurveda texts extend the same vocabulary to trees, describing some of their disorders as disturbances of vāta, pitta and kapha (Chapter 4). These frameworks organised observation; they were not measurements.

    1.6.2 Plants as living beings¶

    Whether plants are living, sentient beings was a long-standing question. The Manusmṛti (1.49) says that plants, enveloped by the darkness that results from past actions, are “inwardly conscious” (antaḥsaṃjña) and experience pleasure and pain (Olivelle 2005). Jain thought is the most explicit: plants are among the beings with a single sense, touch, along with earth-, water-, fire- and air-bodies, and harming them is a form of violence (hiṃsā) (Tatia 1994). Buddhist monastic rules forbid monks to destroy plant life, though Buddhist doctrine generally did not count plants as sentient in the full sense. These views had practical consequences: they shaped attitudes to ploughing, to the felling of trees and to the occupations Jains and some others considered acceptable.

    J. C. Bose’s experiments on the electrical responses of plants, sometimes hailed as a vindication of these ideas, belong to plant physiology and neither confirm nor refute a doctrine about consciousness (Section 4.2.3).

    1.6.3 Dry land and wet land¶

    Āyurvedic texts classify country (deśa) into three kinds: jāṅgala, dry, open land with sparse trees and thorny scrub; ānūpa, wet, marshy land with abundant water and vegetation; and sādhāraṇa, land of a middle character. Each has its characteristic animals, plants and diseases, and the meat of dry-land animals is generally preferred in diet. Francis Zimmermann (1987) showed how this scheme structures Āyurvedic thinking about food and health, and pointed out that the English “jungle”, borrowed from jāṅgala, has come to mean almost the opposite of the original. The Sanskrit word names dry, open savanna; the English word names dense, wet forest. The same distinction between dry and wet land appears in the Arthaśāstra’s rainfall norms (Chapter 2) and in later soil classifications (Chapter 7).

    1.6.4 Seasons and the calendar¶

    The texts divide the year into six seasons (ṛtu) of two months each and time agricultural operations by the lunar months and the nakṣatras, the twenty-seven or twenty-eight lunar mansions. Farmers in practice relied as much on the onset and behaviour of the monsoon as on the almanac. The familiar terms kharīf (monsoon crop), rabī (winter crop) and zāid (the minor summer crop) are Arabic in origin and entered Indian usage through the revenue administration of the Sultanate and Mughal periods; they are not classical Sanskrit categories. The crop calendar is set out in Section 9.12 (Figure 9.1), the lunar calendar of the texts in Chapter 3, and its astronomy in Volume 12.

    1.7 Transmission and Modern Editing¶

    Agricultural and veterinary skills were transmitted mainly within households, castes and occupational communities, by apprenticeship and oral instruction. Texts were transmitted in manuscripts on palm leaf, birch bark and paper. Many of the treatises discussed in this volume survive in only a handful of manuscripts, sometimes in a single one, and copyists added, omitted and altered material. Critical editions are few.

    Modern study of the agricultural texts began largely in Bengal. Girija Prasanna Majumdar published a translation of the Upavanavinoda in 1935 (Majumdar 1935) and, with Sures Chandra Banerji, an edition and translation of the Kṛṣi-Parāśara in the Bibliotheca Indica in 1960 (Majumdar and Banerji 1960). From the 1990s the Asian Agri-History Foundation in Secunderabad, associated above all with the plant pathologist Y. L. Nene, published a series of translations in its Agri-History Bulletin series, including Nalini Sadhale’s versions of Surapāla’s Vṛkṣāyurveda (Sadhale 1996) and the Kṛṣi-Parāśara (Sadhale 1999) and S. M. Ayachit’s Kāśyapīya Kṛṣisūkti (Ayachit 2002). M. S. Randhawa’s four-volume History of Agriculture in India (Randhawa 1980) remains a useful survey, though it should be used with caution on dates; the multi-author History of Agriculture in India (up to c. 1200 AD) edited by Lallanji Gopal and V. C. Srivastava (Gopal and Srivastava 2008) goes deeper into the Sanskrit and epigraphic sources. These works made the texts accessible; they should be read alongside the original Sanskrit wherever possible, since translators sometimes interpret technical terms in the light of modern agronomy.

    European study of Indian plants began with the trading companies. The Hortus Malabaricus (Amsterdam, 1678–1693), compiled for the Dutch governor of Malabar, Hendrik van Rheede, rested on the knowledge of the Ezhava physician Itty Achuden and Konkani brahmin scholars and was later used by Linnaeus (Section 6.9; (Manilal 1980)). Colonial botanical gardens, surveys and gazetteers followed. George Watt’s Dictionary of the Economic Products of India (Watt 1889) and Kirtikar and Basu’s Indian Medicinal Plants (Kirtikar and Basu 1935) compiled vernacular names and uses on a vast scale. This colonial science depended heavily on Indian informants whose contributions were often unacknowledged, and it served the revenue and commercial purposes of the state (Arnold 2000). It is nevertheless the foundation of much later ethnobotany, and it is discussed in Chapters 6, 12 and 18.

    1.8 Who Did the Work¶

    Any honest account of Indian agriculture must ask who ploughed, sowed, weeded, harvested, herded and skinned, and who owned the land and the animals. The answers are uncomfortable. The Brahmanical texts assign agriculture, cattle-keeping and trade to the vaiśya and service to the śūdra, but they also discourage the higher varṇas from ploughing themselves (Manusmṛti 10.83–84; Chapter 2). In practice, from at least the first millennium CE, the actual cultivators were very often śūdra peasants, dependent tenants and labourers, while ownership and the rights to the produce lay with landholding lineages, temples, brahmin grantees and the state (Sharma 1980).

    Below the cultivating castes stood communities later called untouchable and today Dalit, who in many regions supplied the agricultural labour force, often in hereditary bondage, and who also did the work of removing dead cattle, flaying and tanning on which the leather buckets of irrigation wells and the harnesses of ploughing depended (Ambedkar 1948; Prakash 1990). Pastoral communities — Ahīr, Gujar, Rabārī, Kuruba, Dhangar, Gaddi, Toda and many others — managed the herds and much of the knowledge of breeding and animal health. Fishing, the catching and training of elephants, the keeping of horses and the gathering of forest produce were each the work of particular communities. Women did much of the transplanting, weeding, harvesting, processing of grain, dairying and seed selection, while being largely excluded from ownership of land (Agarwal 1994).

    The knowledge of these people is mostly absent from the texts discussed in this volume, which were written by and for those who did not do the work. The volume tries to keep them in view, and Chapter 18 discusses the history of land, caste and labour from the early centuries CE to 1950 in detail; the place of hides, leather and cattle in the caste order is discussed in Chapter 13.

    1.9 Conventions¶

    Sanskrit, Pali and Prakrit words are given in the International Alphabet of Sanskrit Transliteration (IAST). A short guide to pronunciation: a line over a vowel (ā, ī, ū) marks a long vowel, and e and o are always long; c is pronounced as in English “church”; ś and ṣ are both close to English “sh”; consonants with a dot below (ṭ, ḍ, ṇ) are retroflex, made with the tongue curled back; h after a consonant is aspirated, so that th is as in “hothouse”, not “thin”; ṛ is a vowel, now usually pronounced ri; ṃ nasalises the preceding vowel; and ḥ is a light breath after a vowel. Tamil follows the transliteration of the Tamil Lexicon of the University of Madras, which adds ḻ (a retroflex approximant, as in Uḻavu), ṟ (a trilled r) and ṉ (an alveolar n) and distinguishes short e and o from long ē and ō. Persian and Arabic words are transliterated in a simplified form of the system used by the historians cited (for example Āʾīn-i Akbarī, zamīndār), with ʿ for the Arabic letter ʿayn and ʾ for the glottal stop. Words from Hindi and the other modern languages are given in their modern form, with long vowels marked where this helps (khejrī, bājrā), and without the final a that Hindi does not pronounce. Personal names of modern people, place names and the names of communities are given in their usual English spelling (Bishnoi, Raika, Kannauj) unless a transliterated form is needed to show a Sanskrit or Tamil word. Dates follow the tables above.

    Primary texts are cited by the divisions of the edition named: the Ṛgveda and Atharvaveda by book, hymn and verse; the Mahābhārata by the book, chapter and verse of the Poona critical edition; the Arthaśāstra by the book, chapter and sentence of Kangle’s edition; the Bṛhatsaṃhitā by the chapter numbers of Bhat’s translation; and the Sutta Nipāta by sutta number with the verse number of the Pali Text Society edition (so “Sn 1.4, v. 77”). Quotations from primary texts are given in the original only when both the wording and the reference can be verified; otherwise the content is paraphrased and attributed. Where chapters discuss poisons, remedies for humans or animals, or the consumption of cattle products, they include a safety note: classical prescriptions are described as historical evidence, not as advice, and no doses or usable recipes are given.

    1.10 Plan of the Volume¶

    The volume has twenty chapters. The first two are introductory and historical; Chapters 3 to 12 deal with agriculture and plants; Chapters 13 to 17 with animals; and the last three with agrarian history, the rise of agricultural science, and the present. Readers who want the social frame before the treatises may read Sections 18.2 and 18.3 of Chapter 18 after Chapter 2.

    1. Introduction (Chapter 1): scope, sources, dating and frameworks.

    2. From Mehrgarh to the early historic period (Chapter 2): archaeobotany and the domestication of plants and animals; Harappan and Neolithic farming; agriculture in Vedic, Buddhist, Jain, Mauryan and early Tamil sources.

    3. The Kṛṣi-Parāśara and rain forecasting (Chapter 3): the Kṛṣi-Parāśara, the Bṛhatsaṃhitā’s rain chapters, folk forecasting, and the other agricultural treatises.

    4. The vṛkṣāyurveda tradition (Chapter 4): the texts on arboriculture and horticulture, from the Bṛhatsaṃhitā to the Upavanavinoda and the Viśvavallabha.

    5. Gardens, flowers and aromatics (Chapter 5): the flowers of worship and poetry, garland-makers and temple gardens, perfumery and the attar distillers, and betel.

    6. Classifying plants (Chapter 6): Vedic, legal, medical, lexical, Jain and Buddhist schemes; folk taxonomies; comparison with Linnaean botany.

    7. Soil, tillage and implements (Chapter 7): concepts and classifications of soil, manuring and conservation, and the plough and other tools of field work.

    8. Water and irrigation (Chapter 8): tanks, anicuts, canals, wells and stepwells, lifting devices, and the social management of water.

    9. Crops and seeds (Chapter 9): the major crops, introduced crops, seasons, rotation and mixed cropping, seed saving and landraces.

    10. Agroecology (Chapter 10): pest management, crop–livestock integration, agroforestry, home gardens, shifting cultivation and the farming systems of the Himalaya and the North-East, and the limits of “sustainability” claims.

    11. Forests and sacred groves (Chapter 11): forests in texts and inscriptions, sacred groves and trees, the Bishnoi and Chipko, and colonial forestry.

    12. Medicinal and useful plants (Chapter 12): identification, cultivation, trade and conservation of plants used in medicine.

    13. Livestock and veterinary knowledge (Chapter 13): domestication, cattle and other livestock, pastoralists, breeds, ethnoveterinary knowledge, the history of cow veneration, and hides and caste.

    14. Dairy and food processing (Chapter 14): milk products, fermentation, grain processing, oil pressing, sugar and preservation.

    15. Elephants and horses (Chapter 15): the Hastyāyurveda, the Mātaṅgalīlā, the capture and keeping of elephants, the Śālihotra tradition and the horse trade.

    16. Wild animals (Chapter 16): wild mammals in texts and art, hunting and its critics, royal and colonial hunting, the history of wildlife protection, and conflict between wildlife and farming.

    17. Birds, insects and aquatic life (Chapter 17): birds in literature and practice, silk, lac and bees, fish and fisheries, pearls and fishing communities.

    18. Agrarian society and change, from the early centuries CE to 1950 (Chapter 18): land grants, Indo-Islamic agriculture and gardens, the Mughal agrarian system and the Persian agricultural record, caste and labour, and colonial agrarian change.

    19. Agricultural science and the state, 1870–1970 (Chapter 19): famine commissions and agricultural departments, Pusa and the research institutes, crop breeding, crop surveys, the Royal Commission on Agriculture, and the coming of the high-yielding varieties.

    20. The modern period (Chapter 20): the Green Revolution and its critics, natural-farming movements, genetically modified crops, the documentation and protection of traditional knowledge, biodiversity law and conservation.

    Summary¶

    This volume treats agriculture, plants and animals together because they formed a single economy in which field, pasture, forest and household depended on one another. Its sources are diverse: Vedic hymns, legal and political treatises, medical compendia, the Bṛhatsaṃhitā, a small number of specialised agricultural, horticultural and veterinary treatises that mostly date from after 900 CE, Tamil poetry, Persian memoirs and records, inscriptions, archaeology, and the sayings and practices of farmers and herders. The texts are prescriptive, written from above and selective, and they should be read as evidence of what their authors thought and recommended, not as proof of early modern science. The people who did most of the work — śūdra and Dalit cultivators and labourers, pastoralists, fishers and women — are largely absent from them, and the chapters that follow try to keep both the texts and the workers in view.

    Further Reading¶

    Fuller (2006) is the best synthesis of the archaeological evidence for early agriculture. Gopal and Srivastava (2008) is the fullest critical survey of the Sanskrit, Pali and epigraphic evidence for agriculture up to about 1200 CE, and Randhawa (1980) offers a broad, well-illustrated survey of agricultural history. Zimmermann (1987) is a classic study of ecological ideas in Āyurveda. Sadhale (1996) gives an accessible translation of a major horticultural text. Trautmann (2015) shows how a single animal can illuminate Indian political and environmental history, and Ludden (1999) provides the long-term agrarian context.
