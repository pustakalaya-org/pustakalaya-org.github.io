    Chapter 13: Livestock, Pastoralists and Veterinary Knowledge¶

    13.1 Animals in the Agrarian Economy¶

    Indian agriculture has always depended on animals. Bullocks and buffaloes pulled the plough, the cart, the oil-press and the leather bucket of the well; cattle, sheep and goats turned crop residues and uncultivated pasture into milk, meat, wool and dung; dung was the principal manure and, dried into cakes, the principal fuel of the countryside. Alongside the settled farmers lived pastoral communities whose whole way of life was organised around herds and seasonal movement. This chapter traces the history of livestock in India — domestication, the place of cattle in Vedic society and the long history of cow veneration, the state’s management of herds in the Arthaśāstra, the surviving veterinary knowledge, the pastoral communities and the breeds they created — and treats honestly the social costs of an economy built on cattle. Elephants and horses, the animals of kings, are treated in Chapter 15; dairying in Chapter 14.

    13.2 Domestication¶

    The archaeological record shows that South Asia was an important centre of animal domestication (Meadow 1996; Fuller 2006). At Mehrgarh, on the Kachi plain at the edge of the Indus valley, goats and sheep appear in the earliest Neolithic levels (from c. 7000 BCE), and over the following millennia the bones of cattle increase in number and decrease in size — a classic sign of domestication. Richard Meadow argued that the humped zebu (Bos indicus) was domesticated locally in this region, independently of the taurine cattle of West Asia; genetic studies of modern cattle support a separate origin for zebu. By the Harappan period zebu cattle were the dominant domestic animal, depicted on seals and in figurines, and used for traction, as the ploughed field at Kalibangan and model carts show (Kenoyer 1998).

    The water buffalo (Bubalus bubalis) was probably domesticated in South Asia as well, and buffaloes are depicted on Harappan seals; whether particular Harappan buffaloes were wild or domestic is debated. The domestic chicken descends from the red junglefowl (Gallus gallus), native to the forests of South and Southeast Asia; where and when it was first domesticated is still disputed, and both regions may have contributed. The dromedary camel became important in western India later, and the horse, as Chapter 15 explains, arrived in the second millennium BCE. Pigs, descended from the native wild boar, were kept in many regions, though they came to be regarded as impure by high-caste Hindus and by Muslims.

    13.3 Cattle in Vedic Society¶

    In the Ṛgveda cattle (go, paśu) are the principal form of wealth. Poets pray for cows, describe raids to capture them (gaviṣṭi, literally “desire for cows”, also a word for battle), and use the image of the cow and her calf, of the milk and the cow-pen, for the most important things in their world — the dawns, the waters, poetic speech itself (Jamison and Brereton 2014). A hymn welcomes the cows to the settlement:

    Ṛgveda 6.28.1

    ā gāvo agmann uta bhadram akran sīdantu goṣṭhe raṇayantv asme
    prajāvatīḥ pururūpā iha syur indrāya pūrvīr uṣaso duhānāḥ
    “The cows have come and have brought good fortune. Let them settle in the cow-pen and take their pleasure with us. Rich in offspring, of many colours, let them be here, giving milk for Indra through many dawns.”

    The cow is often called aghnyā, “not to be killed”, in the Ṛgveda. Yet the Vedic texts also describe the sacrifice of cattle in a number of rituals, and later Vedic texts discuss the eating of their meat. The Śatapatha Brāhmaṇa (3.1.2.21) reports the sage Yājñavalkya as saying that he eats the flesh of cows and oxen provided it is tender, and Pāṇini’s grammar (Aṣṭādhyāyī 3.4.73) records the word goghna, “one for whom a cow is killed”, meaning a guest. These and other passages show that the slaughter of cattle for sacrifice and hospitality was accepted in Vedic society, alongside a growing sense of the special value of the cow (Jha 2002; Alsdorf 2010).

    13.4 The History of Cow Veneration¶

    How the cow came to be regarded as sacred and inviolable is one of the most debated questions in Indian social history, and one that has acquired great political sensitivity in modern India. The main lines of the scholarly account are nevertheless clear.

    Ludwig Alsdorf showed that two separate developments — the rise of vegetarianism grounded in the ethic of non-injury (ahiṃsā), and the specific veneration of the cow — must be distinguished (Alsdorf 2010). The first owes much to the renouncer traditions, Buddhist and especially Jain, and to the Upaniṣadic and Dharmaśāstric absorption of their values. The Manusmṛti (c. 2nd century CE) still permits the eating of meat in certain circumstances, but praises abstention:

    Manusmṛti 5.56

    na māṃsabhakṣaṇe doṣo na madye na ca maithune
    pravṛttir eṣā bhūtānāṃ nivṛttis tu mahāphalā
    “There is no fault in eating meat, in drinking liquor or in sex; that is the natural inclination of creatures. But abstaining from them bears great fruit.”

    The specific inviolability of the cow developed over the first millennium CE. In the Arthaśāstra, the superintendent of slaughterhouses is told that calves, bulls and milch cows are not to be killed, which implies that the slaughter of other cattle was regulated rather than forbidden (Kangle 1965; Olivelle 2013). Later Dharmaśāstra texts list beef-eating among the practices forbidden in the present age (kalivarjya) and treat the killing of a cow as a grave sin; by the early medieval period the protection of cows and brahmins had become a standard formula of royal duty. D. N. Jha’s survey of the textual evidence for beef-eating in early India provoked strong reactions, but its central documentary claims rest on well-known passages (Jha 2002).

    B. R. Ambedkar connected this history with the origins of untouchability. He argued that when beef-eating came to be abhorred, groups who continued to eat the flesh of dead cattle — because they were poor and because their work involved disposing of carcasses — were stigmatised as untouchable (Ambedkar 1948). Whatever the merits of his theory of origins, it is certain that in historical times the removal of dead cattle, the flaying of hides and the tanning of leather were assigned to Dalit castes and were central to their exclusion (see Chapter 18). The veneration of the living cow and the stigmatisation of those who handled the dead one were two sides of the same agrarian order.

    In the late nineteenth century cow protection became a mass political movement, with the founding of cow-protection societies (gaurakṣiṇī sabhās) and serious communal riots in the 1890s. The Constitution of India (Article 48, among the Directive Principles) directs the state to take steps to prohibit the slaughter of cows, calves and other milch and draught cattle, and most states have passed laws to that effect, of varying severity. The enforcement of these laws and violence by self-appointed cow-protection groups have become contested issues in contemporary India. The historian’s task is to describe how ideas about the cow changed over three thousand years, not to settle present-day disputes by appeal to an imagined unchanging tradition.

    13.5 The State and the Herds: The Arthaśāstra¶

    The Arthaśāstra (composite, c. 50 BCE–300 CE) gives the most detailed early picture of livestock management, from the standpoint of a state that owned large herds (Kangle 1965; Olivelle 2013). Its superintendent of cattle (go’dhyakṣa) keeps registers of the state’s cattle, buffaloes, goats, sheep, asses, camels and pigs, classified by age, sex and condition; assigns herds to herdsmen on various terms (for wages, for a fixed return of butter, or for a share of the increase); requires herdsmen to report deaths and produce the brand-marked hide; and specifies grazing seasons, the protection of herds from thieves, tigers and snakes, the expected yield of ghee from a given measure of milk for cows, buffaloes, goats and sheep, and the rations for working and breeding animals. Veterinary attendants are mentioned among the staff of herds, as of the horse and elephant stables.

    The edicts of Aśoka (r. c. 268–232 BCE) give an independent picture of royal concern for animals: Rock Edict II records medical treatment for animals as well as people, and Pillar Edict V restricts the castration and branding of cattle on particular days (Chapter 11, Section 11.4). These are statements of royal policy and piety; how far they were implemented we do not know.

    13.6 Veterinary Knowledge¶

    13.6.1 What Survives in the Texts¶

    For elephants and horses there are substantial Sanskrit treatises (Chapter 15). For cattle there is much less. Popular accounts speak of a classical Gavāyurveda with its own texts and verses, but no early independent treatise of this kind is securely attested. What survives is scattered: chapters on the care and treatment of cattle in some Purāṇas, notably the Agni Purāṇa; the Bṛhatsaṃhitā’s chapter on the auspicious marks of cattle (Bhat 1981); passages on the cattle-shed, the care of draught bullocks and the use of dung in the Kṛṣi-Parāśara (Chapter 3); and short treatments in compendia such as the Mānasollāsa (Shrigondekar 1925). Āyurvedic texts classify animals — for example by habitat into land, marsh and water animals, and by feeding habit — mainly for the purposes of human diet and medicine (Sharma 1981; Zimmermann 1987); their classification is discussed in Chapter 16 (Section 16.3) and in Volume 13.

    Where these sources discuss disease, they apply the categories of human Āyurveda — the three doṣas, the classification of diseases as curable, manageable or incurable — and prescribe treatments by oils, decoctions, fumigation, purgation and diet, often together with ritual measures. They are evidence for the history of veterinary ideas, not a tested pharmacopoeia.

    13.6.2 Ethnoveterinary Knowledge¶

    The greater part of Indian veterinary knowledge was never written in Sanskrit. Herders, cattle-keepers and village healers — often specialists from pastoral or service castes — treated wounds, fractures, bloat, retained placenta, eye diseases and parasites with plant preparations, bone-setting, cautery, bleeding and charms. Such practices have been documented by ethnoveterinary researchers in many regions, and some plant treatments have been tested; most have not (Jain 1991). Their value lies in the close observation of animals that underlies them and in their availability where modern veterinary care is scarce. Several of the plants used — the latex of Calotropis, for instance — are toxic, and the safety of these remedies cannot be assumed.

    Health and Safety Note

    This chapter describes historical and traditional veterinary practices; it does not recommend them. Many traditional remedies use plants or substances that are toxic to animals or people in quantity, and none should be used without veterinary advice. The consumption of cow urine or of pañcagavya preparations by people is a ritual and Āyurvedic practice; claims that it cures diseases are not supported by reliable clinical evidence, and raw animal products can carry infections.

    13.6.3 Colonial and Modern Veterinary Science¶

    Modern veterinary science came to India with the army’s need for horses and transport animals and with the devastating cattle epidemics of the nineteenth century, above all rinderpest. A veterinary college was opened in Bombay in 1886, and the Imperial Bacteriological Laboratory, founded in 1889, moved to Mukteswar in the Kumaon hills in 1893; it developed sera and vaccines against rinderpest and other diseases and is the ancestor of the present Indian Veterinary Research Institute (Arnold 2000). Rinderpest was finally eradicated worldwide in 2011, after a long campaign in which India was a major participant.

    13.7 Pañcagavya and the Uses of the Cow¶

    The five products of the cow (pañcagavya) — milk, curd, ghee, urine and dung — have a long ritual history. In Dharmaśāstra a mixture of them is drunk as a rite of purification and expiation (prāyaścitta), and they are used in consecrations. Āyurvedic texts include cow’s urine among the urines of eight animals used in certain preparations (Sharma 1981). In agriculture, dung was the main manure and, mixed with water, the material for plastering floors and walls; dung-cakes were the fuel of the poor. In recent decades pañcagavya preparations have been promoted as agricultural inputs and as medicines. Their agricultural use is discussed in Chapters 10 and 20; as medicines for people, their claimed benefits have not been established by adequate clinical studies, and statements to the contrary in popular literature should be treated with great caution (see the safety note above).

    13.8 Pastoral Peoples¶

    Much of India’s livestock, and much of its knowledge of animals, belonged to pastoral communities who moved their herds between seasonal pastures and lived in symbiosis — and sometimes conflict — with settled farmers. Farmers valued the manure that herds left when penned on harvested fields, and bought animals, milk and wool; herders depended on access to stubble, fallow, forest and common grazing lands (Gadgil and Guha 1992). Among the best-known communities are:

    • the Raika or Rabari of Rajasthan and Gujarat, breeders of camels, sheep and cattle, whose camel lore is renowned;

    • the Maldhari cattle and buffalo herders of the Banni grasslands of Kachchh and the Gir forest of Saurashtra;

    • the Gaddi shepherds of Himachal Pradesh, who move flocks between the high Himalayan pastures and the plains of Kangra;

    • the Gujjar and Bakarwal of Jammu and Kashmir, and the Van Gujjar buffalo herders of the Shivalik forests;

    • the Dhangar shepherds of Maharashtra, the Kuruba of Karnataka and the Golla of Andhra, many of whose communities trace their identity to herding;

    • the Toda of the Nilgiri hills, whose buffaloes and sacred dairies are the centre of their religious life;

    • the Changpa of eastern Ladakh, who herd the goats that produce the fine undercoat (pashm) used in pashmina shawls.

    Their knowledge of breeding, grazing, seasonal routes, animal disease and the plants of the pastures is extensive and largely unwritten. Colonial forest laws, the enclosure and encroachment of common lands (gocara, “cattle-pasture”), irrigation of former grazing lands and modern conservation policies have all restricted their movements, and many pastoral communities today face severe pressure on their way of life. The next section looks more closely at how these communities moved, what they exchanged with farmers, and how their access to pasture was lost.

    13.9 Pastoral Mobility¶

    13.9.1 Routes and Seasons¶

    Pastoral movement in India is not aimless wandering but a disciplined use of pastures that are productive at different times of year. In the mountains the movement is vertical: flocks spend the winter in the warm foothills and valleys and climb in spring and summer, following the melting snow, to the alpine meadows above the tree line, returning before the passes close. In the plains and the arid zone the movement is horizontal, away from dry home tracts in the hot season towards regions with crop stubble, canal-irrigated land or forest grazing, and back again with the rains. Ecologists and historians call the first pattern transhumance; but in practice most Indian herding communities combine some settled cultivation with seasonal movement, and within a single community some households migrate while others stay at home with the fields (Gadgil and Guha 1992).

    The Gaddi of Himachal Pradesh are the classic case of Himalayan transhumance. Their homeland is the upper Ravi valley around Brahmaur (Bharmour) in Chamba, the region known as Gadderan, where many Gaddi households farm terraced fields. Migrating Gaddi shepherds take their sheep and goats in winter down to the lower hills of Kangra, Mandi and neighbouring districts and towards the Shivalik foothills; in spring they move up again, crossing the Dhauladhar range, and spend the summer on high pastures in Chamba and, beyond the Pir Panjal, in Lahaul, before turning back in the autumn. Each flock follows customary routes and halting places, and grazing on particular pastures was recorded in the colonial forest settlements as a right attached to named permit-holders. Vasant Saberwal’s study of Gaddi grazing argues that the forest officials’ long-standing belief that Gaddi flocks were degrading the Himalayan slopes rested on weaker evidence than its confidence suggested, and that grazing policy in the hills was shaped as much by bureaucratic interests and by the herders’ own political bargaining as by ecological findings (Saberwal 1999).

    Further west, in Jammu and Kashmir, the Gojri-speaking Gujjar and Bakarwal communities follow a similar pattern on a larger scale. The Bakarwal (“goat-keepers”) are predominantly sheep and goat herders who winter in the foothills of the Jammu region and in spring take their flocks, with pack horses and dogs, over the passes of the Pir Panjal to summer meadows in the Kashmir valley and the high country beyond. Many Gujjars keep buffaloes and cattle and sell milk and milk products; some are settled cultivators, others move seasonally. Both communities were recognised as Scheduled Tribes in Jammu and Kashmir in 1991. The Van Gujjars (“forest Gujjars”) of the Uttarakhand and Himachal foothills, Muslim buffalo herders, wintered in the Shivalik forests and took their herds to high pastures in summer; their position inside what became the Rajaji National Park is discussed in Chapter 16.

    In western Rajasthan the Raika (Rabari) sheep herders of Marwar leave their villages in the dry months, travelling in groups that pool flocks and labour, and move east and south into Madhya Pradesh, Haryana and neighbouring regions, where harvested fields, fallows and roadside grazing are available, returning home with the monsoon. Arun Agrawal’s study of Raika migrant camps shows how such groups make collective decisions about routes, negotiate with landowners and officials along the way, and balance the advantages of moving together against the costs of coordination (Agrawal 1999). In eastern Ladakh, finally, the Changpa move their goats, sheep and yaks between fixed seasonal campsites on the high Changthang plateau, at altitudes where cultivation is barely possible; their goats are discussed below (Section 13.14).

    13.9.2 Manure for Grazing¶

    Mobile herds and settled fields were bound together by an exchange of dung for fodder. As Chapter 10 describes, farmers in the Deccan, Rajasthan, Gujarat, the Tamil country and elsewhere invited shepherds to pen their flocks overnight on harvested or fallow fields, so that the animals’ dung and urine were deposited where they were needed; the herder was paid in grain, cash or food, or simply allowed to graze the stubble and field margins. Night-penning moved from field to field over successive nights, and a large flock could manure a whole holding in the weeks before sowing. For many herding households these payments were as important as the sale of animals, and the relationship between particular herders and particular villages often lasted for generations.

    The arrangement depended on a particular agrarian calendar: fields had to lie empty for a period between crops, and farmers had to value dung more than the herders’ animals cost them in damage. Both conditions have weakened. Where canal or tubewell irrigation allowed two or three crops a year, fields were seldom empty long enough for flocks; chemical fertilisers reduced the value of dung; and the enclosure of field boundaries and the spread of cash crops increased the occasions for conflict over trespass. It has been widely observed that relations between herders and farmers in many regions have shifted from reciprocity towards tolerance or hostility, although penning contracts continue wherever dry farming remains.

    13.9.3 The Erosion of Grazing Rights¶

    Before the nineteenth century, grazing in most regions was governed by custom: village pastures (gocara, in Hindi gocar or gauchar) were used by the village’s own animals, and passing herds negotiated access with village headmen or local lords, paying in kind or in dues. Several developments of the colonial and post-colonial periods narrowed this access.

    The first was forest law. The Indian Forest Acts of 1865, 1878 and 1927 (Chapter 11, Section 11.9) turned large areas of hill and forest grazing into state property, in which grazing was permitted only under licence, for fees, for limited numbers of animals and in limited seasons, and in which goats in particular were regarded by foresters as destructive and sometimes excluded (Gadgil and Guha 1992; Guha 1989). The second was the colonial preference for settled cultivators over mobile people. Revenue settlements recorded rights in land for those who cultivated it, while the claims of herders, which rested on seasonal use, were much harder to record (Ludden 1999); and the Criminal Tribes Act of 1871 placed a number of itinerant communities, including some groups of herders, carriers and traders, under police surveillance as “criminal” by birth — a stigma that long outlived the repeal of the Act in 1952.

    The third was irrigation. The canal colonies of western Punjab, created from the 1880s onwards, turned the bār uplands between the rivers — the grazing grounds of pastoral groups whom colonial officials called “Janglis” — into irrigated wheat and cotton land allotted mainly to peasant colonists from the older districts of central Punjab, to soldiers and to landed notables (Ali 1988). After independence, the Indira Gandhi Canal brought irrigation to large parts of the Thar and converted rangelands into fields. The fourth was the shrinkage of village commons. N. S. Jodha’s surveys of villages in the dry regions of India in the 1980s documented a marked decline in the area of common property resources after independence, much of it through privatisation and distribution of common land under land-reform and welfare programmes and through encroachment, and showed that the poorest households, who depended most on the commons for grazing, fuel and fodder, lost most (Jodha 1986; Agrawal 2001). Finally, the national parks and sanctuaries created from the 1970s excluded or restricted grazing in some of the best pastures (Chapter 16) (Rangarajan 2001).

    The Banni grasslands of Kachchh in Gujarat show several of these pressures at once. Banni, a low-lying plain on the northern edge of the Kachchh mainland bordering the Great Rann, was one of the largest grasslands in the subcontinent and is the home of mainly Muslim Maldhari pastoral communities, who raise the Banni buffalo and Kankrej cattle and supplied milk and animals to the region. Its legal status as forest land under the control of the state has long been at odds with the herders’ customary use. From the 1960s the forest department planted the South and Central American mesquite Prosopis juliflora — in Gujarati gāṇḍo bāval, “mad babul” — in and around Banni to check the spread of salinity and desert. The tree spread aggressively and has since invaded much of the former grassland, reducing the grass on which the herds depended; reduced freshwater inflows from upstream dams and increased salinity are also thought to have contributed to the decline of the pastures. Some Maldharis have adapted by making charcoal from Prosopis, and Maldhari organisations have sought recognition of community rights over the grassland. Banni is a case in which a well-intentioned ecological intervention by the state, made without the knowledge of the people who used the land, produced a lasting ecological problem.

    13.9.4 Herders’ Breeding Knowledge¶

    The regional breeds listed in Section 13.11 are the product of centuries of selection by herders and farmers, though the details of that selection were rarely written down. What ethnographers and animal scientists working with pastoral communities have recorded can be summarised under a few heads.

    Individual knowledge of animals. Herders commonly know each animal in their herd individually, by name, by colour and markings, and by its mother. Names are often descriptive — of colour, horn shape, temperament or place of purchase — and are passed on, so that a herder can recite the female lines of the herd over several generations. Among the Toda of the Nilgiris, whose buffaloes are bound up with their religion, ethnographers since the early twentieth century have recorded that buffaloes are named and their descent remembered. This genealogical memory is the basis of selection: a good mother is expected to produce good daughters and bulls.

    Selection of breeding males. Because a single bull, ram or male camel sires many offspring, herders paid particular attention to their choice. A breeding male was typically chosen from a mother of proven milk yield, hardiness or fertility, and judged by conformation, colour, gait and temperament; in many communities breeding males were shared within a lineage or village, and exchanged between herds to avoid inbreeding. A distinct and older institution was the release of a bull in a funerary rite (vṛṣotsarga): a young bull, branded and dedicated, was set free to roam and serve the village cows. Colonial officials, who called these animals “Brahmani bulls”, complained that they were often of poor quality; but where they were chosen well they served as village breeding bulls.

    Breed as community identity. For many herding communities a breed is bound up with the community’s own identity: the Raika with particular camel breeds, the Maldharis of Banni with the Banni buffalo, Gaddi shepherds with their sheep and goats. Some breeds that herders had long recognised were only recently registered as distinct breeds by the national authority, the National Bureau of Animal Genetic Resources at Karnal — among them the Banni buffalo and the Kharai camel of Kachchh, which grazes on mangroves and swims between the coastal islands where they grow. Livestock breeds, like crop landraces (Chapter 9), are collective creations whose survival depends on the survival of the communities and grazing systems that made them.

    13.10 Animal Shelters, Fairs and Markets¶

    Two institutions illustrate the different ways in which Indian society cared for and traded in animals. The first is the animal shelter. In Gujarat and Rajasthan, Jain and Hindu merchant communities maintained pāñjarāpoḷs (piñjrāpoḷ) and gośālās, where old, sick and unproductive cattle and other animals were kept until they died a natural death, financed by charitable endowments and levies on trade. European travellers of the seventeenth century were struck by such institutions, which applied the ethic of non-injury to animals that had ceased to be economically useful. Many still exist. They are best understood as religious charities; they have never been able to house more than a small fraction of the cattle population, and the fate of unproductive cattle has always been a difficult question in an economy where slaughter of cows is forbidden or restricted.

    The second institution is the animal fair. Great seasonal fairs, held at pilgrimage sites and river confluences, have for centuries been the main markets for cattle, horses, camels and elephants: the fair at Sonepur in Bihar, held at the full moon of Kārttika, was famous for its elephants and horses; that at Pushkar in Rajasthan for camels and cattle; and those at Nagaur and many other places for draught bullocks. At these fairs herders and breeders sold their young stock to cultivators, and the reputation of regional breeds was made and maintained. The fairs were also religious occasions, with bathing and worship, and centres of the exchange of information among herders, dealers and farmers.

    13.10.1 The Decline of Draught Power¶

    For most of Indian history animals supplied nearly all the power used in agriculture. From the 1960s onwards, tractors, power tillers, electric and diesel pumps and mechanical threshers began to replace bullocks, first in the Green Revolution regions of the north-west and then more widely. The result has been a profound change in the place of cattle in the farm economy: male calves of indigenous breeds have lost much of their value, dairying has become the main reason for keeping cattle, and the old integration of draught animals, dung and crop residues described in Chapter 10 has weakened. Camels in Rajasthan, once essential for ploughing and transport, have declined steeply in number as their work has been taken over by vehicles. These changes have consequences for breeds, for pastoral communities and for the many people whose livelihoods depended on working animals.

    13.11 Breeds¶

    Over many centuries farmers and herders shaped distinct regional breeds of zebu cattle, buffalo, sheep and goat adapted to local climates, fodder and work. India’s national registry today recognises about fifty breeds of cattle and a smaller number of buffalo breeds, as well as many breeds of sheep, goats, camels and poultry. Some of the best known are listed in Table 13.1. Characterisations are general; productivity varies greatly with management.

Indian zebu breeds were exported in the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries to the Americas, where they became the foundation of tropical cattle ranching: the Nelore of Brazil derives from Ongole cattle shipped from the Nellore region, and the American Brahman breed was developed from Gir, Kankrej, Ongole and other zebu stock. In India itself, the promotion of crossbreeding with European dairy breeds from the 1960s onwards, and the decline of draught use with mechanisation, have reduced many indigenous breeds; conservation programmes now seek to maintain them. The history of these state breeding efforts is the subject of the next section.

    13.12 Colonial and Modern Cattle Breeding¶

    13.12.1 Hissar and the Army’s Cattle¶

    Organised cattle breeding by the state in India began with the needs of the East India Company’s army, which depended on bullocks to draw its artillery and baggage. The government cattle farm at Hissar (now Hisar, in Haryana), founded in the early nineteenth century — its foundation is usually dated to 1809 — was established to breed draught bullocks and other transport animals for the army on the extensive grazing lands of the region. The farm worked with the local grey-white cattle of the Hariana tract, a breed valued for draught and for a reasonable yield of milk, and over the nineteenth and twentieth centuries it became the principal centre for the Hariana breed and a source of bulls distributed to villages for the improvement of local herds. In the south, the Amrit Mahal establishment of the Mysore rulers (Section 13.11) was taken over by the Company after the fall of Tipu Sultan in 1799 and maintained, with interruptions, for the bullocks of the Madras army. Draught power for the military, not milk, was the first object of the colonial state’s interest in Indian cattle.

    Milk for British troops created a second institution. Because European soldiers were thought to need fresh milk and butter of assured quality, the army established its own dairy farms; the first of these Military Farms is said to have been opened at Allahabad in 1889, and they grew into a network of farms attached to cantonments across India. In the twentieth century the Military Farms were among the first establishments in India to keep large herds of European dairy cattle and their crosses, and after independence they took part, with the Indian Council of Agricultural Research, in developing the Frieswal, a cross between the Holstein-Friesian and the Sahiwal. The Military Farms have recently been wound up, their dairy role having long been overtaken by civilian supply.

    13.12.2 Science, Surveys and the “Cattle Problem”¶

    In the late colonial period the condition of Indian cattle became an object of official investigation. The rinderpest campaigns and the veterinary institutions described in Section 13.6 gave the state a veterinary service; quinquennial livestock censuses were begun in 1919; and the Royal Commission on Agriculture in India (1926–28) devoted much attention to cattle, fodder and dairying. Official opinion tended to hold that India had too many cattle of too poor a quality — that large numbers of underfed and “uneconomic” animals competed for limited fodder — and that improvement required the selection of good bulls, the castration or removal of poor ones, better fodder and, where possible, the culling of unproductive animals. The last point brought officials into conflict with Hindu sentiment against slaughter, and the “cattle problem” became a recurrent theme of debates on agricultural policy. Indian critics, for their part, pointed out that what officials called surplus cattle were often the working capital of poor households, which fed them on crop residues and roadside grazing that had no other use. The same argument was later made in anthropology by Marvin Harris, whose article “The cultural ecology of India’s sacred cattle” (1966) argued that the large number of cattle, the ban on slaughter and the use of dung, traction, milk and hides formed an adapted system in which few animals were in fact useless (Harris 1966). Harris’s thesis started a long debate. His critics questioned his figures and argued that religious and political values could not be reduced to ecological function (Simoons 1979). The debate is a reminder that the economics of Indian cattle cannot be read either from official complaints or from religious ideals alone.

    The scientific institutions of dairying date from this period. An Imperial Institute of Animal Husbandry and Dairying was established at Bangalore in the 1920s; renamed the Imperial Dairy Research Institute in the 1930s, it became the National Dairy Research Institute after independence and moved its headquarters to Karnal in Haryana in the mid-1950s, keeping regional stations in the south and east. The Imperial (later Indian) Council of Agricultural Research, founded in 1929, came to coordinate research on animal husbandry as on crops.

    13.12.3 Crossbreeding after Independence¶

    After independence the improvement of cattle became part of national planning. The Key Village Scheme of the First Five-Year Plan (from 1952) set up centres for the selective breeding of local cattle with approved bulls and for artificial insemination, and later programmes extended artificial insemination widely. From the 1960s policy turned increasingly to crossbreeding the large population of non-descript (unclassified) cattle with European dairy breeds, chiefly the Jersey and the Holstein-Friesian, whose crossbred daughters could give much more milk under good feeding. A pioneering bilateral project, the Indo-Swiss project in Kerala begun in the 1960s, produced a crossbred population known as Sunandini from local cattle and the Brown Swiss and other European breeds. Frozen semen, liquid nitrogen and the dairy cooperatives of Operation Flood (Chapter 14) carried crossbreeding into villages throughout the country, especially in Kerala, Tamil Nadu, Punjab and other states with good markets for milk (George 1985). Official policy generally envisaged that recognised indigenous breeds would be bred pure in their home tracts while non-descript cattle were upgraded by crossing, but in practice the line was not always held.

    The result has been a large increase in milk production, in which crossbred cows and, above all, buffaloes have played a central part, and a corresponding decline in the numbers and purity of many indigenous cattle breeds. Since the 2010s government policy has given new emphasis to indigenous breeds, notably through the Rashtriya Gokul Mission launched in 2014, with breeding centres for native cattle. The debate over the two approaches is summarised below.

    Debate: Crossbred or Indigenous Cattle?

    Advocates of crossbreeding point out that crossbred cows give substantially more milk than most indigenous cows under the same reasonable management, that they have allowed small farmers to earn a living from one or two animals, and that dairy income has been one of the most widely shared gains of Indian agriculture since the 1970s. Advocates of indigenous breeds reply that crossbreds need better feed, housing and veterinary care than poor households can provide; that they are more susceptible to heat stress and to some tropical diseases and ticks; that the male calves of crossbreds are of little use for draught; and that indiscriminate crossing has diluted breeds adapted over centuries to Indian climates and work, which may be valuable in a hotter future. Both sides have good arguments, and much depends on the region, the market and the resources of the farmer. One argument should be set aside: the claim that the milk of indigenous cows is uniquely health-giving because it contains only the “A2” form of the protein beta-casein, while that of European breeds is harmful, has not been established; a review by the European Food Safety Authority in 2009 found no established cause-and-effect relationship between the relevant milk peptides and disease (European Food Safety Authority 2009).

    13.13 Other Livestock¶

    Buffaloes became the principal dairy animal of northern and western India, valued for the high fat content of their milk; they are also the main draught animal of wet-rice cultivation in many regions. Sheep and goats supplied meat, milk, wool, skins and, through penning on fields, manure; goats are the livestock of the poor and of the drylands. Caraka’s dietetics compares the milks of different animals, and goat’s milk is described as light and easily digestible (Sharma 1981). Sheep, goats and wool, camels, and poultry and pigs are treated more fully in the sections that follow.

    Dogs occupied an ambiguous place: they guarded herds and houses and hunted, and the Mahābhārata’s story of Yudhiṣṭhira refusing to enter heaven without the dog that had followed him is famous; but the dog was also a standard image of impurity, and the term śvapaca, “dog-cooker”, was used of the lowest outcastes. Several Indian hound breeds — the Mudhol hound of Karnataka and the Rajapalayam, Chippiparai and Kombai of Tamil Nadu — were developed for hunting and guarding.

    13.14 Sheep, Goats and Wool¶

    13.14.1 Shepherds and Blankets of the Deccan¶

    India’s sheep were, and mostly still are, animals of the drylands, kept for meat, manure and skins as much as for wool. Most Indian breeds produce coarse wool or hair rather than the fine fleece of the European Merino, and the wool of the peninsular breeds is largely coarse, hairy and dark. On the Deccan plateau sheep herding is the hereditary occupation of large shepherd castes — the Dhangar of Maharashtra, the Kuruba of Karnataka, the Kuruma and Golla of Andhra Pradesh and Telangana — many of whose members are now settled farmers or have left herding altogether. These communities have their own pastoral deities and shrines, such as Khandobā and Birobā in Maharashtra and Bīrappa and Mailāra in Karnataka, and their own histories of political and religious distinction: the Holkar rulers of Indore were of Dhangar origin, and the sixteenth-century Kannada poet-saint Kanakadāsa came from a Kuruba family.

    The characteristic product of the Deccani shepherds was the rough woollen blanket, kambaḷi in Kannada (kambal in Hindi), ghongaḍī in Marathi, woven on simple looms from the black, brown and grey wool of the flock. Shepherd families sheared the sheep, spun the wool and wove the blankets themselves or through weaving sub-groups within the shepherd castes, and sold them at weekly markets and fairs. The blanket served the herder as cloak, rain cape, bedding and mat, and was carried by farmers and labourers throughout the Deccan. The trade has declined with competition from mill-made blankets and synthetic fibres, though it survives in particular centres and has been supported by craft cooperatives. The weaving of wool and its place among India’s textile traditions are treated in Volume 17, Śilpa–Kāru.

    13.14.2 Rajasthan and the Himalaya¶

    Rajasthan has long been India’s largest producer of wool, and its breeds — among them the Chokla, Magra, Marwari, Malpura, Jaisalmeri, Nali and Pugal — were selected by Raika and other herders in the Thar and its margins. Their fleece is mostly of the coarse “carpet” type, and Bikaner became the centre of a wool trade supplying spinning mills and the carpet-weaving industry of northern India, which also uses imported wool. In the twentieth century government farms and research institutes, including the Hissar farm and the Central Sheep and Wool Research Institute at Avikanagar in Rajasthan, crossed Indian sheep with Merino and other fine-wooled breeds in the hope of producing apparel wool; new crossbred strains such as the Hissardale were developed, but fine-wool crossbreeding never displaced the indigenous breeds in the hands of herders, who valued hardiness, mobility and meat at least as much as fleece.

    In the western Himalaya, the wool of Gaddi and other hill flocks was spun and woven at home; the Gaddi’s characteristic dress, a long woollen coat (cola) bound at the waist with many turns of woollen rope (ḍorā), is made from it, as were the blankets and shawls of the Kullu and Kinnaur valleys.

    13.14.3 Goats¶

    Goats are the livestock of the poorest households and of land too dry or broken for cattle; because they browse shrubs and trees and need little capital, they are often called “the poor man’s cow”. Regional breeds include the large Jamunapari of the Etawah region in Uttar Pradesh, the Beetal of Punjab, the Barbari, the Sirohi and Marwari of Rajasthan and the small Black Bengal, valued for meat and for its skin. Colonial and later forest officials regarded the goat as an enemy of the forest and sought to restrict its grazing (Section 13.9.3); the goat’s defenders argued that degradation had many causes and that the goat was blamed because its owners were poor and politically weak (Saberwal 1999).

    13.14.4 Pashmina and the Kashmir Shawl¶

    The most valuable fibre produced by any Indian animal comes from the goats of the Changthang plateau in eastern Ladakh, adjoining Tibet. To survive winters of extreme cold the Changthangi goat grows a fine, soft undercoat beneath its outer hair. The Changpa herders, who keep these goats together with sheep and yaks, collect the undercoat in spring by combing as the animals moult; the raw fibre is called pashm (Persian, “wool”), and the fabric woven from it is pashmina. Similar fibre came from goats in western Tibet and Central Asia, and for centuries the raw pashm of Ladakh and western Tibet was carried through Leh to Kashmir, where it was cleaned, spun by hand, very largely by women, and woven into shawls. By the treaty of Tingmosgang concluded between Ladakh and Tibet in the later seventeenth century, Ladakh secured control over the trade in raw pashm from western Tibet, and the Kashmir weavers’ supply of fibre was a recurrent concern of the rulers of Kashmir.

    The Kashmir shawl was a luxury of the Mughal court: Abū’l-Faẓl records Akbar’s interest in shawls and the improvements he made to their manufacture (Abū’l-Faẓl 1873). Under the Afghan, Sikh and Dogra rulers of Kashmir the shawl industry was heavily taxed, and in the late eighteenth and nineteenth centuries the shawl became a fashion in Europe, where its designs were imitated by mechanised weavers at Paisley in Scotland, in France and elsewhere. The European market collapsed in the 1870s, with grave consequences for the Kashmiri weavers (Maskiell 2002). A still finer fibre, shahtūsh, came from the Tibetan antelope or chiru (Pantholops hodgsonii), which, unlike the pashmina goat, had to be killed for it; the trade in shahtoosh is now banned under international and Indian law. Pashmina remains the livelihood of the Changpa herders and of the Kashmiri spinners and weavers, though many Changpa families have moved to settled life near Leh. The textile history of the shawl is treated in Volume 17.

    13.15 Camels¶

    13.15.1 The Raika and Their Camels¶

    The one-humped dromedary is the animal of the Thar desert and of the drylands of Gujarat, Haryana and Punjab. It was bred above all by the Raika (Rabari), but also by other communities, including some Muslim pastoral groups of Kachchh and Sindh. According to a widely told Raika origin story, the god Śiva created the first camel to please Pārvatī and then created the first Raika to look after it; and the Raika, like many other Rajasthani communities, revere the folk hero Pābūjī, whose oral epic, sung by priest-bards before a painted cloth scroll, tells how he brought the first she-camels to Marwar. Camel breeding was thus not only an occupation but the core of a community’s identity.

    Raika herds grazed on the common lands and village sacred groves (oran, Chapter 11) of western Rajasthan and moved with the seasons in search of browse. Researchers working with Raika breeders have reported that the community traditionally regarded the sale of female camels outside the community, and the sale of camel milk, as improper: milk was drunk by the herders and given away rather than sold, and it was male camels that were sold, chiefly at the great fairs such as Pushkar (Section 13.10). Breeders distinguish regional types, now recognised as breeds — the Bikaneri, Jaisalmeri, Marwari, Mewari and Kachchhi, and the Kharai of the Kachchh coast mentioned above — and select breeding males with care. The Indian Council of Agricultural Research maintains a research centre on the camel at Bikaner.

    13.15.2 Work, War and Milk¶

    For centuries camels carried the goods of the desert trade, drew ploughs in the sandy fields of the Thar, lifted water from deep wells and pulled carts in towns and villages. Their hair was made into ropes, bags and rugs, and their hides were worked; the Bikaner craft of decorating camel-hide vessels and lamps with gilded and painted designs (usta work) belongs to the craft history treated in Volume 17. Camels also served in war. The Bikaner Camel Corps, known as the Ganga Risala after Mahārājā Gaṅgā Siṃh of Bikaner (r. 1887–1943), served as one of the Imperial Service contingents of the princely states and was sent overseas, to China in 1900 and to Egypt during the First World War; mounted camel patrols remain in service with the Border Security Force on the Thar frontier. The military history of animals is treated in Volume 20, Yuddha.

    Camel milk was a staple of the herders’ own diet, drunk fresh or soured; it is difficult to make into butter by traditional churning, which partly explains why it never entered the ghee-based dairy economy of settled India. In recent years camel milk has begun to be collected and marketed by dairies in Gujarat and Rajasthan, and claims have been made for its value in particular diseases; such claims are under investigation and should not be treated as established.

    13.15.3 Decline and the Law of 2015¶

    The steep decline of camel numbers in Rajasthan over recent decades has been widely reported in successive livestock censuses. Its causes include the replacement of camels by tractors and motor vehicles, the loss of grazing to irrigation, enclosure and the restriction of grazing in forests and sanctuaries, and the declining interest of young people in a hard and poorly rewarded occupation. In 2014 the Government of Rajasthan declared the camel its state animal, and in 2015 it passed the Rajasthan Camel (Prohibition of Slaughter and Regulation of Temporary Migration or Export) Act, which banned slaughter and restricted the movement of camels out of the state. Breeders and organisations working with them have argued that the restrictions on sale and movement destroyed much of the market for male camels, which had been the herders’ main source of income, and so accelerated rather than halted the decline. The camel’s case illustrates a general point of this chapter: a breed survives only if the people who keep it can make a living from it.

    13.16 Poultry and Pigs¶

    13.16.1 Poultry¶

    Chickens descend from the red junglefowl of the forests of South and Southeast Asia (Section 13.2). For most of Indian history they were kept in small numbers, scavenging around the house, by Adivasi, Dalit, Muslim and many other rural households, and often belonged to and were managed by women; eggs and birds supplied food, a little cash, and offerings in many Adivasi and village rituals. Many high-caste Hindu and Jain communities avoided domestic fowl and eggs as impure, and the chicken was in this sense a marker of caste and community as much as an economic resource. Since the 1970s an industrial poultry sector based on imported hybrid broiler and layer strains has grown very rapidly, and chicken has become the most widely eaten meat in India; the indigenous “desi” bird nevertheless remains important in tribal and poor households, where its hardiness and its meat, preferred by many consumers, give it a value beyond its low productivity. Among recognised indigenous breeds are the Kadaknath of the Jhabua region of western Madhya Pradesh, kept by Bhil and Bhilala households and known for its black skin and flesh, which has been granted a geographical indication.

    The Aseel (Asil, from Arabic aṣīl, “of noble descent, pure-bred”) is a heavy, upright, hard-feathered game fowl bred in India for fighting. Aseel stock taken to Britain in the nineteenth century is thought to have contributed to the breed known as the Indian Game or Cornish, itself an ancestor of modern broiler chickens. Cockfighting has a long history in India: the list of arts in the Kāmasūtra includes the staging of fights between rams, cocks and quails. Today it is most prominent in the coastal districts of Andhra Pradesh, where fights are staged on a large scale during the Sankranti festival in January, with blades tied to the birds’ legs and heavy betting; it also survives in Tulu-speaking coastal Karnataka (kōri kaṭṭa), associated with local shrine festivals, and in parts of Tamil Nadu, Odisha and the North-East. Inciting animals to fight is an offence under the Prevention of Cruelty to Animals Act of 1960, and courts have repeatedly directed the authorities to stop the Sankranti fights, with limited effect. The tradition thus sets the claims of local custom and festival against the law and the welfare of the birds.

    13.16.2 Pigs¶

    Pigs, descended from the native wild boar, were kept across India by communities outside or at the margins of the caste order: by many Adivasi peoples of central and eastern India, by communities throughout the North-East, and by particular Dalit castes in the towns and villages of the north and elsewhere, for whom pig-rearing was both a livelihood and a further mark of the stigma attached to them by caste Hindus and Muslims, both of whom regarded the pig as impure. Village pigs were mostly small, dark, hardy scavengers that lived on waste and household refuse, and so also performed a sanitary function for which their keepers received little recognition. In the North-East, by contrast, pork is the most valued meat, pigs are fed with care on household and garden produce, and their slaughter marks marriages, funerals and the feasts by which households and lineages gain prestige; among several Naga and other hill peoples the semi-domesticated mithun (Bos frontalis), a bovine not milked but kept as wealth and slaughtered at the greatest feasts, holds a still higher place. Pig-keeping in the North-East suffered heavy losses from African swine fever, first reported in India in Assam in 2020.

    Summary¶

    South Asia was a centre of animal domestication, above all of the humped zebu. In Vedic society cattle were wealth and were also sacrificed and eaten; the inviolability of the cow developed over the first millennium CE alongside, but distinct from, the ideal of vegetarianism, and became a mass political cause in modern times. The Arthaśāstra and Aśoka’s edicts show state management of herds and royal concern for animals. Most practical veterinary knowledge lay with herders and village healers and remains largely undocumented. Pastoral peoples created the regional breeds that are among India’s most valuable agricultural legacies, while forest law, enclosure and mechanisation have narrowed their livelihoods. The state bred cattle first for the army and then for milk, and crossbreeding raised yields while eroding indigenous breeds. Much of the livestock economy lay with shepherd castes, Adivasi, Dalit and other marginal communities, and the handling of dead cattle and hides was imposed on Dalits as a mark of exclusion.
