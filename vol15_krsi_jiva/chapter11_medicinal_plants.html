    Chapter 12: Medicinal Plants: Identity, Cultivation, Trade and Conservation¶

    India’s medical traditions — Āyurveda, Siddha, Unani, Sowa-Rigpa and the many unwritten traditions of village healers and Adivasi communities — draw most of their materials from plants. This chapter treats those plants as plants. It asks what species a classical name refers to, where the plants grow, how they are cultivated or collected, how they are traded and substituted, what roles they play in religious and everyday life, and how their wild populations are faring. It does not discuss what the plants are used to treat, how they are prepared or in what doses: those are questions of therapeutic doctrine and clinical evidence.

    Cross-Reference: Therapeutics in Volume 13

    The doctrine of substances and their actions (dravyaguṇa), the classical uses of the plants named here, formulations, and the modern clinical evidence for and against them are treated in Volume 13, Āyurveda, including its chapters on dravyaguṇa and on Siddha and Unani. Nothing in the present chapter should be read as a recommendation to use any plant as a medicine.

    The chapter is concerned with the botany, cultivation, trade, cultural history and conservation of the plants, and it quotes no verse that cannot be checked in an edition (Section 1.5).

    12.1 The Scale of the Classical Materia Medica¶

    The classical medical texts name several hundred plants. The fourth chapter of the Sūtrasthāna of the Caraka-saṃhitā arranges five hundred drugs, most of them plants, into fifty groups of ten, each defined by an action, and Caraka presents these lists as illustrative rather than exhaustive (Sharma 1981). The Suśruta-saṃhitā arranges its drugs into a different set of named groups (gaṇa) (Bhishagratna 1907), and the later lexicons (nighaṇṭu) expand the number of plants considerably, adding new species and regional names (Chapter 6). Large ethnobotanical compilations of the twentieth century record thousands more plants used in folk and tribal medicine (Jain 1991). How many distinct species all these names represent is unknown, because many names are synonyms and many identifications are uncertain.

    12.1.1 Named groups¶

    Some plants are so often used together that their combinations have become names in their own right. Three are particularly well known and illustrate the range of botanical problems involved.

    Triphalā, “the three fruits”, is the combination of the dried fruits of āmalakī (Phyllanthus emblica, syn. Emblica officinalis, the Indian gooseberry or amla), harītakī (Terminalia chebula, the chebulic myrobalan) and vibhītakī (Terminalia bellirica, the belleric myrobalan). All three are trees of India’s deciduous forests; their fruits are gathered largely from the wild and from village trees, and the two Terminalia fruits, under the trade name “myrobalans”, were also an important colonial export for tanning and dyeing (Watt 1889). A similar triad, trikaṭu, “the three pungents”, combines dried ginger (śuṇṭhī), black pepper (marica) and long pepper (pippalī, Piper longum).

    Daśamūla, “the ten roots”, combines the roots of five trees (the “greater five roots”, bṛhat pañcamūla) and five smaller plants (the “lesser five roots”, laghu pañcamūla), listed in Table 12.1. The Sanskrit names are fixed, but the identity of several members varies by region, and two of the trees are disputed. Because the group requires the roots of trees, it is also one of the most destructive formulations to supply from the wild.

    Aṣṭavarga, “the group of eight”, consists of eight plants — jīvaka, ṛṣabhaka, medā, mahāmedā, kākolī, kṣīrakākolī, ṛddhi and vṛddhi — described as growing in the Himalaya and used as ingredients of tonic preparations, of which the best known is cyavanaprāśa. Their identities are among the most uncertain in the whole materia medica. Proposed identifications include small terrestrial orchids (species of Malaxis and Habenaria), species of Polygonatum, and members of the lily family such as Lilium and Fritillaria; none of these is secure. The problem is old. The Bhāvaprakāśa (sixteenth century) is commonly cited as remarking that the group was very hard to obtain, and it permits substitutes taken from commoner plants, among them śatāvarī, vidārī and aśvagandhā. Commercial preparations today generally use such substitutes, while the candidate Himalayan species are themselves under pressure from collection for the trade.

    12.2 Identity, Substitution and Adulteration¶

    A medicinal plant in trade is rarely seen whole. It reaches the buyer as a root, a piece of bark, a dried fruit, a resin or a powder, often after passing through several hands. Three distinct problems arise, and they are often confused.

    Disputed identity. A classical name may be applied to different species in different regions, as with brāhmī, śaṅkhapuṣpī and rāsnā (Chapter 6). Such regional differences are often long established and are a part of the history of the tradition, not simply errors.

    Authorised substitution. The classical texts themselves allow substitutes (pratinidhi) when a plant is unavailable. The Bhāvaprakāśa gives lists of such substitutes, of which the aṣṭavarga replacements are an example. Substitution is also driven by scarcity in the market. The bitter Himalayan herb kirātatikta (chiretta, Swertia chirayita), for instance, is widely replaced or mixed with the cheaper kālamegha (Andrographis paniculata), a lowland plant known in Tamil as nilavēmpu.

    Adulteration and contamination. Material may be mixed with cheaper plant parts, with other species, or with non-plant matter; and finished products may be contaminated. Turmeric powder, for example, has repeatedly been found adulterated with synthetic dyes such as metanil yellow, and in parts of South Asia with lead chromate added to brighten its colour. The latter is a serious public health hazard.

    The response of modern pharmacognosy has been to define each drug by its source species and by standards of identity and purity. The colonial dictionaries began this work by attaching Latin binomials to Indian names (Watt 1889; Kirtikar and Basu 1935); the official Ayurvedic Pharmacopoeia of India, published by the Government of India in successive volumes, now sets monographs for individual plant drugs; and techniques such as microscopy, chemical fingerprinting and DNA barcoding are used to check trade samples. None of these removes the underlying historical problem: deciding which species a classical author meant often remains a matter of scholarly judgement.

    12.3 Some Important Plants¶

    The following short accounts treat fifteen plants that are prominent in Indian medicine and in Indian life more broadly. The first eight are household names in India today; the other seven have mattered above all in trade, in industry or in the history of modern pharmacology, and several of them are now scarce in the wild. Sandalwood, which belongs to both groups, is treated with the other aromatics in Chapter 5. Each account covers the plant’s botanical identity, the part used, how it is grown or collected, its cultural roles, and, where relevant, its conservation status. Their therapeutic uses are left to Volume 13.

    12.3.1 Tulasī¶

    Tulasī, holy basil (Ocimum tenuiflorum, syn. O. sanctum; Lamiaceae), is a small aromatic shrub, usually under a metre tall, found throughout India and widely grown in South and Southeast Asia. Two forms are commonly distinguished: a purple-tinged form, called kṛṣṇa or śyāma tulasī, and a green form, often called rāma or śrī tulasī. The leaves are the part chiefly used, and the seeds swell into a mucilage when soaked. The plant grows easily from seed, is not threatened, and is now also grown commercially as a field crop for leaf and essential oil.

    Tulasī is above all a sacred plant. In Vaiṣṇava devotion it is regarded as a form of the goddess and beloved of Viṣṇu; its leaves are offered in worship, its stems are made into beads for rosaries, and a tulasī plant is grown in a raised masonry planter (tulasī-vṛndāvana) in the courtyard of many Hindu homes and temples. The annual “marriage of tulasī” (tulasī-vivāha) to Viṣṇu, celebrated in the month of Kārttika, marks in many regions the start of the season of weddings. Popular claims that the courtyard tulasī was designed as a form of pest management or that it noticeably enriches the household’s air are not supported by evidence.

    12.3.2 Nimba (neem)¶

    Neem (Azadirachta indica; Meliaceae), Sanskrit nimba, also ariṣṭa and picumarda, is a large, fast-growing, nearly evergreen tree of dry tropical regions. Its exact native range is uncertain — the dry zones of the Indian subcontinent and Myanmar are usually proposed — but it has been planted across India for so long, and more recently across Africa, the Middle East and the Americas, that its wild distribution is hard to separate from cultivation (Schmutterer 1995). Leaves, bark, twigs, flowers, fruit, seed oil and the seed cake left after pressing are all used, and all are intensely bitter.

    Neem has an unusually wide range of everyday uses. Twigs are chewed as tooth-cleaning sticks (dātun); dried leaves are put among stored grain, clothes and books to deter insects; the seed oil is used for lamps and soap; the seed cake is used as manure; and the tree is planted for shade along roads and in villages. In Karnataka, Andhra Pradesh, Telangana and Maharashtra the bitter neem flower or leaf, eaten with sweet jaggery at the new year (Ugādi, Guḍhī Pāḍvā), stands for the mixture of sweetness and bitterness in the year ahead. Neem is also venerated as a sacred tree and is associated in many places with goddesses such as Śītalā and Māriyammaṉ.

    In the twentieth century neem became a model case of plant-based pest control. The limonoid azadirachtin, isolated from the seed in 1968, acts on insects as a feeding deterrent and growth regulator, and neem-seed products are used in integrated pest management (Chapter 10) (Schmutterer 1995). Since 2015 the Government of India has required urea fertiliser sold in the country to be coated with neem oil, mainly to slow the release of nitrogen and to discourage the diversion of subsidised urea to industrial use. Neem was also at the centre of one of the first international disputes over patents and traditional knowledge, discussed in Chapter 20.

    12.3.3 Haridrā (turmeric)¶

    Turmeric (Curcuma longa; Zingiberaceae), Sanskrit haridrā, also poetically niśā and rajanī, is a perennial herb grown for its orange-yellow rhizome. The cultivated plant is sterile and is propagated entirely from pieces of rhizome. It is planted at the start of the monsoon and harvested some seven to nine months later; the rhizomes are traditionally boiled, dried in the sun and polished before sale, and the colour comes from a group of pigments, the curcuminoids. India is by far the world’s largest producer and consumer, and the crop is grown in many states, with major markets at Erode in Tamil Nadu, Sangli in Maharashtra and Nizamabad in Telangana. Many local varieties are recognised, and some, such as Lakadong turmeric from Meghalaya, are sold under their place names.

    Turmeric is a spice, a dye and a cosmetic as well as a drug. Its colour makes it a sign of auspiciousness: the bride and groom are anointed with turmeric paste before a Hindu wedding in many regions; ritual threads and rice are coloured with it; and kuṅkuma, the red powder used for the forehead mark, has traditionally been made by treating turmeric with lime. The revocation in 1997 of a United States patent on the use of turmeric in wound healing is discussed in Chapter 20. The clinical evidence on turmeric and curcumin, which is weaker than popular accounts suggest, is a matter for Volume 13.

    12.3.4 Guggulu¶

    Guggulu (Indian bdellium) is the oleo-gum resin of Commiphora wightii (syn. C. mukul; Burseraceae), a small, thorny, slow-growing shrub or tree of the arid and semi-arid zones of western India, especially Rajasthan and Gujarat (including Kachchh), and of adjoining Pakistan. The resin is obtained by cutting the bark, from which it oozes and hardens. Guggulu is also burnt as incense, and it is praised for its fragrance already in the Atharvaveda (Whitney 1905).

    The plant is now seriously threatened. Commercial demand has led to heavy and repeated tapping, often with deep cuts that kill the plant, and its slow growth and poor natural regeneration mean that wild stands have declined sharply; the species has been assessed as Critically Endangered on the IUCN Red List. Forest departments and research institutions in Rajasthan and Gujarat have run programmes of nursery propagation from cuttings and of replanting, and less damaging methods of tapping have been advocated.

    Guggulu is also an instructive case in the history of modern research on Indian plant drugs. Work begun in the 1960s, notably by G. V. Satyavati, led to a standardised extract, guggulipid, developed at the Central Drug Research Institute, Lucknow, and marketed in India as a lipid-lowering drug. When guggulipid was tested in a randomised, placebo-controlled trial in the United States, however, it did not lower LDL cholesterol, and some participants developed skin rashes (Szapary et al. 2003). The case shows why early positive reports of a traditional plant’s effect need independent replication.

    12.3.5 Aśvagandhā¶

    Aśvagandhā (Withania somnifera; Solanaceae), whose name means “smelling of horse” after the odour of its fresh root, is a small, erect, woody-based shrub of dry, open ground, distributed from the Mediterranean and Africa through western and southern Asia. The root is the part chiefly used. Unlike many medicinal plants, aśvagandhā is now supplied largely by cultivation: it is grown as a rain-fed crop on a large scale in the Mandsaur and Neemuch districts of western Madhya Pradesh and adjoining parts of Rajasthan, and roots are graded in the trade by thickness and origin, with names such as “Nagori” (after Nagaur in Rajasthan) used for particular grades. Demand has risen steeply with its international sale as a dietary supplement, raising questions of quality control, of the substitution of aerial parts for root, and of the plant’s safety in unregulated products, which are matters for Volume 13.

    12.3.6 Śatāvarī¶

    Śatāvarī (Asparagus racemosus; Asparagaceae) is a spiny climbing asparagus of forests and scrub throughout much of India, with clusters of fleshy, spindle-shaped tuberous roots, which are the part used. Its synonym śatamūlī, “having a hundred roots”, describes these clusters; the popular gloss of śatāvarī as “she who has a hundred husbands” is a folk etymology. Harvesting the roots kills or severely damages the plant, and heavy collection from the wild for the herbal trade has depleted it in many areas. It is now cultivated in several states, and cultivation is one of the plants promoted under national programmes for medicinal plants, but a large share of the trade is still thought to come from wild collection.

    12.3.7 Guḍūcī¶

    Guḍūcī (Tinospora cordifolia; Menispermaceae), Hindi giloy, is a large deciduous climber of forests, hedges and village trees across India, with heart-shaped leaves, a corky stem and long, thread-like aerial roots. The stem is the part chiefly used. Its Sanskrit synonyms include amṛtā, “deathless”, and chinnaruhā, “growing again when cut”, which records the plant’s ability to regenerate from pieces of stem — one reason why it is easy to propagate and is not considered threatened. Tradition prefers plants that have climbed on a neem tree (nimba-guḍūcī).

    Guḍūcī became a mass-market product during the COVID-19 pandemic, when it was widely promoted in India as an “immunity” remedy. In 2021 hepatologists in India reported cases of liver injury in people who had taken guḍūcī products (Nagral et al. 2021). The Ministry of AYUSH disputed the attribution and suggested that the products might have contained related species, such as Tinospora crispa, rather than T. cordifolia. Whichever explanation is correct, the episode illustrates the theme of this chapter: the safety of a plant product depends both on the plant and on whether the product actually contains the plant named on the label.

    12.3.8 Brāhmī¶

    Brāhmī is the clearest example of a name with two plants. The Ayurvedic Pharmacopoeia of India, the practice of Kerala and that of much of the rest of the country take it to be Bacopa monnieri (Plantaginaceae; Malayalam brāhmi), a small, succulent creeping herb of marshes, pond margins and wet fields. In Bengal and parts of north India, and in some of the medical literature, the name is given instead to Centella asiatica (Apiaceae; Bengali thānkuni), a creeping herb of moist, shady ground with kidney-shaped leaves, which is also the classical maṇḍūkaparṇī, “frog-leaf”. The two plants belong to different families and look quite different. Classical texts mention both brāhmī and maṇḍūkaparṇī, but which name originally referred to which plant, and whether the classical authors always distinguished them, is debated (Sivarajan and Balachandran 1994). Usage also varies within regions, so that the identity of the plant in any particular product has to be checked rather than assumed. Both plants are collected from the wild in large quantities, and both are now cultivated; because both grow in wetlands, their wild supply is affected by the draining and pollution of ponds and paddy margins as much as by collection.

    12.3.9 Sarpagandhā¶

    Sarpagandhā (Rauvolfia serpentina, also spelled Rauwolfia; Apocynaceae), Indian snakeroot, Hindi chhota chand, is a small, erect, evergreen undershrub, usually well under a metre tall, with glossy leaves in whorls, small white or pinkish flowers and fleshy fruits that ripen purplish-black. It grows in the shade of moist forests in the sub-Himalayan tract, in eastern and north-eastern India and in the Western and Eastern Ghats, and it extends to Sri Lanka and Southeast Asia. The part used is the root, a twisted, greyish-brown taproot with an intensely bitter taste. The genus was named by the French botanist Charles Plumier in honour of Leonhard Rauwolf, a sixteenth-century German physician who travelled in the Levant. The Sanskrit name means “smelling of (or like) a snake”; why the plant received it is not certain. The root had a long reputation in Indian folk and learned medicine, which the colonial compilers recorded (Kirtikar and Basu 1935).

    Sarpagandhā is the plant through which Indian medicine made its largest single contribution to twentieth-century pharmacology. In 1931 the chemists Salimuzzaman Siddiqui and Rafat Hussain Siddiqui, working in Delhi, isolated a group of crystalline alkaloids from the root, one of which they named ajmaline after the physician Hakim Ajmal Khan; in the same year the Calcutta physicians Gananath Sen and K. C. Bose published an account of the root’s use in insanity and high blood pressure. Through the 1940s Indian physicians reported their clinical experience with the drug, and a paper by the Bombay cardiologist Rustom Jal Vakil in the British Heart Journal in 1949 brought it to international attention (Vakil 1949). In 1952 chemists of the Swiss firm Ciba in Basel isolated the alkaloid chiefly responsible for the effect on blood pressure, which they named reserpine. Reserpine was quickly adopted in Europe and North America as one of the first effective drugs for hypertension and, for some years, as a tranquillizer in psychiatry; R. B. Woodward announced a total synthesis of it in 1956. Its use declined from the 1960s as its side effects, notably depression, became clear and newer drugs became available. The episode remains a landmark in the history of research on the plants of Indian medicine (Volume 13).

    The sudden international demand of the 1950s was met largely from wild roots, since the plant had hardly been cultivated, and wild stands were heavily depleted over much of its range. Collection of the root kills the plant. Cultivation has been promoted since then, but it is hampered by the poor and uneven germination of the seed, and the plant is also raised from root and stem cuttings. Sarpagandhā is listed in Appendix II of CITES (Section 12.8).

    12.3.10 Pippalī¶

    Pippalī, long pepper (Piper longum; Piperaceae), is a slender perennial that creeps and climbs in hot, moist forests; it grows wild in north-eastern India, the eastern Himalayan foothills and the Western Ghats, and in neighbouring countries. Black pepper bears its fruits separately along a hanging spike; in long pepper the minute fruits are fused into a cylindrical, catkin-like spike a few centimetres long, and it is these spikes, picked when nearly mature and dried in the sun, that form the spice and the drug. The thicker roots and stem bases are traded separately as pippalīmūla. The plant is cultivated on a small scale in several states, among them Assam and Kerala, and is propagated from stem cuttings. With dried ginger and black pepper it makes up the classical triad trikaṭu (Section 12.1).

    Long pepper has an unusually well-documented history in the west. The English word “pepper” goes back, through Latin piper and Greek peperi, to a Middle Indo-Aryan form of pippalī. In Rome long pepper was the costliest of the peppers: Pliny the Elder gives its price as well above that of white or black pepper. It remained a familiar spice in the medieval Islamic world, where Arabic calls it dār fulful, and in medieval Europe, but it fell out of European use in the early modern period; the spread of the American chilli is usually given as one reason. In India the chilli likewise displaced it as a source of pungency in cooking (Chapter 9), and today it is used mainly as a drug and in a few regional dishes and spice mixtures.

    12.3.11 Kuṣṭha¶

    Kuṣṭha, costus or kuth (Saussurea costus, syn. S. lappa; Asteraceae), is a tall perennial herb of moist slopes in the high valleys of Kashmir and the western Himalaya, at altitudes of very roughly 2,500 to 3,500 metres. Its thick, aromatic root, with a strong and persistent odour, is the part used. Two confusions should be avoided: the Sanskrit word kuṣṭha also names a group of skin diseases, including leprosy, which has nothing to do with the plant; and the trade name “costus” has nothing to do with the tropical genus Costus (spiral gingers).

    Kuṣṭha is one of the few medicinal plants with a long Vedic history. Several hymns of the Atharvaveda address it as a divine plant born high in the mountains and invoke it, among other things, against the fever called takman (Whitney 1905). It was also among the Indian aromatics best known in the Graeco-Roman world, where it was used in perfumes and incense (Section 12.5), and in Arabic medicine as qusṭ. In China the root, under the name muxiang, became an important drug, and the plant is cultivated in south-western China to supply it. In India the root has been burnt as incense, used in hair washes and, by report, laid among woollens and shawls to keep moths away.

    Colonial accounts describe the collection and sale of kuth in Kashmir as controlled by the state. In the twentieth century farmers in the Lahaul valley of Himachal Pradesh took up kuth as a cash crop on their small high-altitude fields. Wild kuṣṭha, which is collected by digging out the root, has meanwhile become rare. Besides its CITES listing (Section 12.8), it has been listed among the “specified plants” of India’s Wildlife (Protection) Act, 1972, whose cultivation, possession and trade require a licence.

    12.3.12 Jaṭāmāṃsī¶

    Jaṭāmāṃsī, spikenard (Nardostachys jatamansi; Caprifoliaceae, formerly placed in the Valerianaceae), is a small perennial herb of alpine slopes and meadows of the Himalaya, from Uttarakhand eastwards through Nepal, Sikkim and Bhutan to Tibet and south-western China, mostly above about 3,000 metres. Its short, thick rhizome is wrapped in a dense mat of reddish-brown fibres, the remains of old leaf bases, which give the plant its Sanskrit name, “matted-hair flesh”. The aromatic rhizome, and the essential oil distilled from it, are the products traded.

    Jaṭāmāṃsī is generally identified with the nard (Greek nardos, Latin nardus) of the ancient Mediterranean, the most precious of the Indian aromatics in the Roman perfume trade. The Periplus Maris Erythraei, a Greek merchant’s handbook of the first century CE, lists nard among the exports of Barbarikon, at the mouth of the Indus, and of Barygaza (Bharuch), and also mentions a nard from the Ganges region (Casson 1989); Pliny describes the plant and its high price; and in the Gospels a woman anoints Jesus with costly ointment of nard (Mark 14:3; John 12:3). Several aromatic plants were traded under the name “nard” in antiquity, so not every ancient reference need concern this species, but the identification of the costly Indian nard of the Roman trade with Nardostachys is generally accepted. In Arabic and Persian it is sunbul.

    Collection kills the plant, which grows slowly in a harsh environment, and heavy demand from the perfume and herbal trades, met largely from Nepal, has depleted wild populations; the species is listed in Appendix II of CITES (Section 12.8).

    12.3.13 Kirātatikta¶

    Kirātatikta, chiretta (Swertia chirayita; Gentianaceae), is an erect annual or biennial herb of the temperate Himalaya, from Kashmir to Bhutan and Arunachal Pradesh, most abundant in Nepal, at altitudes of roughly 1,500 to 3,000 metres. The whole plant is gathered when in flower or fruit, dried and sold in flat bundles. Its name, “the bitter (plant) of the Kirātas”, refers to the hill peoples of the Himalaya, and it is among the most intensely bitter plants of the Indian materia medica, a quality it shares with its European relatives, the gentians. Chiretta entered British pharmacy in the nineteenth century (Watt 1889) and was for a time official in the British Pharmacopoeia. Because the whole plant is harvested, often before its seed has ripened, the wild stock has declined in many accessible areas, and cultivation has been encouraged in Nepal and in the Indian Himalaya. Its scarcity and price explain the substitution of other, less bitter Swertia species and of kālamegha described above (Section 12.2).

    12.3.14 Vacā¶

    Vacā, sweet flag (Acorus calamus; Acoraceae), is a perennial, iris-like plant of marshes, pond margins and stream banks, with long sword-shaped leaves and a creeping, branched, aromatic rhizome, which is the part used. Despite its English name it is not an iris; it belongs to an early-diverging lineage of monocots placed in a family of its own. The species is widespread in the northern hemisphere; in India it grows wild in wetlands from the Himalaya southwards and is cultivated on a small scale, propagated from pieces of rhizome. It appears among the root medicines that the Pāli Vinaya permits to Buddhist monks, together with turmeric and ginger, and the dried rhizome has long been laid among stored grain and clothes against insects. In Europe it was used in perfumery and to flavour liqueurs and bitters.

    Sweet flag is also an example of a plant whose chemistry varies with its genetic make-up. Most Indian forms differ in chromosome number from the North American plant and contain much more \(\beta\)-asarone, a constituent of the essential oil that causes tumours in laboratory animals; partly for this reason the United States Food and Drug Administration has prohibited calamus as an ingredient of food since 1968. Like brāhmī, vacā is threatened less by collection than by the draining of the wetlands in which it grows.

    12.3.15 Mañjiṣṭhā¶

    Mañjiṣṭhā, Indian madder (Rubia cordifolia; Rubiaceae), Hindi majīṭh, is a perennial climber of hill forests across India, from the Himalaya to the southern hills, with rough four-angled stems bearing small backward-pointing prickles, heart-shaped leaves in whorls and long red roots. The roots, and in trade often the stems as well, are the parts used, both as a drug and, historically more important, as a dye. Its colouring matters, anthraquinones such as purpurin and munjistin (the latter named from the Indian name of the plant), bind to cotton with the help of mordants: with alum they give reds, with iron purples and blacks.

    Madder dyes, from this species and from the related West Asian and European Rubia tinctorum, gave the reds of many Indian dyed and printed cottons, although on the Coromandel coast the root of chay (Oldenlandia umbellata) was preferred for the finest painted chintz. A fragment of cotton cloth from Mohenjo-daro has often been reported to carry a madder dye, though the analysis is old. The trade in madder roots, in India as in Europe, collapsed after the German chemists Carl Graebe and Carl Liebermann synthesised alizarin, the main colouring matter of European madder, in 1868, and synthetic dyes took over the market. Indian textile dyeing is treated in Volume 17, Śilpa–Kāru. Mañjiṣṭhā is widespread and, unlike the Himalayan root drugs, is not generally regarded as threatened.

    12.4 Collection and Trade¶

    Most medicinal plant material in India has long been collected from forests, wastelands, field margins and village commons rather than cultivated. Collection is done mainly by Adivasi and other rural households, often as part of a wider economy of “minor” or non-timber forest produce; the material passes through local traders and commission agents to wholesale markets in the larger cities and then to manufacturers of Āyurvedic and other products, to the spice and food trade, and to exporters. Under the Forest Rights Act of 2006 such produce is formally recognised as the property of forest-dwelling communities, but in practice collectors usually receive only a small part of the final price, and the trade is poorly documented. Reliable figures for the volume of wild collection, and for the numbers of species used and threatened, are scarce, and widely repeated estimates should be treated with caution.

    This structure explains several features of the trade. Plants whose roots, bark or whole bodies are harvested — daśamūla trees, śatāvarī, the Himalayan root drugs — suffer far more than those whose leaves, fruits or resins are taken, provided the latter are harvested with care. Demand is distant from supply, so price signals of scarcity reach collectors late. And because material is traded dried and broken, identity is hard to check, which encourages substitution and adulteration (Section 12.2). The longer history of the trade in Indian plant drugs is outlined in the next section.

    12.5 Indian Drug Plants in World Trade¶

    Many of the plants described in this chapter were known far beyond India long before modern pharmacology, because their dried roots, rhizomes, resins and fruits were light, durable and valuable enough to be carried over long distances. The maritime and overland trade of which they formed a part, and the monsoon navigation on which it depended, are treated in Volume 19, Vāṇijya and Samudra; this section follows only the plants.

    12.5.1 Westward: the Roman and Islamic worlds¶

    The fullest ancient list comes from the Periplus Maris Erythraei, a Greek handbook for merchants sailing from Roman Egypt to Arabia and India, written in the first century CE (Casson 1989). Among the exports it lists from Barbarikon, at the mouth of the Indus, are costus, bdellium (the resin of the guggulu shrub), lycium (an extract of Berberis species, Sanskrit rasāñjana) and nard; from Barygaza (Bharuch) on the Gulf of Khambhat it lists nard, costus, bdellium, lycium and long pepper; and from the ports of the Malabar coast, besides great quantities of black pepper, it lists malabathrum, the aromatic leaves of species of Cinnamomum, and nard brought from the Ganges region. Roman authors confirm the picture. The Greek physician Dioscorides, writing in the first century CE, describes several of these drugs in his De materia medica, and Pliny the Elder, in his Natural History, records their prices and complains of the drain of Roman money to the East to pay for luxuries of this kind. Most of these goods were used in Rome as perfumes, incense and spices as much as medicines; the categories were not sharply divided.

    The trade continued into the Islamic world, which absorbed much Indian medical knowledge along with the drugs. In the late eighth century, in Abbasid Baghdad, Sanskrit medical works, among them versions of the Caraka- and Suśruta-saṃhitā, were translated into Arabic, partly by physicians brought from India (Volume 13). Arabic and Persian medical writing adopted many Indian plant names: halīlaj or ihlīlaj, the chebulic myrobalan, comes through Persian from harītakī; the Unani compound medicines called iṭrīfal take their name, by the same route, from triphalā; costus appears as qusṭ and long pepper as dār fulful. Through Arabic medicine, and later through Latin translations of Arabic works, the myrobalans entered the pharmacy of medieval Europe, which recognised “five myrobalans” — the yellow (citrine), chebulic, black (“Indian”), belleric and emblic — of which the first three are fruits of Terminalia chebula gathered at different stages, the fourth is T. bellirica and the fifth is āmalakī. The Greek name myrobalanos had originally denoted a quite different fruit; its transfer to the Indian fruits is a small example of how drug names travelled and shifted with the trade.

    12.5.2 Eastward: the Buddhist world and China¶

    Indian drugs travelled east with Buddhist monks and with the merchants of the Bay of Bengal and the South China Sea. The Pāli Vinaya, the monastic code, already lists the root medicines, fruits, resins and other substances a monk may keep, and as Buddhism spread these lists, and the drugs in them, went with it. Several Chinese names of Indian drugs are thought to be loans from Sanskrit or Prakrit: biba, long pepper, from pippalī, and helile, the chebulic myrobalan, from harītakī. Costus root, under the name muxiang, became a standard Chinese drug (Section 12.3.11). In Tibetan Buddhist art the Medicine Buddha, Bhaiṣajyaguru, is commonly shown holding a branch or fruit of the myrobalan, and the fruit remains one of the most important drugs of Sowa-Rigpa, the Tibetan medical tradition (Section 12.6). The flow was not one-way: drugs from Southeast Asia, China and Iran, such as camphor, cloves and asafoetida, were in turn imported into India and absorbed into its materia medica.

    12.5.3 Garcia da Orta and the European record¶

    The Portuguese brought the trade in Indian drugs under the scrutiny of European physicians on the spot. Garcia da Orta, a physician of New Christian (converted Jewish) family who lived in Goa from 1534, published there in 1563 his Colóquios dos simples e drogas da Índia, a series of dialogues on the drugs of India, among them costus, long pepper, the myrobalans, sandalwood and sweet flag (Orta 1913). Orta drew on conversations with Indian and other Asian physicians and traders (Chapter 6), and he corrected many errors in the European and Arabic authorities about where these drugs came from and what plants produced them. A Latin abridgement by the Flemish botanist Carolus Clusius, published at Antwerp in 1567, carried his work across Europe. Orta’s own family suffered at the hands of the Inquisition in Goa, and after his death his remains were exhumed and burnt as those of a Judaiser — a reminder of the conditions under which this early European knowledge of Indian plants was produced.

    12.5.4 Colonial transfers: cinchona in India¶

    Under British rule the trade in Indian drugs became one part of a larger imperial economy of plants. Myrobalans, for example, were exported in quantity for tanning and dyeing (Section 12.1), and in 1868 the India Office published a Pharmacopoeia of India, edited by Edward John Waring, which admitted a number of Indian drugs alongside those of British pharmacy. The most consequential colonial medicinal-plant enterprise, however, concerned a plant brought into India rather than one sent out of it.

    Quinine, the most effective remedy for malaria available in the nineteenth century, was extracted from the bark of Cinchona trees native to the eastern slopes of the Andes, and the supply of bark was controlled by the South American republics in which the trees grew. Malaria was among the chief causes of death and sickness in British India, for the army, the administration and the population at large. In 1859–60 the India Office sent an expedition led by Clements Markham to collect cinchona seeds and plants in Peru and Bolivia, and plantations were established from the early 1860s on the Nilgiri hills of the Madras Presidency and, a little later, near Darjeeling in Bengal, where a government plantation and factory at Mungpoo produced quinine and a cheaper mixture of the bark alkaloids, sold as “cinchona febrifuge”. In Bengal from the 1890s quinine was sold in small packets through post offices to make it available in the countryside. The Dutch, working from seed of a high-yielding cinchona obtained in Bolivia by the trader Charles Ledger, built a larger and more productive industry in Java, which dominated world supply by the early twentieth century, and Indian production never fully met India’s needs.

    Arnold (2000) places cinchona among the characteristic projects of colonial science: a deliberate transfer of a valuable plant across continents, organised by botanic gardens and the state and presented as a benefit to Indians, in which the needs of the army and the administration weighed heavily. The plantations around Darjeeling remain in government hands, although quinine has long been displaced as the main treatment for malaria by synthetic drugs.

    12.6 Regional and Community Pharmacopoeias¶

    The Sanskrit materia medica is only one of several. The Siddha tradition of the Tamil country, associated with the eighteen Siddhars, has its own literature in Tamil and its own plant names, and uses some plants that are more prominent in it than in Āyurveda; nilavēmpu (Andrographis paniculata), for example, the main ingredient of a decoction distributed by the Tamil Nadu government during dengue outbreaks, is a Siddha favourite. The Unani tradition, which came to India with the Sultanates, combined Graeco-Arabic doctrine and Persian and Arabic drugs with many Indian plants under Persian and Arabic names, and Sowa-Rigpa, the Tibetan medical tradition of the Himalayan regions, draws heavily on high-altitude species. These traditions are treated in Volume 13, Āyurveda.

    Beyond the written systems lies a vast body of oral knowledge held by village healers, midwives, bonesetters and Adivasi communities, recorded only partially by ethnobotanists (Jain 1991). The case of the Kani of Kerala and the plant ārōgyappacca, an early and much-discussed experiment in sharing the benefits of such knowledge, is described in Chapter 20; tribal medical knowledge is treated in Volume 23, Jana–Jāti.

    12.7 Household Knowledge of Plants¶

    Between the written medical systems and the specialist healer lies the most widespread plant knowledge of all: that held in households, and in large part by women. It is rarely written down, it is passed on by example in the kitchen, the courtyard and the field, and it is usually known by homely names — in Hindi dādī-nānī ke nuskhe, “grandmothers’ recipes”, in Tamil pāṭṭi vaittiyam, “grandmother’s medicine”. The classical texts were composed by men and addressed to male physicians, though they themselves refer to experienced women attending childbirth; the knowledge of the women who cooked, gathered, gardened and nursed has to be recovered from ethnography and from the practices themselves (compare Chapter 18).

    Much of this knowledge concerns food rather than medicine in any narrow sense. Which spices and pulses are cooked in which season, what is given to a convalescent or to a woman after childbirth, which leaves are eaten in the rains and which avoided, how pickles, papads and dried vegetables are made: these are matters of household custom, varying by region, caste and community, in which the kitchen spices — turmeric, ginger, cumin, ajwain, fenugreek, asafoetida, long pepper — and many leafy greens play the leading part. The line between a cooking practice and a remedy is not sharply drawn in such knowledge, and the present chapter, as elsewhere, records the practices without endorsing any therapeutic claim.

    The house garden is the other seat of this knowledge. Besides the larger homegardens described in Chapter 10, many Indian households, rural and urban, keep a few plants by the door or on a roof: tulasī in its courtyard planter, a curry-leaf tree (Murraya koenigii), a drumstick tree (Moringa oleifera), aloe, a betel vine, chillies, gourds climbing a fence. Who chooses, plants and tends these plants is in many households a woman’s decision. Plants also carry meanings in women’s ritual and adornment: henna (mehndi, Lawsonia inermis) applied to the hands and feet at weddings and festivals, turmeric paste in the rites before marriage (Section 12.3.3), and the worship of tulasī and of trees such as the banyan and pipal in vows (vrata) observed mainly by women (Chapter 11).

    Outside the house, women do much — in many regions most — of the gathering of wild plants: leafy greens and tubers for the household, mahua flowers, tendu leaves and other forest produce for sale, and many of the medicinal plants that enter the trade (Section 12.4). Their knowledge of where and when each plant can be found, and of how to tell it from its look-alikes, is the first link in the chain of identification on which the whole trade depends, yet they are usually the least paid in that chain. Village midwives (dāī), often women of low-ranked castes, and women healers in many Adivasi communities hold more specialised plant knowledge, whose status has been ambiguous: relied upon, but socially devalued and, in the twentieth century, increasingly displaced by institutional medicine.

    Household plant knowledge is changing quickly. Migration to towns, smaller households, packaged spice mixtures and branded herbal products all weaken its transmission from one generation to the next, while efforts to document it — in ethnobotanical surveys and in the People’s Biodiversity Registers prepared under the Biological Diversity Act of 2002 (Chapter 20) — raise the questions of ownership and benefit-sharing discussed in Chapter 20. It should not be idealised: household remedies can be mistaken or unsafe, as the safety note below emphasises, and much of what passes as “grandmother’s wisdom” in modern advertising is marketing. But it is a large and under-recorded part of India’s knowledge of plants, and much of it has been women’s knowledge.

    12.8 Conservation¶

    The pressures on India’s medicinal plants are the familiar ones of habitat loss and over-collection, intensified by the rapid growth of the domestic and international herbal market. Those most at risk combine several features: they are slow-growing, they are harvested for roots, bark or whole plants, they grow in restricted habitats (often at high altitude), and they are in high commercial demand.

    Some Indian medicinal plants are listed under the Convention on International Trade in Endangered Species (CITES). Kuṣṭha (Section 12.3.11) is listed in Appendix I, which bars international commercial trade in wild-collected specimens, while cultivation in parts of the western Himalaya is intended to supply legally traded material. Several other Himalayan root and rhizome drugs, among them jaṭāmāṃsī (Nardostachys jatamansi) and kaṭukī (Picrorhiza kurroa), are in Appendix II, which permits regulated trade, as is sarpagandhā (Rauvolfia serpentina). India also restricts the export of a number of wild-collected plants and their products. Red sanders and sandalwood are threatened above all by the smuggling of their timber.

    Within India, the National Medicinal Plants Board, set up by the Government of India in 2000, supports the cultivation of medicinal plants, nurseries, conservation areas in forests and good collection practices; several state forest departments have established in situ medicinal plant conservation areas; and botanic gardens and research institutes maintain living collections and seed banks. Cultivation can relieve pressure on wild stands, as with aśvagandhā, but it is difficult for slow-growing perennials such as guggulu and many Himalayan species, and it raises its own questions of whether cultivated material is equivalent to the wild plant in the eyes of practitioners. Sacred groves, which often shelter medicinal plants, and community forest management are discussed in Chapter 11. Threat assessments change over time; current statuses should be checked in the IUCN Red List and the CITES appendices rather than taken from secondary lists.

    Health and Safety Note

    This chapter describes plants, not treatments. Self-medication with herbal products can cause harm: plant products may be misidentified, substituted or adulterated; they may interact with prescription medicines; and some, including products containing guḍūcī and aśvagandhā, have been associated with liver injury. Some Āyurvedic products, particularly those that deliberately include metals or minerals, have been found to contain lead, mercury or arsenic at levels that can cause poisoning, including products sold over the internet (Saper et al. 2004, 2008). Anyone considering an herbal product, especially during pregnancy, in childhood, or alongside other medicines, should consult a qualified medical practitioner.

    Summary¶

    The classical Indian materia medica names several hundred plants, organised in Caraka’s fifty groups of ten, Suśruta’s gaṇas and the later nighaṇṭus, and folk and tribal traditions use thousands more. Named groups such as triphalā, daśamūla and aṣṭavarga show the range of botanical problems: well-identified fruit trees, root drugs of disputed identity that are costly to the plants supplying them, and Himalayan tubers whose identity was uncertain even in the sixteenth century and for which substitutes were already permitted. The fifteen plants surveyed range from sacred household plants and field crops to a critically endangered desert shrub and over-collected Himalayan roots, from a plant with a single identity to a name shared by two unrelated species, and from a dye plant ruined by synthetic chemistry to the source of reserpine. Costus, nard, long pepper and the myrobalans were carried to the Roman, Islamic and Chinese worlds and left their names in Greek, Arabic and Chinese; under colonial rule the flow was also reversed, as Andean cinchona was planted in the Nilgiris and around Darjeeling to supply quinine. Alongside the written systems, a large and under-recorded body of household plant knowledge, much of it held by women, belongs to the same history. Identity, substitution and adulteration are the central practical problems of the trade; over-collection of roots, bark and slow-growing Himalayan species is its central conservation problem. Therapeutic questions are left to Volume 13.
