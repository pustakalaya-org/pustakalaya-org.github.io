    Chapter 17: Birds, Insects, Reptiles and Aquatic Life¶

    17.1 Knowledge of Smaller and Wilder Creatures¶

    Beyond the cattle of the farm and the elephants and horses of the court lay a much larger world of animals that Indians observed, named, hunted, protected, used and wrote about: birds, insects, snakes and other reptiles, frogs, fishes, shellfish, turtles, crocodiles and river dolphins. Knowledge about them was rarely gathered into a single treatise. It is scattered across Vedic hymns, the Arthaśāstra and the edicts of Aśoka, medical and gemmological texts, the omen literature, court poetry and the memoirs of Mughal emperors — and, above all, it lived in the practice of fowlers, falconers, silk-rearers, lac-cultivators, honey-hunters and fisherfolk, most of whose knowledge was never written down. This chapter surveys these sources, separating, as far as possible, what was observed from what was conventional or mythical.

    17.2 Birds in Vedic and Classical Literature¶

    17.2.1 Vedic Birds¶

    Birds appear in the Ṛgveda chiefly as images and messengers. The falcon or eagle (śyena) brings the soma from heaven (Ṛgveda 4.26–27); a pigeon (kapota) that has entered the house is treated as a messenger of Nirṛti, the goddess of destruction, in a hymn to avert its evil (10.165); pairs of ruddy shelducks (cakravāka) and geese (haṃsa) appear in similes (Jamison and Brereton 2014). In a famous riddle hymn two birds sit on one tree:

    Ṛgveda 1.164.20

    dvā suparṇā sayujā sakhāyā samānaṃ vṛkṣam pari ṣasvajāte
    tayor anyaḥ pippalaṃ svādv atty anaśnann anyo abhi cākaśīti
    “Two birds, companions joined together, embrace the same tree. One of them eats the sweet fig; the other, not eating, looks on.”

    The verse, taken up again in the Muṇḍaka Upaniṣad (3.1.1), was read in later tradition as an image of the individual self and the witnessing self. Its value here is as a reminder that birds in Vedic poetry are usually symbols; the ornithological content is incidental. The Vedic haṃsa, for example, is often a solar or spiritual image rather than a particular species.

    17.2.2 Poetic Conventions and Real Birds¶

    Classical Sanskrit poetry developed a repertoire of bird conventions (kavisamaya): the haṃsa that can separate milk from water; the pair of cakravākas condemned to spend each night apart and calling to each other across the river; the cātaka that drinks only raindrops falling from the clouds; the cakora that feeds on moonlight; the peacock (mayūra) that dances at the approach of rain clouds, declared India’s national bird in 1963; the kokila (koel) whose call announces spring. Some of these conventions rest on real observation. The haṃsa of the poets is most probably the bar-headed goose, a winter visitor that leaves the plains for Central Asia in spring — the “departure of the geese for Mānasa” of the poets. The ruddy shelduck does live in pairs and calls loudly at dusk. The pied cuckoo, often identified with the cātaka, arrives in northern India with the monsoon. Others — the milk-separating swan, the moon-drinking cakora — are poetic fancies. K. N. Dave’s Birds in Sanskrit Literature is the standard attempt to identify the birds named in the texts, and shows both how rich the vocabulary is and how uncertain many identifications remain (Dave 1985).

    17.2.3 Protected Birds¶

    Lists of birds also appear in administrative and legal contexts. Aśoka’s fifth Pillar Edict (c. 242 BCE; Chapter 11) exempts from killing, among other creatures, parrots, mynas, ruddy shelducks, wild geese and pigeons (Singh 2008). The Arthaśāstra, in its chapter on the superintendent of slaughterhouses, likewise protects a list of birds and animals, including geese, cranes, ruddy shelducks, peacocks, parrots and mynas, mostly birds valued for their beauty, their song or their auspiciousness (Kangle 1965; Olivelle 2013). These are early examples of legal protection for named animals; their selection reflects royal, religious and aesthetic preferences rather than ecological criteria.

    17.3 Omens, Hunting and Court Natural History¶

    17.3.1 Bird Omens¶

    The interpretation of omens from the calls, flight and behaviour of birds and animals (śakuna) was an important branch of Indian divination. Varāhamihira’s Bṛhatsaṃhitā devotes a group of chapters to it, describing how the direction, time and quality of a bird’s cry predict success or failure on a journey or undertaking (Bhat 1981); later handbooks of śakuna elaborated the system. The omen literature preserves a great many bird names and some acute observations of behaviour, but its purpose was divination, not natural history.

    17.3.2 The Mṛgapakṣiśāstra¶

    Modern accounts often cite a Mṛgapakṣiśāstra (“Treatise on Beasts and Birds”) ascribed to a Jain author named Haṃsadeva and dated to the thirteenth century, which describes the appearance, habits and temperament of many animals and birds. The work is known only through a twentieth-century publication with an English version; its manuscript basis has not been properly described, and its date and authenticity are uncertain. Some scholars regard it as a late compilation. Until a critical study is available it should not be used as evidence for medieval Indian zoology.

    17.3.3 Falconry and Fowling¶

    Hawking was a royal sport across the Persianate and Indian worlds. A Sanskrit treatise on falconry, the Śyainikaśāstra, edited and translated by Haraprasad Shastri (Shastri 1910), is ascribed to a king Rudradeva of Kumaon; it classifies hunting birds, describes their training and care and treats hunting as a royal pleasure with its own ethics. Falconry flourished at the courts of the Delhi Sultans and the Mughals, whose falconers (mīr-shikār) formed a specialised service. Fowling with nets, snares, decoys and lime was practised by specialist communities — the Pārdhī of western and central India, the Bahelia of the north and many others — whose knowledge of bird behaviour was intimate and largely unwritten, and who were stigmatised under colonial law as “criminal tribes”.

    17.3.4 Jahāngīr and the Painters¶

    The Mughal emperor Jahāngīr (r. 1605–1627) recorded in his memoirs many careful observations of animals and birds: he noted, for example, the breeding behaviour of a pair of sarus cranes kept at court, including the sharing of incubation between male and female, and described birds and animals brought to him from distant lands (Thackston 1999). He had his painters, above all Ustād Manṣūr, record birds, animals and flowers with a precision unmatched in earlier Indian art. These paintings and notes are among the most valuable documents of pre-modern natural history in India.

    17.4 Birds in the Modern Landscape¶

    17.4.1 Modern Ornithology¶

    Scientific ornithology in India took shape in the colonial period, notably through Allan Octavian Hume, who founded the journal Stray Feathers (1872), and through the Bombay Natural History Society, founded in 1883. Sálim Ali (1896–1987) made the study of Indian birds a field of Indian science; his Handbook of the Birds of India and Pakistan, written with S. Dillon Ripley, remains a standard reference (Ali and Ripley 1968). Ali and his successors drew extensively on the knowledge of local hunters, trappers and villagers, often without acknowledgement in print (Rangarajan 2001).

    17.4.2 Birds and Agriculture¶

    Farmers have always had an ambivalent relationship with birds. Parrots (śuka) are listed among the six traditional agricultural calamities (īti) — excessive rain, drought, locusts, rats, parrots and the incursions of neighbouring kings — and bird-scaring from crop platforms was, and is, the work of children and the poor. At the same time birds such as owls, egrets, drongos and mynas eat rodents and insects in the fields. In recent decades India has witnessed one of the most rapid declines of any bird group recorded: the collapse of the populations of Gyps vultures, which once disposed of the carcasses of cattle across the subcontinent, caused by residues of the veterinary drug diclofenac in carcasses (Oaks et al. 2004). The drug was banned for veterinary use in India in 2006. The vulture collapse illustrates how closely wild birds were bound to the agrarian economy of cattle described in Chapter 13.

    17.4.3 Village Heronries and Bird Refuges¶

    Some of India’s best-known bird sites owe their survival to villagers. At Kokkare Bellur in Karnataka, painted storks and spot-billed pelicans nest every year in the trees of the village itself, and the villagers have long tolerated and protected them, valuing the droppings that accumulate under the nesting trees as manure; the village name itself refers to the storks (Kannada kokkare). At Vedanthangal in Tamil Nadu, a tank whose trees support a large colony of waterbirds, the local cultivators protected the birds by custom, and the colonial government recognised their protection in the nineteenth century; the site later became a bird sanctuary. Such places show a practical bargain between farmers and birds, in which the manure from heronries enriched the fields and tank water downstream. Elsewhere the relationship was one of royal sport: the wetlands of Keoladeo at Bharatpur in Rajasthan were developed by the rulers of Bharatpur as a duck-shooting preserve, at which enormous bags were recorded in the colonial period, before becoming a national park in 1982 and a World Heritage Site. The sarus crane, the tallest flying bird, still nests in the wet fields and village ponds of the Gangetic plain and Gujarat, where it is widely regarded as a symbol of marital fidelity and left unharmed.

    17.5 Insects: Silk, Lac and Honey¶

    17.5.1 Silk¶

    The Sanskrit word for silk, kauśeya, derives from kośa, “cocoon”. The Arthaśāstra distinguishes kauśeya from cīnapaṭṭa, “Chinese cloth”, showing that both local and imported silks were known (Kangle 1965). India is unusual in having several native silk-producing moths. Besides the mulberry silkworm (Bombyx mori), domesticated in China and introduced to India, wild or semi-domesticated species yield tasar silk (Antheraea mylitta and related species, on trees such as Terminalia), eri silk (Samia ricini, reared on castor) and the golden muga silk of Assam (Antheraea assamensis). Microscopic study of fibres from Harappa and Chanhu-daro has shown that silk from wild Antheraea moths was being used in the Indus civilisation in the third millennium BCE (Good et al. 2009). Rearing tasar and eri has long been the work of tribal and peasant communities in central and north-eastern India. Mulberry sericulture expanded in Bengal in the Sultanate and Mughal periods and was promoted in Mysore by Tipu Sultan in the late eighteenth century. The textile side of the story belongs to Volume 17.

    17.5.2 Lac¶

    Lac (lākṣā; Hindi lākh, whence English “lac” and ultimately “lacquer”) is the resinous secretion of the scale insect Kerria lacca, which encrusts the twigs of host trees such as palāśa (Butea monosperma), kusum (Schleichera oleosa) and ber (Ziziphus mauritiana). It yields both a red dye (the source of alaktaka, the red colour with which women decorated their feet) and the resin used for varnish, sealing, ornaments, bangles and, in modern times, shellac. A hymn of the Atharvaveda (5.5) is addressed to lākṣā as a healing substance for wounds (Whitney 1905), and the Mahābhārata’s “house of lac” (lākṣāgṛha), built to burn the Pāṇḍavas, shows that its flammability was well understood. Lac cultivation — inoculating host trees with brood lac and harvesting the encrusted twigs — is an old practice of the forest communities of present-day Jharkhand, Chhattisgarh, Odisha and West Bengal, and India remains the world’s leading producer.

    17.5.3 Bees and Honey¶

    Honey (madhu) is prominent in Vedic ritual and imagery, and the medical texts distinguish several kinds of honey according to the insects that produce them: Suśruta lists eight (Bhishagratna 1907). Three species of honeybee are native to the subcontinent: the giant rock bee (Apis dorsata), which builds huge open combs on cliffs and tall trees; the Asian hive bee (Apis cerana), which nests in cavities and has long been kept in log, pot and wall hives in Kerala, the Himalaya and the North-East; and the dwarf bee (Apis florea); there are also stingless bees. Honey-hunting from rock-bee colonies is a specialised and dangerous skill of forest communities such as the Jēnu Kuruba (“honey Kuruba”) of Karnataka and the Kurumba of the Nilgiris, who combine climbing technique, smoke and ritual with detailed knowledge of the bees’ seasonal movements. Modern apiculture with movable-frame hives was introduced in the twentieth century, and the European honeybee (Apis mellifera) later still.

    17.5.4 Pollinators¶

    The greatest service insects render to agriculture is pollination. Many Indian crops — mustard and other oilseeds, pulses such as pigeon pea, cucurbits, many fruit trees, and cardamom and coffee in the hills — depend wholly or partly on bees and other insects to set seed and fruit. Wild bees, flies, wasps, beetles, butterflies and moths, as well as the honeybees, do this work, and the hedgerows, field margins, trees and fallows of mixed farming landscapes sustain them. Farmers and beekeepers have long observed the connection between flowering crops and honey; the rock-bee colonies of the Western Ghats and the cardamom and coffee plantations below them are linked in this way. The texts of the vṛkṣāyurveda tradition, concerned with making trees flower and fruit (Chapter 4), do not describe pollination by insects, which was first understood in Europe in the eighteenth century. In modern India, as elsewhere, the intensive use of insecticides, the loss of uncultivated land and the simplification of cropping systems are causes of concern for pollinator populations, and the deliberate placing of hives in orchards and oilseed fields to improve pollination has become part of agricultural extension.

    17.6 Insects in Medicine, Lore and the Fields¶

    The anthill (valmīka) has a special place in Indian lore. The poet Vālmīki is said to have been so absorbed in austerity that an anthill grew over him; in south India anthills (termite mounds) are worshipped as the dwelling of snakes; and Varāhamihira’s chapter on finding underground water (dakārgala) uses anthills near particular trees as signs of water below (Bhat 1981) (see Chapter 8). Termite-mound earth is used in some regions as a soil amendment and in building.

    Locusts (śalabha) appear among the agricultural calamities mentioned above, and swarms periodically devastated crops in north-western India; the colonial and modern states organised locust warning services. The medical texts classify stinging and biting insects (kīṭa) among venomous creatures and describe the effects of their bites, including those of mosquitoes (maśaka) (Bhishagratna 1907). These classifications are of real historical interest, but they do not amount to a theory of insect-borne disease; that mosquitoes transmit malaria was established by Ronald Ross, working in India, in 1897–1898.

    17.7 Snakes and Other Reptiles¶

    17.7.1 Serpents in Religion¶

    No group of animals has a larger place in Indian religion than the snakes. Serpent beings (nāga) appear in the Vedic texts, in the epics — the Mahābhārata opens its narrative frame with Janamejaya’s great snake sacrifice — and in Buddhist and Jain art and story: the serpent king Mucalinda shelters the meditating Buddha with his hoods, and the twenty-third Jain tīrthaṅkara, Pārśvanātha, is shown beneath a canopy of snake hoods. Viṣṇu reclines on the cosmic serpent Śeṣa, and Śiva wears snakes as ornaments. In village religion snakes are worshipped above all as guardians of fertility, of water and of the household: in the month of Śrāvaṇa, at the height of the monsoon when snakes are driven from their holes, women offer milk and flowers at anthills and serpent images on Nāgapañcamī; in south India stones carved with entwined serpents (nāgakal) are set up beneath sacred trees, often by women seeking children; and in Kerala the serpent groves (sarpakkāvu) attached to households and temples are among the best-known sacred groves (Chapter 11). The association of snakes with anthills and termite mounds, noted above, is part of the same complex of belief.

    17.7.2 Snake Charmers and the Law¶

    Snake charming was the hereditary occupation of several itinerant communities, among them the Kalbeliyā (Sapera) of Rajasthan, who caught snakes, displayed them to the music of the gourd pipe (pūṅgī or bīn), and sold amulets and remedies against snakebite. The Wildlife (Protection) Act of 1972, which protected Indian snakes and forbade keeping them, made the occupation illegal, and many such families lost their livelihood without any alternative being provided. The Kalbeliyā songs and dances, which developed from their performances, were inscribed on UNESCO’s list of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity in 2010. Their case illustrates a recurring tension in Indian conservation between protecting animals and protecting the communities whose way of life depended on them (Chapter 16).

    17.7.3 Snakes in the Medical Texts¶

    The medical texts give snakes careful attention because of the danger they pose. The Kalpasthāna of the Suśruta-saṃhitā, devoted to poisons, classifies venomous snakes into groups that are recognisable to a modern reader: the hooded snakes (darvīkara), corresponding broadly to the cobras; the snakes marked with circular patches (maṇḍalin), broadly the vipers; and the striped or banded snakes (rājimant), among which the kraits are usually placed; together with non-venomous snakes and hybrids (Bhishagratna 1907). It describes the signs of bites by each group, distinguishes the effects of their venoms by the language of the doṣas, and lays down first aid and treatment: the binding of the limb above the bite, incision and suction, cautery, and a great range of antidotes and incantations. Caraka’s chapter on poisons covers similar ground (Sharma 1981). The classification by external appearance reflects long observation, and the recognition that the bites of hooded snakes and of vipers produce different effects is accurate; the treatments, as far as they have been examined, are not effective, and some, such as incision and tight tourniquets, are now known to do harm.

    Snakebite

    Snakebite is a medical emergency. The only effective treatment for envenoming is antivenom, given in a hospital by trained staff, together with supportive care. The victim should be kept still and taken to hospital at once. Cutting, sucking or burning the wound, tight tourniquets, herbal applications, incantations and visits to traditional healers are ineffective and can cause harm or fatal delay. Nothing in this chapter describes a usable remedy.

    17.7.4 Snakebite and Agriculture¶

    Snakebite is, above all, an occupational hazard of farming. Four species — the spectacled cobra (Naja naja), the common krait (Bungarus caeruleus), Russell’s viper (Daboia russelii) and the saw-scaled viper (Echis carinatus), often called the “big four” — cause most serious bites in India. Most victims are farmers and agricultural labourers bitten in the fields during the monsoon and harvest seasons, or people bitten at night while sleeping on the floor of their homes, where kraits come in search of rodents. A nationally representative mortality study estimated that snakebite killed about 58,000 people a year in India on average between 2000 and 2019, mostly in rural areas and among the poor (Suraweera et al. 2020), which makes it one of the gravest health burdens of agricultural work in the country. Delay in reaching hospital, often caused by first resorting to traditional healers, is a major factor in these deaths. Polyvalent antivenom against the big four is produced in India from venom collected in large part by the Irula snake-catchers’ cooperative of Tamil Nadu (Chapter 16).

    Snakes are also farmers’ allies. The Indian rat snake (Ptyas mucosa), which is not venomous, and many other snakes feed largely on rats and mice in fields and granaries, and their killing on sight removes one of the main natural checks on rodents — themselves one of the traditional calamities of agriculture.

    17.7.5 Monitor Lizards, Turtles and Tortoises¶

    The Manusmṛti (5.18) lists the five-nailed animals that may be eaten: the porcupine, the hedgehog, the monitor lizard (godhā), the rhinoceros, the tortoise and the hare (Olivelle 2005). The monitor lizard was hunted for meat and for its skin, which was used for drum-heads and leather, and is still hunted illegally; the Bengal monitor and other Indian monitors are now protected. Tortoises and freshwater turtles were eaten in many regions and protected in others: several temple tanks keep sacred turtles, and the tortoise appears in myth as the kūrma incarnation of Viṣṇu. On the coast of Odisha, at Gahirmatha and the mouth of the Rushikulya river, olive ridley sea turtles come ashore in their thousands to nest together in mass arrivals (arribadas) that are among the largest in the world; their protection from fishing trawlers, in whose nets many drown, has been a long-standing concern of conservationists and a point of conflict with fishing communities.

    17.7.6 Frogs¶

    The Ṛgveda contains a famous hymn to the frogs (7.103), in which the croaking of the frogs at the coming of the rains, after lying silent through the dry season, is compared to the chanting of brahmins at a year-long rite (Jamison and Brereton 2014); whether its tone is reverent, humorous or both has been debated. In the twentieth century Indian frogs became an export commodity: large bullfrogs were caught in paddy fields for the trade in frogs’ legs to Europe and the United States, until India banned the export in 1987, in part because the depletion of frogs was thought to increase insect pests in the rice fields. The episode is a small but clear example of the connection between the wildlife of the fields and the health of the crops.

    17.8 Fishes and Aquatic Animals¶

    17.8.1 Fish in Texts and Edicts¶

    Fish were a major source of food in much of India, particularly in Bengal, Odisha, Assam, the coasts and the river valleys, as well as being the subject of religious restrictions in others (Achaya 1994). The Arthaśāstra expects fishermen to pay dues to the state for the use of boats and waters, under the superintendent of shipping (Kangle 1965). Aśoka’s fifth pillar edict forbids the catching and sale of fish on certain days of the month and protects some kinds of fish altogether (Singh 2008). The Mānasollāsa of Someśvara III (1131) includes fishing among the pleasures of a king and names many kinds of fish, with the baits and methods appropriate to each (Shrigondekar 1925). Among the freshwater fishes most valued in the east were the carps — rohita (rohu, Labeo rohita) above all — and the hilsa (ilīśa); the rearing of carp in village ponds, stocked with fry collected from rivers in the monsoon, is an old practice of Bengal and neighbouring regions.

    17.8.2 Inland Fisheries and Fish Culture¶

    Inland fisheries — in rivers, floodplain lakes, tanks, ponds and estuaries — were as important as sea fishing for most Indians who ate fish. Village tanks were commonly leased for fishing, and the right to fish them was often a source of income for temples and village bodies as well as a matter of dispute between communities. In Bengal and Assam, floodplain wetlands (bil, bhēṛī) were managed for fish, and in the twentieth century the wetlands east of Calcutta developed a remarkable system in which the city’s sewage is led into shallow ponds where it is purified by sunlight and algae while supporting large fish harvests, with vegetables grown on the embankments and rice downstream; the East Kolkata Wetlands were designated a wetland of international importance under the Ramsar Convention in 2002. In the coastal backwaters of Kerala and the khazan lands of Goa, fish and prawns are trapped in tidal fields between rice crops (Chapter 10). Since the 1970s, scientific carp culture, using induced breeding of the major Indian carps (rohu, catla and mrigal) developed by Indian fisheries scientists, has transformed inland aquaculture.

    17.8.3 Lagoons, Migratory Fish and Temple Fish¶

    Some of India’s most productive fisheries lie in coastal lagoons. The largest, Chilika in Odisha, is a brackish lagoon whose fish, prawns and crabs have long supported the fishing villages around its shores, which held customary rights to particular fishing grounds. From the 1980s and 1990s the spread of commercial prawn culture in the lagoon, backed by outside investors and landholders, brought bitter conflict with the traditional fishing communities, while the silting of the lagoon’s mouth threatened its ecology until a new channel to the sea was cut in 2000. The lagoon is also home to a small population of Irrawaddy dolphins.

    The hilsa (Tenualosa ilisha), the most prized fish of Bengal, lives in the sea and the estuaries and migrates far up the rivers to spawn during the monsoon. Its runs once reached well up the Ganga and its tributaries; barrages and dams, among them the Farakka barrage on the Ganga, have blocked or reduced these migrations, and river catches upstream have declined. The mahseer (species of Tor), the great game fish of Himalayan and peninsular rivers, has suffered from dams, dynamite fishing and pollution. In several places, however, fish have long been protected by religious custom: at riverside temples in the Western Ghats — the stretch of the Tuṅgā at Śṛṅgeri is a well-known example — and at shrines in the Himalayan foothills, the fish in the temple waters are fed by pilgrims and may not be caught. Such temple pools have served, in effect, as refuges for mahseer and other fish whose populations have collapsed elsewhere, and conservationists have drawn attention to them as examples of the protective effect of sacred sites, comparable to the sacred groves of Chapter 11.

    17.8.4 The Fish in Myth¶

    The oldest version of the Indian flood story, in the Śatapatha Brāhmaṇa (1.8.1), tells how Manu rescued a small fish that grew and in return warned him of the coming flood and towed his boat to safety; in the Purāṇas the fish becomes the first incarnation (avatāra) of Viṣṇu. Modern suggestions that the sequence of avatāras encodes biological evolution are a reading imposed on the myth, not a meaning found in the texts.

    17.8.5 Pearls, Conch and Coral¶

    Pearls (muktā) were among India’s most famous products. The pearl banks of the Gulf of Mannar, between south India and Sri Lanka, were fished from antiquity: the Periplus of the Erythraean Sea (1st century CE) mentions the pearl fishery near Korkai in the Pāṇḍya kingdom, and the Arthaśāstra lists the sources of pearls, beginning with the Tāmraparṇī river (Kangle 1965). The pearl-divers of this coast were mainly Paravar fishermen, most of whom became Catholic Christians in the 1530s under Portuguese influence. Gemmological texts — the Bṛhatsaṃhitā’s chapter on pearls, the Ratnaparīkṣā ascribed to Buddhabhaṭṭa, edited with other Sanskrit lapidaries by Finot (1896) — grade pearls by size, shape, lustre and colour with much practical knowledge, but also list mythical “sources” of pearls such as elephants’ heads, serpents, clouds and bamboo, alongside the oyster.

    The sacred conch (śaṅkha, the shell of Turbinella pyrum) has been fished in the Gulf of Mannar and worked into bangles, ladles and trumpets since Harappan times (Kenoyer 1998); the conch-cutters of Bengal (śāṅkhārī) were a well-known craft caste. Coral (pravāla, vidruma), by contrast, was largely red Mediterranean coral imported through the Red Sea trade, though the reefs of the Gulf of Mannar, Lakshadweep and the Andaman Islands were well known to local fishermen.

    17.8.6 River Dolphins, Crocodiles and Turtles¶

    The Ganges river dolphin (Platanista gangetica), an almost blind dolphin of the Ganga–Brahmaputra system, is probably the creature called śiśumāra or śuśuka in some Sanskrit texts, though the identification is not certain (śiśumāra also names a crocodile-like constellation). It was declared India’s national aquatic animal in 2009 and is endangered by dams, pollution and fishing nets. The gharial and the mugger crocodile were common in the rivers; the makara, the composite water-monster that serves as the mount of the river goddess Gaṅgā and decorates temple doorways, draws on them and on fishes, though it is a creature of art rather than natural history. Freshwater turtles and tortoises were eaten, protected in some temple tanks, and given cosmic meaning in the tortoise (kūrma) incarnation of Viṣṇu.

    17.9 Fishing Communities and Their Knowledge¶

    The real repository of Indian knowledge about the sea and rivers is the practice of the fishing communities: the Koḷī of the Konkan and Gujarat coasts, the Mukkuvar of Kerala and Kanyakumari, the Paravar and Paṭṭiṉavar of the Tamil coast, the Kaivarta and Jalia castes of Bengal and Odisha, the Mallāh and Nishād of the Ganga, and many others. Their knowledge covers the seasons and movements of fish shoals, the relation of catches to winds, currents, tides and the moon, the location of fishing grounds and submerged rocks, the construction of boats, nets and traps, and the reading of the sea’s colour and of birds working over shoals. On the Kerala coast fishermen exploited the chākara, the calm, fish-rich mud banks that form near the shore during the monsoon. Customary institutions regulated access to fishing grounds and settled disputes in many coastal villages (Agrawal 2001).

    Most of these communities occupied a low place in caste hierarchies, and their knowledge was seldom recorded by the literate. Since the mid-twentieth century mechanised trawling, the motorisation of boats, coastal pollution and the decline of river fisheries have transformed their livelihoods; seasonal bans on trawling during the monsoon breeding season, adopted by coastal states from the late 1980s, owed much to the campaigns of traditional fishworkers. Fisheries and the maritime world are treated more fully in Volume 19.

    Summary¶

    Indian knowledge of birds, insects and aquatic animals is spread across poetry, law, medicine, divination and court memoirs, and lived above all in the practice of specialist communities. Snakes, central to Indian religion and classified with real care in the medical texts’ chapters on poison, remain a grave hazard of agricultural work, for which antivenom, not traditional remedy, is the only effective treatment; frogs, monitor lizards and turtles have their own histories of use and protection. Vedic and classical poetry used birds mainly as symbols and developed conventions, some grounded in observation and some fanciful; Aśoka and the Arthaśāstra protected lists of birds and animals; the omen literature, falconry treatises and Jahāngīr’s memoirs and paintings record real observation; the Mṛgapakṣiśāstra is too uncertain to be relied upon. India’s native silk moths, its lac insect and its honeybees supported old industries in which tribal and forest communities were the experts. Fishes, pearls, conch and coral were important in diet, trade and ritual, and the knowledge of fisherfolk, though rarely written, was the most detailed of all.
