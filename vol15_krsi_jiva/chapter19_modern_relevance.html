    Chapter 20: Traditional Knowledge in Modern India: Agriculture, Biodiversity and Rights¶

    20.1 From History to the Present¶

    The earlier chapters of this volume have traced Indian knowledge of fields, plants and animals from the Neolithic villages of Mehrgarh to the eve of independence. This closing chapter asks what has become of that knowledge in modern India, and how it is used, contested and protected today. It covers the Green Revolution and its aftermath; the organic and natural-farming movements, which often appeal to tradition; the documentation of traditional knowledge; the international disputes over patents on Indian plants and their uses; the laws on biodiversity and farmers’ rights that followed; genetically modified crops and the politics of seed; the discipline of ethnobotany; and India’s biodiversity hotspots. It ends with some principles for evaluating traditional practices and with a conclusion for the volume as a whole.

    Traditional knowledge is often presented as a ready-made solution to the crises of modern agriculture (Section 1.5). The relationship between traditional knowledge and modern science is more interesting than a story of vindication: some traditional practices have proved valuable, some are neutral, some are harmful, and many have never been properly tested.

    20.2 The Green Revolution¶

    20.2.1 Origins¶

    The scientific and institutional origins of the Green Revolution, from the import of Mexican semi-dwarf wheats after the droughts of 1965 and 1966 to the release of Indian varieties in 1967–1968, and the debates among its historians, are set out in Sections 19.13 and 19.14. This section is concerned with its consequences. The new varieties were promoted as a package, with chemical fertilisers, pesticides, irrigation (above all private tubewells), institutional credit, guaranteed minimum support prices and state procurement through the Food Corporation of India.

    20.2.2 Gains and Costs¶

    The mainstream assessment is that the Green Revolution achieved its central purpose. Wheat production rose very rapidly in Punjab, Haryana and western Uttar Pradesh, rice production later rose in irrigated regions, and by the 1970s India no longer depended on food aid and had begun to build public grain stocks. The fear of mass famine that had been widespread in the mid-1960s receded, and the increase in output was achieved largely by raising yields on existing land rather than by clearing new land (Kumar 1983; Ludden 1999).

    The costs became clearer over the following decades. The gains were concentrated in irrigated regions and, at least at first, among larger farmers who could afford the inputs; rain-fed regions and the crops grown in them — millets and pulses above all — received far less attention. Intensive irrigated rice–wheat rotations drew down groundwater in north-western India; soils developed deficiencies of micronutrients; heavy and sometimes indiscriminate use of pesticides raised problems for health and for the environment; and the replacement of local landraces by a small number of modern varieties narrowed the genetic base of the major crops. The most sweeping critique came from Shiva (1991), who argued that the Green Revolution was ecologically destructive and socially disruptive, and linked it to the political crisis in Punjab in the 1980s.

    Debate: Assessing the Green Revolution

    Defenders of the Green Revolution point to the famines it is credited with preventing, to the land it spared from clearance and to the food security it gave a rapidly growing population. Critics such as Shiva (1991) point to its ecological costs, its dependence on purchased inputs and the inequalities it deepened. Most agricultural economists and historians now hold a position between these poles: the high-yielding package was a necessary response to a real crisis, and its gains were large, but its costs were also real, and the policy framework built around it — subsidised fertiliser, electricity and water, and procurement concentrated on rice and wheat — has made those costs harder to correct (Ludden 1999). Slogans that the Green Revolution saved India, or that it destroyed Indian agriculture, are both inadequate summaries.

    20.2.3 Agrarian Distress¶

    Since the 1990s Indian agriculture has been marked by what is often called agrarian distress. Most holdings are small and have been subdivided further with each generation; farm incomes are low and vulnerable to drought, pests and price falls; input costs have risen; and many farmers are indebted, often to informal lenders. Suicides among farmers and agricultural labourers, recorded in official statistics since the 1990s, have become the most painful sign of this distress; their causes are complex and debated, and include indebtedness, crop failure, family circumstances and the absence of social support. The National Commission on Farmers (2004–2006), chaired by M. S. Swaminathan, recommended measures on prices, credit, insurance, land and water. Disputes over agricultural marketing came to a head in the farmers’ protests of 2020–2021 against three central farm laws, which were repealed in 2021. These are questions of economics and politics rather than of traditional knowledge, and no appeal to tradition can resolve them; but they form the setting in which movements for “natural” and “traditional” farming have gained support.

    20.3 Groundwater and the Limits of Irrigation¶

    The most important change in Indian irrigation since independence has been the spread of private wells and tubewells pumping groundwater (Chapter 8). From the 1960s onwards, cheap electric and diesel pumps, bank credit and, in many states, free or heavily subsidised electricity for agriculture allowed millions of farmers to drill their own wells. Groundwater became the largest single source of irrigation in the country. It gave individual farmers a reliable, controllable supply that canals and tanks had never offered, and it underpinned the expansion of the Green Revolution beyond the canal-irrigated tracts.

    The costs have been severe in many regions. Where pumping exceeds recharge, water tables fall, shallow wells dry up, and farmers must drill deeper at greater expense; the poorest, who cannot follow the falling water table, lose access first. Analyses of satellite gravity measurements by Rodell et al. (2009) showed large losses of groundwater storage beneath north-western India in the early 2000s, consistent with the official well records. In parts of the hard-rock peninsula, failed borewells have been a significant source of farmers’ debt. In some hard-rock areas, water drawn from deeper levels carries harmful concentrations of fluoride, and in parts of the Ganga–Brahmaputra plains shallow groundwater used for irrigation and drinking is contaminated with naturally occurring arsenic.

    Responses have been of several kinds. Punjab and Haryana passed laws in 2009 forbidding the transplanting of paddy before mid-June, so that the thirstiest phase of the rice crop would coincide with the monsoon rather than with the hottest weeks of summer. Programmes of watershed development since the 1980s, often drawing on the older water-harvesting techniques described in Chapter 8, have sought to increase recharge by checking runoff with bunds, trenches and small dams. Experiments in participatory groundwater management have tried to give village communities the information and the institutions to regulate their own pumping, and the national Atal Bhujal Yojana (2019) supports such community-based management in water-stressed districts. The revival of tanks and johads has a place in this effort, but no return to older systems can by itself sustain the present scale of irrigated agriculture; the central problems are the incentives created by free electricity and by procurement policies that favour water-intensive rice and sugarcane, and the absence of effective rules for a resource that every landholder can tap.

    20.4 Climate Change¶

    Indian agriculture is especially exposed to climate change. Most cultivated land is still rain-fed, the monsoon is the decisive factor in most harvests, and the wheat of the north-west is already grown close to the upper limit of the temperatures it tolerates during grain filling. Rising temperatures, more frequent heat waves, changes in the timing and intensity of monsoon rainfall, and rising sea levels in the coastal deltas all threaten yields and livelihoods. These are subjects for agricultural and climate science rather than for the history of knowledge, but they bear on this volume in two ways. First, the diversity of crops, landraces and farming systems described in Chapters 9 and 10 — drought-tolerant millets, flood-tolerant and salt-tolerant rices, mixed and relay cropping, trees on farms — is a store of options for adaptation, which plant breeders and farmers are already drawing on; the flood-tolerant rice varieties bred in recent decades, for example, derived their tolerance from a traditional landrace from Odisha. Second, the experience of the past shows that farming systems adapted to one climate can fail badly when conditions change, and that the poorest farmers and labourers bear the greatest share of the losses. Adaptation requires both the knowledge preserved in farming communities and the resources of modern science and public policy.

    20.5 Organic and Natural Farming¶

    20.5.1 The Organic Movement¶

    The international organic farming movement has an important Indian strand in Albert Howard’s Indore method and his An Agricultural Testament, one of its founding texts (Section 10.7). In independent India, organic farming has been promoted by non-governmental organisations, by seed-saving networks such as Navdanya, founded by Vandana Shiva, and increasingly by governments; in 2016 the government of Sikkim declared the state’s agriculture fully organic. Organic agriculture is a certified, standards-based system practised worldwide; it overlaps with traditional practice (manuring, composting, rotations, mixed cropping) but is not the same thing as it.

    20.5.2 Zero Budget Natural Farming¶

    The most influential of the Indian “natural farming” systems is Zero Budget Natural Farming (ZBNF), developed in Maharashtra from the 1990s by the agriculturist Subhash Palekar. Palekar rejects both chemical fertilisers and the large inputs of compost required by conventional organic farming. His system rests on four components:

    • jīvāmṛta, a fermented preparation of the dung and urine of indigenous cattle with jaggery, pulse flour and soil, applied to the soil as a supposed stimulant of microbial and earthworm activity;

    • bījāmṛta, a seed treatment made from similar materials;

    • ācchādana, mulching with crop residues or living cover;

    • vāpasā, the maintenance of a soil condition in which air and moisture are both present, with reduced irrigation.

    Palekar associates the system with the indigenous cow and with intercropping and trees. The names of its components are Sanskritic, but ZBNF is a modern system; despite claims sometimes made, jīvāmṛta is not the kuṇapajala of the Vṛkṣāyurveda texts, which is a fermented liquid manure made from animal remains (Chapter 4).

    In 2016 Andhra Pradesh began a large state programme, later renamed Andhra Pradesh Community-managed Natural Farming, implemented through a state not-for-profit agency (Rythu Sadhikara Samstha) and working through women’s self-help groups and farmer-to-farmer extension, with the stated aim of eventually extending natural farming to all the state’s farmers. The Union Budget of 2019 referred to zero budget farming, and the central government has since supported natural farming through national schemes. Supporters report lower input costs and less debt, improved soil cover and health, and greater farmer autonomy.

    20.5.3 The Scientific Critique¶

    These claims have been strongly questioned. In 2019 the National Academy of Agricultural Sciences, after a brainstorming session of agricultural scientists, publicly questioned the evidence base for ZBNF, describing its claims as unproven and urging that it not be promoted at scale without rigorous, multi-location testing (National Academy of Agricultural Sciences 2019). The main scientific objections are these:

    • Nutrient balance. Crops remove nitrogen, phosphorus, potassium and other nutrients from the soil with every harvest. Small applications of fermented dung and urine cannot replace these in the quantities needed for sustained yields; microbial activity can make existing nutrients more available, but cannot create phosphorus or potassium. Without adequate external inputs, natural farming risks mining soil nutrients over time.

    • Evidence. Published, peer-reviewed comparisons over several seasons and locations are few, and many evaluations have been carried out or commissioned by the programmes themselves. Some of Palekar’s claims about how plants obtain nutrients are not compatible with plant physiology.

    • Cost. “Zero budget” is a slogan. The system requires labour, mulching material, cattle (or access to their dung and urine) and time during the transition, all of which have costs.

    • Scale. Even if natural farming suits some farmers and crops, a rapid national shift could endanger food production. Sri Lanka’s abrupt ban on imported chemical fertilisers in 2021, reversed within months after harvest losses, has been widely cited as a warning of what happens when such transitions are imposed without evidence and preparation.

    The fair conclusion is that natural farming makes testable claims, that some of its elements (mulching, intercropping, reduced use of pesticides, attention to soil cover) are consistent with established agronomy, and that its overall claims about yields and soil fertility have not been demonstrated. The proper response is long-term comparative research, not either official promotion or dismissal.

    Scholarly Note: Fire Rituals and Cow Products

    Some writers on “Vedic agriculture” claim that fire rituals (agnihotra or “homa farming”), mantras, music or the application of pañcagavya (a preparation of five cow products) have special effects on crop yields, soil or pests. No such effects have been demonstrated in properly controlled trials, and the same is true of the claim that pañcagavya contains growth hormones. The religious meaning of these practices for those who perform them is a separate matter and is not in question here.

    20.6 Documenting Traditional Knowledge¶

    20.6.1 Texts and Their Editors¶

    Much of what this volume knows about the Sanskrit agricultural literature comes from a small number of twentieth-century scholars and institutions — G. P. Majumdar and S. C. Banerji, M. S. Randhawa, and the Asian Agri-History Foundation of Y. L. Nene with its translations by Nalini Sadhale and others — whose work is described in Section 1.7. These editions are the basis for much of Chapters 3 and 4. Their translations and commentaries sometimes go further than the texts in finding modern meanings, and they need to be read critically; but without them the texts would be accessible only to Sanskritists.

    Unwritten knowledge has been documented in other ways: by ethnographers and ethnobotanists (Section 20.10), by farmers’ seed-saving networks and community seed banks that maintain landraces, by the national gene bank of the National Bureau of Plant Genetic Resources in New Delhi, and by the People’s Biodiversity Registers that local Biodiversity Management Committees are required to prepare under the Biological Diversity Act of 2002 (Section 20.8).

    20.6.2 The Traditional Knowledge Digital Library¶

    The Traditional Knowledge Digital Library (TKDL), begun in 2001 by the Council of Scientific and Industrial Research together with the government department responsible for the Indian systems of medicine (now the Ministry of AYUSH), is a different kind of documentation. It is a defensive database. Formulations and uses described in classical texts of Āyurveda, Unani, Siddha and Yoga have been transcribed, classified according to a scheme compatible with the International Patent Classification, and translated into several languages, so that patent examiners can find evidence that a claimed invention was already known. Access is restricted to patent offices that have signed agreements with India, beginning with the European Patent Office. TKDL has been credited with the rejection or withdrawal of many patent applications. Its limits are equally important: it covers codified textual knowledge, not the oral knowledge of farmers and forest communities; it prevents patents rather than securing benefits for anyone; and inclusion in TKDL says nothing about whether a remedy works or is safe.

    20.7 Intellectual Property and Biopiracy¶

    In the 1990s three patent disputes made the appropriation of Indian biological knowledge a matter of national debate. They are described in detail, from a strongly critical standpoint, by Shiva (2001).

    Neem.
    The European Patent Office granted a patent in 1994 to the United States Department of Agriculture and the chemical company W. R. Grace for a method of controlling fungi on plants with a neem-oil extract. An opposition was filed in 1995 by Vandana Shiva’s Research Foundation for Science, Technology and Ecology, the International Federation of Organic Agriculture Movements and the Belgian politician Magda Aelvoet, who produced evidence that neem preparations had long been used in India against fungal diseases of crops. The patent was revoked in 2000, and the revocation was upheld on appeal in 2005. The insecticidal and antifeedant properties of neem, and the chemistry of its main active compound azadirachtin, are reviewed in Schmutterer (1995).
    Turmeric.
    In 1995 the United States Patent and Trademark Office granted a patent to the University of Mississippi Medical Center for the use of turmeric in healing wounds. The Council of Scientific and Industrial Research requested re-examination, citing published Indian sources documenting this use, and the patent was revoked in 1997 for lack of novelty. The case is often cited as the first successful challenge to a US patent on the basis of traditional knowledge.
    Basmati.
    In 1997 the US company RiceTec was granted a patent on “basmati rice lines and grains”. India challenged it in 2000, and most of its claims were withdrawn or rejected, leaving a much narrower patent on particular lines bred by the company. The related question of who may use the name “basmati” is a matter of geographical indications rather than patents.

    These cases established that documented traditional knowledge can count as prior art, and they spurred the creation of TKDL. They also exposed a deeper asymmetry. Under the World Trade Organization’s agreement on Trade-Related Aspects of Intellectual Property Rights (TRIPS, 1994), countries must protect plant varieties by patents or by an effective sui generis system, and companies can protect their improvements to plants; but the farmers and communities who selected, maintained and understood those plants over centuries had no comparable rights. The laws described in the next section were attempts to address this.

    20.8 Biodiversity Law and Benefit-Sharing¶

    The Convention on Biological Diversity (1992) affirmed the sovereign rights of states over their biological resources and called for the fair and equitable sharing of benefits arising from their use, including the use of traditional knowledge. India responded with two laws:

    • The Protection of Plant Varieties and Farmers’ Rights Act, 2001, India’s sui generis system under TRIPS, protects new plant varieties but also recognises farmers as cultivators, conservers and breeders. Farmers may save, use, sow, re-sow, exchange, share and sell their farm produce, including seed (though not branded seed of a protected variety), and farmers’ varieties can themselves be registered.

    • The Biological Diversity Act, 2002 created a three-tier structure: a National Biodiversity Authority, State Biodiversity Boards and local Biodiversity Management Committees. Access to Indian biological resources and associated knowledge for research or commercial use, particularly by foreign entities, requires approval and may be made conditional on benefit-sharing; the Act also provides for People’s Biodiversity Registers and biodiversity heritage sites. It was amended in 2023 in ways that its critics argued weakened benefit-sharing requirements for parts of the domestic herbal industry.

    Internationally, the Nagoya Protocol on Access and Benefit-Sharing, adopted under the Convention in 2010 and in force from 2014, set out more detailed obligations; India ratified it in 2012. Implementation of benefit-sharing has proved difficult everywhere: identifying the holders of widely shared knowledge, valuing it, and ensuring that payments reach communities rather than intermediaries are all unresolved problems.

    Historical Note: The Kani and Arogyapacha

    The best-known Indian example of benefit-sharing involves the Kani, a forest community of the Agasthyamalai hills in southern Kerala. During a botanical expedition in the late 1980s, scientists of the Tropical Botanic Garden and Research Institute (TBGRI), Thiruvananthapuram, observed their Kani guides eating the fruits of a small forest plant, ārōgyapacca (Trichopus zeylanicus subsp. travancoricus), which the Kani said kept them free of fatigue. TBGRI investigated the plant and developed a herbal preparation, marketed as “Jeevani”, and in the mid-1990s licensed it to an Ayurvedic manufacturer in return for a licence fee and royalties, half of which it agreed to share with the Kani through a trust established for the purpose. The arrangement was widely praised as a model. It also ran into difficulties: disputes within the community over who should represent it, conflicts with the forest department over the collection and cultivation of the plant, and limited commercial success. The case shows both that benefit-sharing is possible and how hard it is to make it work. Kani knowledge and Adivasi rights more broadly are discussed in Volume 23 (Jana–Jāti).

    Health and Safety Note

    Patent disputes and benefit-sharing agreements concern who owns knowledge, not whether a remedy is effective or safe. The revocation of the turmeric patent, the inclusion of a formulation in TKDL or the commercial licensing of a herbal product does not demonstrate therapeutic value. Herbal and Ayurvedic products can interact with medicines and can be contaminated; analyses of products sold in India and the United States have found lead, mercury and arsenic in a substantial proportion of samples (Saper et al. 2004, 2008). Medicinal claims are discussed critically in Volume 13 (Āyurveda); readers should not use any plant or preparation mentioned in this volume as a treatment without qualified medical advice.

    20.9 Genetically Modified Crops and the Seed Debate¶

    The most contested change in Indian seeds since the Green Revolution has been the arrival of genetically modified (GM) crops, plants into which genes from other organisms have been inserted in the laboratory. In India the decisive case has been cotton. In March 2002 the Genetic Engineering Approval Committee of the Ministry of Environment and Forests approved the commercial cultivation of the first cotton hybrids carrying a gene from the soil bacterium Bacillus thuringiensis (Bt), which makes the plant produce a protein toxic to bollworms, the caterpillars that were the most destructive pests of the crop. The hybrids were developed by the Maharashtra Hybrid Seeds Company (Mahyco) with the gene licensed from Monsanto. The formal approval had in fact been overtaken by events: in 2001 unapproved cotton seed containing the Bt gene was found growing on a large scale in Gujarat, sold by a local seed company, and “stealth seeds” of this kind continued to circulate alongside the licensed hybrids (Herring 2007). A second-generation product with two Bt genes followed in 2006.

    Adoption was extraordinarily rapid. Within a decade Bt hybrids covered the great majority of India’s cotton area, and the country’s cotton output rose steeply, making it one of the world’s largest producers and exporters. How much of this gain was due to the Bt gene is disputed. A panel study of farm households in four cotton states between 2002 and 2008 by Jonas Kathage and Matin Qaim found that adoption raised yields and profits per acre substantially and improved household living standards (Kathage and Qaim 2012). Keshav Kranthi and Glenn Davis Stone, reviewing the longer record, argued that yields stopped rising after the first years, that much of the early gain came from more fertiliser, irrigation and new hybrids, and that the pink bollworm had evolved resistance to the Bt toxins, so that insecticide use rose again (Kranthi and Stone 2020). Resistance in the pink bollworm was reported from Gujarat from about 2009 and had become widespread by the middle of the following decade. Stone’s earlier fieldwork in Warangal described how the rapid turnover of seed brands left farmers choosing seed by fashion and rumour rather than by experience, a process he called “agricultural deskilling” (Stone 2007).

    The most painful argument concerned farmers’ suicides. Critics, Vandana Shiva prominent among them, linked the spread of costly Bt seed to indebtedness and to the suicides of cotton farmers in Maharashtra, Andhra Pradesh and elsewhere. A review of the evidence by Guillaume Gruère and Debdatta Sengupta found no sign that Bt cotton had caused a resurgence of suicides, while acknowledging that its failure in particular seasons and places, especially on rain-fed land, had contributed to distress (Gruère and Sengupta 2011). The causes of farmers’ suicides are many (Section 20.2), and no single factor, the seed included, explains them.

    Cotton remains the only GM crop approved for commercial cultivation in India. A Bt brinjal developed by Mahyco and public institutions was cleared by the regulator in 2009, but in February 2010 the Minister of Environment, Jairam Ramesh, after public hearings in several cities, imposed an indefinite moratorium on its release. A GM mustard hybrid developed at the University of Delhi was recommended for environmental release in 2022 and became the subject of litigation in the Supreme Court. The arguments on each side are by now familiar: supporters point to higher yields, reduced spraying and the success of public-sector research; opponents point to risks to biodiversity and to India’s many wild relatives of crops, to the weakness of regulation and monitoring, to the dependence of farmers on purchased hybrid seed that cannot be saved, and to the market power of a few seed companies.

    These disputes bear directly on the themes of this chapter. Hybrid seed, GM or not, does not breed true, so that farmers who grow it must buy seed every season; the right to save, exchange and resell seed protected by the Protection of Plant Varieties and Farmers’ Rights Act (Section 20.8) has little meaning for a hybrid crop. Indian patent law excludes plants and seeds as such from patent protection, but the patenting of the genetic traits within them, and the fees charged for them, have been fought over in the courts, and the government has regulated the price of Bt cotton seed since 2015. The spread of Bt hybrids also displaced most of the older Indian tree cottons (Gossypium arboreum) that had been grown for millennia (Section 9.7), some of which are now being revived for their hardiness on rain-fed land. Whatever view one takes of the technology, the history of Bt cotton shows that the questions raised by seed — who owns it, who may save it, and who bears the risk when it fails — are as old as agriculture and as new as the latest regulatory decision.

    20.10 Ethnobotany¶

    The term “ethnobotany” was coined by the American botanist John Harshberger in the 1890s for the study of the plants used by particular peoples. In a broader sense the documentation of plant knowledge from local informants has a long history in India: the Hortus Malabaricus (1678–1693), compiled under the Dutch governor Hendrik van Rheede, drew on the knowledge of Malayali physicians, notably Itty Achuden, and of Konkani brahmin informants (Manilal 1980); and colonial surveys such as Watt’s Dictionary of the Economic Products of India and Kirtikar and Basu’s Indian Medicinal Plants assembled local uses on a large scale (Watt 1889; Kirtikar and Basu 1935). These are discussed in Chapters 6 and 12.

    Ethnobotany as an organised field in India is associated above all with S. K. Jain of the Botanical Survey of India, who from the 1960s carried out and encouraged field studies among Adivasi communities and whose work led to a national coordinated research project on ethnobiology in the 1980s. His Dictionary of Indian Folk Medicine and Ethnobotany (Jain 1991) brought together recorded folk uses of plants from across the country. Since then ethnobotanical studies have multiplied. Their quality varies greatly: the best combine careful botanical identification backed by voucher specimens, ethnographic understanding of how knowledge is held and transmitted, and attention to consent and benefit-sharing; the weakest are lists of plants and uses with little context. A recorded folk use is evidence of what people do and believe; it is a starting point for pharmacological or agronomic research, not a demonstration of efficacy.

    20.11 Biodiversity Hotspots and Agrobiodiversity¶

    Conservation International’s scheme of global “biodiversity hotspots” — regions with exceptional concentrations of endemic species that have lost much of their original vegetation — was put forward by Norman Myers and colleagues (Myers et al. 2000). Parts of four hotspots lie in India: the Himalaya; Indo-Burma, which includes much of north-eastern India; the Western Ghats and Sri Lanka; and Sundaland, represented in India by the Nicobar Islands. These regions are also among the homes of India’s Adivasi communities, and the sacred groves, shifting cultivation systems and forest institutions described in Chapters 11 and 10 lie largely within them.

    India is equally important for agricultural biodiversity. N. I. Vavilov identified the Indian region as one of the major centres of origin and diversity of cultivated plants, among them rice, sugarcane, many pulses, several cucurbits, mango, citrus and other fruits (Vavilov 1951); modern archaeobotany has refined but not overturned this picture (Chapter 2). The landraces of rice, millets, pulses and vegetables maintained by farmers, many of them in rain-fed and hill regions, are a genetic resource of great value for breeding crops adapted to drought, flooding, salinity and new pests. The United Nations’ International Year of Millets in 2023, proposed by India, drew attention to crops that the Green Revolution had neglected. The conservation of this diversity — on farms, where it continues to evolve, and in gene banks — is one of the clearest ways in which traditional agriculture remains relevant.

    20.12 Evaluating Traditional Practices Responsibly¶

    How, then, should traditional agricultural and biological knowledge be evaluated? The chapters of this volume suggest a few principles.

    1. Establish what the source says. A practice must be identified in a reliable edition or in documented field practice before it can be evaluated. Much of what circulates as “ancient Indian agriculture” — invented verses, false attributions, misdated texts — fails at this first step (Section 1.5).

    2. Identify the plants, animals and materials. Sanskrit and vernacular names often cover several species, and identifications in modern editions are sometimes guesses. A test of the “traditional” use of a plant is meaningless if it is the wrong plant.

    3. Test, do not assume. Claims about yields, pest control or soil fertility should be tested in replicated field trials against appropriate controls, over several seasons and sites, and published so that others can check them. Antiquity is not evidence of efficacy, and neither is long use, since ineffective practices can persist for centuries.

    4. Separate ritual from technique. Many texts combine practical instructions with ritual and astrological prescriptions. Both are part of the history of the tradition; only the former can be tested agronomically. The Sun’s passage through the nakṣatras, for example, is a solar calendar, so it is no surprise that sowing dates keyed to it track the average onset of the monsoon; this does not show that nakṣatra-based rules can forecast the rain of a particular year.

    5. Respect context and scale. A practice that worked on a small farm with abundant labour, cattle and common grazing land may not work, or may not be affordable, under present conditions; and what suits one region may fail in another.

    6. Count the harms. Some classical prescriptions use poisonous substances or encode social exclusion. Evaluation must include safety and justice, not only productivity.

    7. Recognise the holders of knowledge. Where living communities hold the knowledge being studied, their consent, participation and share in any benefits are ethical and legal requirements.

    Applied in this way, traditional knowledge can do a good deal. It has supplied useful materials (neem as a botanical pesticide), crops and landraces adapted to difficult environments, cropping systems that spread risk, ways of managing water and commons, and leads for research — as when Tu Youyou’s team in China, guided by a fourth-century Chinese medical handbook, isolated the antimalarial artemisinin, or when clinical work on Rauvolfia serpentina in India in the 1940s preceded the isolation of reserpine (see Volume 13). Traditional knowledge cannot, by itself, feed a population many times larger than any that pre-modern Indian agriculture supported, replace modern weather forecasting, or substitute for fair prices, credit, land rights and public investment. Its value is greatest when it is treated as a body of observations and practices to be understood historically and tested carefully, rather than as an inheritance beyond question or as superstition to be discarded.

    20.13 Conclusion: Knowledge of Fields, Plants and Animals in India¶

    This volume began with the first farmers of Mehrgarh, who cultivated barley and wheat and kept cattle, sheep and goats some nine thousand years ago, and with the archaeobotanical evidence that rice, millets, pulses, cotton and many other plants were domesticated or adopted in the subcontinent over the following millennia (Chapters 1 and 2). It then followed the written tradition: the Vedic and Buddhist texts, the Arthaśāstra and the Tamil Saṅgam poems; the rain-forecasting chapters of the Bṛhatsaṃhitā and the late, probably Bengali Kṛṣi-Parāśara (Chapter 3); the Vṛkṣāyurveda tradition of Varāhamihira, Surapāla and the Upavanavinoda, with its kuṇapajala manure and its view of plants as living beings (Chapter 4), and the flowers, perfumes and betel that gardens supplied (Chapter 5); and the classification of plants in the Vedic, medical, lexical and Jain and Buddhist traditions (Chapter 6). It examined soils, water and irrigation, crops and seeds, and the agroecological practices of mixed cropping, pest management and agroforestry (Chapters 7 to 10); forests and sacred groves (Chapter 11); medicinal and useful plants (Chapter 12); livestock, dairying and food (Chapters 13 and 14); elephants and horses, with the Hastyāyurveda and the Śālihotra tradition (Chapter 15); wild animals, hunting and wildlife protection (Chapter 16); and birds, insects and aquatic life (Chapter 17). It placed all of these within the agrarian society that produced them — its caste hierarchy, its unrecorded labour, its land grants, revenue systems and colonial transformations (Chapter 18) — followed the rise of agricultural research institutions from the famine commissions to the high-yielding varieties (Chapter 19), and, in this chapter, traced their afterlife in modern India.

    Three conclusions stand out. First, Indian knowledge of fields, plants and animals is genuinely rich and genuinely old. Its texts record careful observation of weather, soils, plant growth and animal health; its practitioners developed crops, breeds, irrigation works and land-management institutions that sustained dense populations for millennia. Second, this knowledge was never a single system and never the possession of a single class. The Sanskrit treatises are the work of literate, mostly brahmin and courtly authors; much of the most important knowledge was held by cultivators, herders, fishers, forest peoples and women who did not write it down, and by traditions — Tamil, Buddhist, Jain, Adivasi, Indo-Islamic — that have their own histories. Third, the value of this inheritance today depends on honesty about it. Invented verses, imagined priorities and unverified claims of “scientific validation” do it no service; careful editions, sound dating, field research and fair testing do. Read in that spirit, the agricultural and biological knowledge of India is not a lost golden age to be restored, but a long and varied human achievement that can still teach us how people have lived with, and changed, the land and its living things.

    Summary¶

    The Green Revolution ended India’s dependence on food aid but brought groundwater depletion, soil and pesticide problems, loss of crop diversity and regional inequality. Agrarian distress has encouraged natural-farming movements whose claims remain unproven. Bt cotton spread rapidly after 2002, and the regulation of genetically modified crops remains contested. The neem, turmeric and basmati disputes led to laws on plant varieties, farmers’ rights and biodiversity, and the Kani–Jeevani case shows both the promise and the difficulty of benefit-sharing. Traditional practices deserve to be tested rather than assumed.
