    Chapter 11: Forests, Sacred Groves and Sacred Trees¶

    11.1 The Forest and the Village¶

    Indian agriculture was won from the forest and always lived beside it. The fields of the Ganga plain, the Deccan and the southern deltas were cleared from woodland over many centuries; villages depended on neighbouring forests for fuel, fodder, leaf manure, timber, fibres, fruits, honey and medicines; and forest-dwelling peoples, who were often not farmers in the plough-based sense, were trading partners, subjects, soldiers and objects of suspicion for agrarian states. This chapter follows the forest through several kinds of evidence: early texts and the Arthaśāstra; the edicts of Aśoka; the community-protected patches of forest known as sacred groves; the cultural roles of particular sacred trees; the Bishnoi tradition of the Thar; and the modern history of forest law and forest protest, from colonial reservation to the Forest Rights Act of 2006.

    Two cautions run through the chapter. First, reverence for trees and groves is real and old, but it is not the same thing as a conservation policy, and it has coexisted throughout Indian history with large-scale clearance, hunting and the expansion of cultivation. Second, the knowledge and institutions of forest peoples — Adivasi and other forest-dwelling communities — deserve treatment on their own terms; that treatment is given in Volume 23 (Jana–Jāti), and this chapter refers to it rather than duplicating it.

    11.2 Forests in Early Texts¶

    11.2.1 Araṇya and Grāma¶

    Vedic literature contrasts the settled world of the village (grāma) with the wild space of the forest (araṇya). Domestic animals (grāmya paśu) are distinguished from wild ones (āraṇya paśu), and plants likewise from those of the wild. The forest was a place of danger and of power. A late hymn of the Ṛgveda (10.146) addresses Araṇyānī, the goddess of the forest: the poet describes the sounds heard at dusk in the woods, which make a lonely traveller imagine that someone is calling or that a cart is approaching, and praises the forest as fragrant, rich in food though untilled, and the mother of wild beasts, which does not kill unless another attacks (Jamison and Brereton 2014). The Atharvaveda’s Hymn to the Earth (12.1) asks that the earth’s hills, snowy mountains and forest be kindly to its people (Whitney 1905). The Āraṇyakas, “forest books”, take their name from the forest as a place for teaching rites and doctrines considered too dangerous or esoteric for the village.

    The forest was also the place of the renouncer. In the Upaniṣads and in the early Buddhist and Jain literature, teachers and ascetics live in groves and forests outside settlements; the Buddha’s awakening took place under a tree, and his teaching was given in parks such as the deer park at Isipatana (Sarnath) and the Jetavana grove at Śrāvastī. The Buddhist monastic code counts the destruction of plant life (bhūtagāma) among the offences requiring confession, because laypeople regarded plants as possessing life; Jain ascetic rules, which recognise one-sensed life in plants, are stricter still (see Section 6.7).

    11.2.2 Jāṅgala and Ānūpa¶

    Classical medicine divided land into dry, open jāṅgala, marshy ānūpa and an intermediate type (Section 1.6). Its ideal was not the forest at all but the healthy, game-rich jāṅgala savanna (Zimmermann 1987); that the English “jungle”, derived from this word, now names dense wet forest is a reminder that the vocabulary of Indian landscapes cannot be read through modern English.

    11.2.3 Forests in the Epics and in Kāvya¶

    The epics are full of forests. The Pāṇḍavas and Rāma spend their exiles in forests; the Rāmāyaṇa’s Daṇḍaka forest is the frontier where Rāma encounters ascetics and rākṣasas; and the Mahābhārata narrates the burning of the Khāṇḍava forest by Agni with the help of Kṛṣṇa and Arjuna, with the slaughter of the creatures that tried to escape, after which the Pāṇḍava city of Indraprastha is founded in the region. Historians have often read the Khāṇḍava episode as a literary memory of forest clearance by fire, though it is a mythic narrative and cannot be dated or located as an event. Sanskrit poetry developed the idealised tapovana or hermitage grove, a place where animals are not hunted and ascetics live at peace with deer and trees; in the first act of Kālidāsa’s Abhijñānaśākuntala, King Duṣyanta, hunting a deer, is stopped by an ascetic who tells him that the animal belongs to the hermitage and must not be killed. Such scenes express an ideal of the protected forest; they are not descriptions of forest management.

    11.3 The Forest in the Arthaśāstra¶

    The Arthaśāstra gives the fullest early account of how a state might use forests. The text is composite and in its present form dates from c. 50 BCE–300 CE (Olivelle 2013); its attribution to Kauṭilya, minister of Candragupta Maurya, is traditional. It should be read as a political treatise describing what a king ought to do, not as a record of Mauryan administration. Its treatment of law and statecraft is discussed in Volume 18 (Rājanīti and Dharmaśāstra).

    In its chapter on the disposal of land not fit for cultivation (Book 2), the text recommends that the king set apart several kinds of forest (Kangle 1965; Olivelle 2013):

    • forests granted to brahmins for Vedic study, for the soma plant and for austerities, in which plants and animals are to be given safety;

    • a game forest (mṛgavana) for the king’s recreation, enclosed and stocked with animals rendered harmless, and areas in which animals are declared protected;

    • productive forests (dravyavana) for each kind of forest produce, with workshops for processing it;

    • elephant forests (hastivana, nāgavana) on the frontier, guarded by elephant-forest keepers.

    The productive forests were under the superintendent of forest produce (kupyādhyakṣa), whose chapter lists the classes of material the state should obtain: hard timbers, bamboos, creepers, fibre plants, materials for ropes, leaves, flowers, medicinal substances, poisons, and animal products such as skins, bones, teeth, horns and hooves (Kangle 1965). Fines were laid down for damage to the state’s forests and for killing animals in protected areas; the superintendent of abattoirs was to prevent the slaughter of animals declared protected. The protection of elephants was the most emphatic: the text prescribes death for anyone who kills one, a rule that Trautmann (2015) explains by the Indian practice of capturing rather than breeding war elephants (Section 15.2; Volume 20, Yuddha).

    Scholarly Note: The Āṭavika: Forest Peoples as the State Saw Them

    The āṭavika is sometimes described in modern writing as a “forest superintendent”. This is a mistake. In the Arthaśāstra the āṭavikas are forest peoples and their chiefs, living in the aṭavī (wild forest) beyond or within the borders of the kingdom. The text regards them as a standing danger — they are listed among the troubles that can afflict a country — and at the same time as a resource: forest troops (āṭavikabala) appear among the kinds of troops a king may employ, and forest chiefs can be won over, set against enemies or used as guides (Kangle 1965; Olivelle 2013). Aśoka’s Thirteenth Rock Edict similarly addresses the forest peoples in his dominions, urging them towards his dhamma while reminding them of his power to punish (Singh 2008). The forest peoples’ own view of the forest is not recorded in these texts; for it see Volume 23.

    The Arthaśāstra’s forest categories are sometimes presented as an ancient “protected-area system” that anticipated modern conservation. The resemblance is superficial. The text’s aim is the fiscal and military benefit of the king: forests are to be reserved because they supply revenue, timber, elephants and defensive cover, and the sanctuaries it mentions are royal or brahmanical preserves. Its value for a history of Indian forests lies precisely in showing how early a state could conceive of the forest as a resource to be classified, guarded and taxed.

    Cross-Reference: Forest Peoples’ Knowledge

    The ecological knowledge of Adivasi and other forest-dwelling communities — their classification and use of forest plants and animals, shifting cultivation, honey collection, fire management, and their own sacred groves and forest institutions — is the subject of Volume 23 (Jana–Jāti). Shifting cultivation as an agricultural system is discussed in Chapter 10; medicinal and useful forest plants in Chapter 12; and elephants and their forests in Chapter 15.

    11.4 The Edicts of Aśoka¶

    The inscriptions of Aśoka (r. c. 268–232 BCE) are the earliest datable Indian documents to concern themselves with animals and trees (Singh 2008). Their concern arises from Aśoka’s dhamma, with its emphasis on non-injury, rather than from anything like a modern ecological programme, but their provisions are concrete:

    • The First Rock Edict forbids animal sacrifice in the place of issue and reports that the slaughter of animals for the royal kitchen has been reduced to a few and would cease.

    • The Second Rock Edict states that the king has provided for medical treatment for humans and for animals, has had medicinal herbs, roots and fruits brought and planted where they did not grow, and has had wells dug and trees planted along roads for the use of animals and people.

    • The Fifth Pillar Edict, issued in the twenty-sixth year after Aśoka’s consecration, lists animals that are not to be killed — among them parrots, ruddy geese, wild geese, bats, tortoises, porcupines and squirrels, together with all four-footed animals that are neither used nor eaten — though several names in the list cannot be identified with certainty. It protects she-goats, ewes and sows that are with young or in milk; restricts the killing and sale of fish and the castration of animals on particular days; and forbids burning chaff that contains living creatures and setting fire to forests without purpose or in order to kill living beings.

    • The Seventh Pillar Edict records that banyan trees were planted along roads to give shade to animals and people, mango groves planted, and wells and rest-houses provided at intervals.

    These measures were limited. Fishing and the killing of many animals remained lawful on most days, and the edicts did not prohibit hunting by others or the clearance of forest for agriculture. What they show is a ruler presenting the protection of named animals and the planting of useful trees as a duty of kingship — an idea that recurs in later royal inscriptions recording gifts of tanks, groves and roadside trees.

    11.5 Sacred Groves¶

    11.5.1 Names and Distribution¶

    A sacred grove is a patch of vegetation, from a few trees around a shrine to many hectares of forest, that a community protects because it belongs to a deity, a spirit or the ancestors. Such groves are found throughout India, especially in the Western Ghats, the north-east, the central Indian Adivasi belt, the Himalaya and the Thar, and they have counterparts in West Africa, East Asia and elsewhere. They go by many local names (Malhotra et al. 2001; Gadgil and Vartak 1975):

    • devrāī in Maharashtra, Goa and the Konkan;

    • devarakāḍu (“the god’s forest”) in Karnataka, especially in Kodagu;

    • kāvu, including the serpent groves (sarpakkāvu), in Kerala;

    • kōvilkāṭu (“temple forest”) in Tamil Nadu;

    • oran in Rajasthan, a protected tract often dedicated to a local deity and used as common pasture;

    • sarnā among the Munda, Oraon and other communities of Jharkhand and adjacent regions, and jāherthān among the Santal;

    • law kyntang and law lyngdoh among the Khasi of Meghalaya, where the grove at Mawphlang is among the best known;

    • umanglai among the Meitei of Manipur.

    There are many other names, and the same word may cover rather different institutions in different places.

    How many sacred groves exist is not known. Estimates for India range from some tens of thousands to well over a hundred thousand, depending on what is counted (a single sacred tree, a shrine with a few trees, a large forest) and on how thoroughly particular states have been surveyed; the compilation by Malhotra et al. (2001) collected the state-level surveys then available and showed how uneven they were, so that precise national figures are not possible.

    11.5.2 Deities, Rules and Management¶

    Each grove is associated with a presiding power: a village goddess, a guardian deity such as the tiger-god Vāghobā of the Western Ghats or Aiyaṉār of Tamil Nadu, serpent deities in Kerala, a forest spirit, or the ancestors and deities of an Adivasi community. The rules that protect the grove vary greatly. In some, nothing may be removed, not even dead wood or fallen leaves; in others dead wood may be gathered, or fruits picked, or the grove opened for use at particular times. Hunting and cutting green trees are the most common prohibitions. Enforcement rests on belief that the deity will punish offenders with illness, crop failure or misfortune, on the authority of a priest or headman, and on community fines and sanctions. Periodic festivals, offerings and the maintenance of the shrine renew the grove’s status.

    These institutions should not be idealised. Access to groves and to the rituals held in them is often governed by caste, clan and gender: some groves may be entered only by particular lineages or by men, and the priestly or managing roles are frequently hereditary. Grove rules are part of local systems of authority as well as of local ecology. Their governance is discussed for Adivasi communities in Volume 23.

    11.5.3 What Ecologists Have Found¶

    Scientific interest in Indian sacred groves dates above all from the work of Madhav Gadgil and V. D. Vartak, who surveyed groves in Maharashtra in the early 1970s. In a short paper of 1975 they reported that the groves preserved vegetation much less disturbed than the surrounding countryside, including large old trees, woody climbers and species that had become rare elsewhere, and argued for their continued protection (Gadgil and Vartak 1975). Later work in Kerala, Karnataka, Meghalaya, Manipur and elsewhere, brought together in the volumes edited by Ramakrishnan et al. (1998) and in Malhotra et al. (2001), has confirmed that many groves are refuges for plants and animals lost from the surrounding landscape, that some protect springs and stream heads, and that they can serve as seed sources for the regeneration of degraded land.

    The findings are, however, heterogeneous. Groves differ enormously in size, condition and history. Some are fragments of old forest; others have been planted, are dominated by a few cultivated or sacred species, or are heavily used. A small grove isolated among fields cannot maintain populations of large animals, and edge effects, invasive plants such as Lantana camara and Chromolaena odorata, and grazing can degrade even well-protected patches. Ecologists have therefore moved away from treating sacred groves as untouched remnants of primeval forest towards studying each grove as a particular product of local history, belief and use.

    Debate: Are Sacred Groves a Conservation Strategy?

    It is often said that sacred groves were created to conserve biodiversity and that the rules surrounding them encode ecological knowledge. Gadgil and others have argued that religious sanctions can function as “prudent” rules of resource use, protecting resources whose value a community recognises. Critics reply that the motives recorded by the people who keep groves are religious and social — the deity’s anger, the honour of a lineage, the need for a place of worship — and that conservation, where it occurs, is often a by-product. Both positions can be true in different places. The safest conclusion is that sacred groves show how religious and customary institutions can protect patches of vegetation for long periods, not that they were consciously designed as conservation reserves (Gadgil and Guha 1992; Malhotra et al. 2001).

    11.5.4 Threats and Protection¶

    Sacred groves have been lost or reduced through the extension of cultivation and plantations, road building and urban growth, the decline of belief among younger people, and the conversion of the land to other uses when it becomes valuable. A frequently observed process is the replacement of an open-air shrine in a grove by a built temple: the deity is “Sanskritised”, trees are cleared for the building and its approaches, and the grove shrinks. State action has cut both ways. Colonial and post-colonial forest departments took many groves into government forest, which sometimes protected them and sometimes weakened the community authority that had kept them (Gadgil and Guha 1992).

    Indian law now provides some instruments for their recognition. Amendments to the Wildlife (Protection) Act in 2002 created the categories of “community reserve” and “conservation reserve”; the Biological Diversity Act of 2002 allows state governments to notify “biodiversity heritage sites”; and the Forest Rights Act of 2006 (Section 11.11) allows communities to claim rights over forests they have traditionally protected. Several sacred groves have been brought under such provisions, but most remain protected, if at all, by custom alone.

    11.6 Sacred Trees¶

    Beyond the grove, particular species of tree are themselves sacred. Their protection is uneven — a sacred tree may stand in a field that has otherwise been cleared — but their cultural importance is very old. The following are among the most widely revered; their medicinal uses are discussed in Chapter 12 and in Volume 13 (Āyurveda).

    Aśvattha (pipal, Ficus religiosa).
    Pipal leaves appear as a motif on Harappan seals and pottery (Kenoyer 1998). In Vedic ritual the upper fire-drill (araṇi) is to be made of the wood of an aśvattha growing on a śamī. The aśvattha is the Bodhi tree under which the Buddha attained awakening at Bodh Gaya, and is venerated by Buddhists as such. In the Bhagavad Gītā Kṛṣṇa declares himself “the aśvattha among all trees” (10.26), and the fifteenth chapter opens with the image of the cosmic aśvattha with roots above and branches below. Felling a pipal is widely regarded as a grave offence, and pipal trees are circumambulated, especially by women.
    Vaṭa or nyagrodha (banyan, Ficus benghalensis).
    In the Chāndogya Upaniṣad (6.12) Uddālaka Āruṇi has his son Śvetaketu break open a banyan fruit and then a seed within it, to show that the great tree grows from an invisible subtle essence (Olivelle 1998). Married women in western and northern India worship the banyan in the Vaṭasāvitrī observance, which recalls Sāvitrī’s recovery of her husband from Yama; Śiva as teacher (Dakṣiṇāmūrti) is shown seated beneath a banyan; and the shade of large banyans has long served as a meeting place for village assemblies. Aśoka’s planting of banyans along roads has been noted above.
    Udumbara (cluster fig, Ficus racemosa).
    Its wood was used for ritual posts and implements in Vedic sacrifice. In Buddhist literature the udumbara “flower”, which is never seen because the fig’s flowers are hidden inside the fruit, became a simile for something extremely rare, such as the appearance of a Buddha. In Maharashtra the udumbara is associated with Dattātreya.
    Śamī (khejri, Prosopis cineraria).
    Besides its role in the Vedic fire-drill, the śamī is the tree in which the Pāṇḍavas, in the Mahābhārata, hid their weapons during their year of concealment at Virāṭa’s court; in parts of India it is worshipped on Vijayadaśamī. In the Thar it is a mainstay of farming: a leguminous tree that tolerates drought, its leaves are lopped for fodder and its pods eaten, and crops are grown beneath it. It is the tree at the centre of the Bishnoi tradition (Section 11.8).
    Bilva (bael, Aegle marmelos).
    Its trifoliate leaves are offered to Śiva, and the tree is commonly planted near Śiva shrines.
    Tulasī (holy basil, Ocimum tenuiflorum).
    Not a tree but a small aromatic herb, it is grown in courtyards on a raised platform (tulasī-vṛndāvana) and worshipped daily in many Vaiṣṇava households; its ritual marriage to Viṣṇu (tulasī-vivāha) is celebrated in the month of Kārttika.
    Kadamba (Neolamarckia cadamba).
    Its fragrant ball-shaped flower heads, which open with the rains, made it a tree of the monsoon in Sanskrit poetry, and it is closely associated with Kṛṣṇa’s youth in Vraja.

    Other trees — the nimba (neem), the mango, the aśoka (Saraca asoca), the pārijāta, the coconut, and the palāśa (Butea monosperma) — have their own ritual roles in different regions. The Purāṇas and later ritual literature praise the planting of trees and the dedication of groves as works of merit, comparable to digging wells and tanks; a widely quoted Sanskrit verse, attributed in modern collections to different Purāṇas, promises that one who plants a pipal, a neem, a banyan, flowering trees, pomegranates, citrons and mangoes will not go to hell. Such merit literature encouraged planting near settlements; it was not an ecological design. Modern ecology recognises wild figs as important food sources for many fruit-eating birds and mammals, but that is a modern finding, not the reason for their veneration.

    Bhagavad Gītā 15.1

    ūrdhvamūlam adhaḥśākham aśvatthaṃ prāhur avyayam
    chandāṃsi yasya parṇāni yas taṃ veda sa vedavit
    “They speak of an imperishable aśvattha with roots above and branches below, whose leaves are the Vedic hymns; one who knows it knows the Veda.”

    11.7 The Pañcavaṭī¶

    In the Rāmāyaṇa, Pañcavaṭī is the place in the Daṇḍaka forest, near the Godāvarī, where Rāma, Sītā and Lakṣmaṇa build a hut during their exile and from which Sītā is abducted; tradition locates it at Nashik. The name is usually explained as “five banyans”. In later religious practice the word came to denote a planting of five sacred trees, set out together near a temple, a house or a place of meditation. The lists of the five trees vary between texts, regions and communities. One frequently repeated set comprises the aśvattha, vaṭa, bilva, āmalakī (Phyllanthus emblica) and aśoka; others include the udumbara, nimba or other trees, and some take the name literally as five banyans. There is no single canonical pañcavaṭī. A planting of five long-lived trees creates shade and a small habitat for birds and insects; the texts present it as a sacred planting, not as an ecological one.

    11.8 The Bishnoi¶

    The Bishnoi community of western Rajasthan and adjoining regions follows the teachings of Jambhojī, also called Jambheśvara (1451–1536), who according to tradition founded the community in the Thar towards the end of the fifteenth century. The name is commonly explained from the twenty-nine (bīs + nau) rules of conduct that Jambhojī laid down. These include rules of personal purity and religious practice — such as filtering drinking water, daily bathing and abstention from intoxicants — together with prohibitions on cutting green trees and on killing animals, and an injunction to show compassion to all living beings. Bishnoi villages are known for their protection of the blackbuck and the chinkara, which can often be seen grazing close to Bishnoi fields, and for their care of khejri trees.

    According to Bishnoi tradition, in 1730 soldiers of the ruler of Jodhpur came to the village of Khejarli to fell khejri trees for use in construction. A Bishnoi woman, Amṛtā Devī, clung to a tree and was killed, and her daughters and then many others followed; the tradition gives the number of the dead as 363. The ruler is said to have stopped the felling and issued an order protecting trees and animals in Bishnoi villages. The event is commemorated annually at Khejarli. Its details rest on community tradition rather than on contemporary documents, and they should be presented as such; but the Khejarli story has become one of the founding narratives of Indian environmentalism, and the Bishnoi themselves are a living example of a religious community whose rules protect trees and wild animals.

    11.9 Colonial Forestry¶

    The most profound change in the history of Indian forests came under British rule. Before the mid-nineteenth century, forests were cleared for cultivation, exploited for timber (notably Malabar teak for shipbuilding) and hunted over, but much forest land remained under the customary use of local communities. The expansion of the railways from the 1850s created an enormous demand for timber for sleepers and fuel, and the colonial state moved to assert control over forests as state property (Gadgil and Guha 1992).

    A Forest Department was established in 1864 under the German forester Dietrich Brandis as the first Inspector General of Forests. The first Indian Forest Act was passed in 1865. The far more comprehensive Act of 1878 divided state forests into “reserved” forests, in which customary rights were largely extinguished or strictly regulated, “protected” forests, in which certain uses were permitted, and “village” forests. Within the administration there was debate over how far the rights of villagers should be recognised. K. Sivaramakrishnan’s study of eastern India has shown how forest policy was worked out regionally, through negotiation with local elites and the shifting categories of “forest” and “waste”, rather than imposed by a single plan (Sivaramakrishnan 1999). The overall outcome, as Gadgil and Guha (1992) argue, was a system that turned the customary use of forests by peasants, pastoralists and forest peoples into a privilege granted, and often withdrawn, by the state. The Indian Forest Act of 1927 consolidated this law and remains, with amendments, in force.

    The consequences were far-reaching. Shifting cultivation was restricted or banned in many areas; grazing, lopping and the collection of forest produce were regulated or charged for; and commercially valuable species such as teak, sal and deodar were favoured in managed plantations at the expense of mixed forest used by villagers. Forest peoples lost access to land and resources on which their livelihoods depended. Resistance took many forms, from petitions and the breaking of forest rules to rebellions in which forest grievances played a large part, and the incendiary fires and protests in the Kumaon hills in the early 1920s. After the Kumaon disturbances, rules framed in 1931 allowed villages in Kumaon to form forest councils (van pañcāyat) to manage some forests as commons, an institution that still exists (Guha 1989).

    Colonial rule also transformed the relationship with wild animals, through bounties on “vermin”, the shikār of British and princely elites, and game reserves that excluded local people; this history, and the extinction of the cheetah, are traced in Chapter 16 (Rangarajan 2001).

    11.10 Chipko¶

    The best-known Indian forest movement began in the Garhwal Himalaya (then in Uttar Pradesh, now Uttarakhand) in 1973. Villagers organised by the Dasholi Gram Swarajya Mandal, a cooperative led by Chandi Prasad Bhatt, protested when the forest department refused them ash trees for agricultural implements but allotted trees in the same area to a sports-goods company. They threatened to embrace the trees to prevent felling — cipaknā in Hindi means “to stick, to cling” — and the movement took its name from this. In 1974 at Reni, women led by Gaura Devi stopped a felling party while the men of the village were away. Over the following years the movement spread through the hills, with Sunderlal Bahuguna becoming its best-known spokesman, and in 1980 the central government imposed a moratorium on commercial green felling in the Himalayan forests of Uttar Pradesh.

    Guha (1989) placed Chipko within a long history of peasant resistance to commercial and state forestry in the region, going back to the protests against forest restrictions in the colonial period. He emphasised that the villagers’ demands were about subsistence and local control of forests — fodder, fuel, small timber, employment — as much as about preserving trees for their own sake. Other writers have read Chipko as a women’s movement or as an expression of a religious attitude to nature. All of these readings capture part of the truth, but Guha’s caution against turning Chipko into a simple parable of tree-hugging is well taken. The connection often drawn between Chipko and the Khejarli story of 1730 is one of later inspiration and commemoration, not of historical continuity between the two events.

    11.11 After Independence: Forest Policy and Forest Rights¶

    Independent India at first continued the colonial approach. The National Forest Policy of 1952 asserted the national interest in forests over local claims, and large areas were cleared for agriculture, dams, mining and industry or converted to commercial plantations. The Wildlife (Protection) Act of 1972 and Project Tiger (1973) created a network of protected areas, often at the cost of displacing or restricting the people who lived in them (Rangarajan 2001). The Forest (Conservation) Act of 1980 required central approval for the diversion of forest land to other uses.

    From the 1980s policy began to change. The National Forest Policy of 1988 gave priority to ecological stability and to meeting the needs of local people for fuel, fodder and minor forest produce over commercial timber production. Building on experiments in which villagers had been given a share of forest produce in return for protecting degraded forests — the best known being at Arabari in West Bengal from the early 1970s — the Government of India in 1990 issued guidelines for Joint Forest Management, under which village forest protection committees and forest departments share responsibility and usufruct. Joint Forest Management spread widely, but critics noted that it left control in the hands of the forest department and that benefits were often small and unequally shared within villages.

    The Scheduled Tribes and Other Traditional Forest Dwellers (Recognition of Forest Rights) Act of 2006, usually called the Forest Rights Act, went further. Its preamble acknowledges that the forest rights of forest-dwelling communities had not been adequately recognised in the consolidation of state forests in the colonial period and after, resulting in a “historical injustice”. The Act provides for the recognition of individual rights to land under cultivation and of community rights, including rights to protect, regenerate and manage community forest resources, with the village assembly (gram sabhā) at the centre of the process. Its implementation has been uneven and contested, and it remains the subject of political and legal argument; one widely noted application came in 2013, when the Supreme Court required the village assemblies of the Niyamgiri hills in Odisha, home of the Dongria Kondh, to be consulted on a proposed bauxite mine, which they then rejected. These questions of Adivasi rights are treated in detail in Volume 23.

    Commons and Their Governance

    Much recent scholarship on Indian forests is part of a wider study of common-pool resources — forests, pastures, fisheries, irrigation systems — managed by communities rather than by the state or by private owners. Reviewing this literature, Agrawal (2001) identified the conditions under which local institutions tend to govern such resources sustainably: among them, small and clearly defined groups and boundaries, rules matched to local conditions, effective monitoring and graduated sanctions, low-cost means of resolving disputes, and support (or at least non-interference) from external authorities. Sacred groves, van pañcāyats, orans and community forests under the Forest Rights Act can all be studied in these terms. The research does not show that community management always succeeds, but it does show that it can, and that the colonial assumption that only the state could protect forests was mistaken.

    Summary¶

    Indian texts distinguish the village from the forest and treat the forest as both dangerous and sacred: the home of the Ṛgvedic Araṇyānī, of ascetics and renouncers, and of the epic exiles. The Arthaśāstra classifies forests by their use to the state — forests for brahmins, game and sanctuary forests, productive forests and elephant forests — and treats forest peoples as both a threat and a resource. Aśoka’s edicts protect named animals, forbid the pointless burning of forests and record the planting of medicinal plants and roadside trees. Sacred groves, known by many regional names, are protected by religious and customary rules; ecologists since Gadgil and Vartak have shown that many are important refuges for plants and animals, though they vary greatly and their number is uncertain. Particular trees — the aśvattha, vaṭa, udumbara, śamī, bilva, kadamba and the tulasī plant — carry deep cultural meanings, and the lists of the five trees of a pañcavaṭī vary. The Bishnoi tradition and the Chipko movement show communities defending trees, while colonial forest law, from the Acts of 1865 and 1878 to that of 1927, turned forests into state property and customary use into privilege. Joint Forest Management and the Forest Rights Act of 2006 have begun, unevenly, to return a share of control to forest communities.
