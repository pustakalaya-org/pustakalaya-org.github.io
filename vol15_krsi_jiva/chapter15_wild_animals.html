    Chapter 16: Wild Animals: Hunting, Protection and Conservation¶

    16.1 Wild Animals beside the Field¶

    The farmers whose knowledge this volume describes never worked in a landscape empty of wild animals. Deer and antelope grazed their crops, wild pigs rooted in their fields, elephants raided their millet and paddy, wolves, leopards and tigers took their goats and cattle, and monkeys stripped their orchards. Wild animals were also food, a source of skins, horns, ivory and medicines, objects of worship and fear, and the quarry of kings. The history of wild animals in India is therefore part of agrarian history: the expansion of cultivation over thousands of years was also the contraction of wild habitat, and the long story of hunting, from the ritual and royal hunts of the texts to the colonial war on “vermin”, was in large part a story about who controlled the land and its produce.

    This chapter follows the larger wild mammals of South Asia from rock art and the Harappan seals, through texts on medicine, statecraft and ethics, to the royal, Mughal and colonial hunt and the rise of modern wildlife protection. Elephants are treated in Chapter 15, birds (including falconry) in Chapter 17, and forests, sacred groves and the Bishnoi in Chapter 11; this chapter refers to them rather than repeating them.

    One caution runs throughout. Texts that forbid killing, kings who protected particular animals and communities that revere particular species are an important part of this history, but they are not evidence of an ancient “conservation policy” in the modern sense. They coexisted, often in the same courts and the same centuries, with enthusiastic hunting and with the steady clearance of forest for the plough.

    16.2 Early Evidence: Rock Art, Seals and Names¶

    16.2.1 Hunters in Rock Art¶

    The oldest images of Indian wild animals are the rock paintings of central India, above all those of the rock shelters of Bhimbetka near Bhopal, a World Heritage Site. Their dating is debated, but the earliest layers are generally assigned to the Mesolithic. They show deer, wild cattle, boar, elephants, rhinoceros and big cats, often in scenes of hunting with bows, spears and traps, and later layers add horses, riders and armed warriors (Singh 2008). The animal bones from Mesolithic and early Neolithic sites, and from the earliest levels at Mehrgarh, show that hunting of gazelle, deer, wild cattle, wild pig and the onager long continued alongside the first herding and farming (Chapter 2).

    16.2.2 The Harappan Seals¶

    The square stamp seals of the Mature Harappan civilisation (c. 2600–1900 BCE) are carved with animals. The most frequent is the one-horned animal usually called the “unicorn”, which is not a real species; but the seals also show, with great naturalism, the zebu bull, the water buffalo, the elephant, the rhinoceros and the tiger (Kenoyer 1998; Possehl 2002). On a famous seal from Mohenjo-daro a seated, horned figure is surrounded by an elephant, a tiger, a rhinoceros and a buffalo, with deer or antelopes below the seat. John Marshall identified the figure as a “proto-Śiva” in his aspect as Paśupati, lord of animals; that identification is now widely doubted, and the meaning of the scene is unknown. The presence of these animals on seals does not mean that they were domesticated; it does show that the rhinoceros and the tiger lived in or near the Indus plains and that they mattered to the people who made the seals.

    16.2.3 Names in the Vedic and Later Texts¶

    The Ṛgveda knows many wild animals, mostly as images in hymns: the lion (siṃha), an image of terrifying strength; the wolf (vṛka), the archetypal enemy of the herds; the bear (ṛkṣa); the wild boar (varāha); the monkey (kapi); and the elephant, called the “beast with a hand” (mṛga hastin) (Jamison and Brereton 2014). The word mṛga originally meant any wild beast, as opposed to domestic animals (paśu); only later did it come to mean above all the deer and antelope, so that the king of beasts, the lion, is mṛgarāja or mṛgendra, and hunting itself is mṛgayā, “the pursuit of wild animals”. The tiger (vyāghra) is not mentioned in the Ṛgveda but appears in the Atharvaveda and later Vedic texts, and its skin is spread for the king in the royal consecration; scholars have often linked its arrival in the texts with the movement of Vedic culture east into the forested Ganga valley, though the argument from silence is not conclusive.

    Some of the commoner Sanskrit and Tamil names are listed in Table 16.1. Identifications are often uncertain, because the same word could denote different animals in different periods and regions.

    The blackbuck had a special place. A wild animal thus served as the boundary marker of a ritual geography — a reminder that the open plains of northern and western India, the jāṅgala of the medical texts, were once antelope country.

    16.3 Animals in Āyurvedic Classification¶

    The fullest systematic classification of wild animals in classical Sanskrit is found not in a work of natural history but in the dietetic chapters of the medical compendia, because meat was an important part of Āyurvedic diet and therapy. Caraka’s chapter on food and drink (Sūtrasthāna 27) groups the animals whose flesh may be eaten into eight classes according to their habitat and manner of feeding (Table 16.2) (Sharma 1981). Suśruta (Sūtrasthāna 46) divides meats first into those of dry-land (jāṅgala) and wet-land (ānūpa) animals and then subdivides each (Bhishagratna 1907).

    Table 16.2. The eight classes of animals whose meat is discussed in the Caraka-saṃhitā (Sūtrasthāna 27), with examples. After Sharma (1981) and Zimmermann (1987).
    Class Meaning Examples
    prasaha those that seize their food Predators such as the lion, tiger, leopard, wolf, hyena and jackal, and birds such as hawks, crows and vultures.
    bhūmiśaya those that lie in the ground Burrowing animals such as the porcupine and the monitor lizard.
    ānūpa marsh animals Large animals of wet country: buffalo, rhinoceros, boar, elephant.
    vāriśaya water-dwellers Fish, tortoises, crabs.

    Francis Zimmermann’s study of these chapters showed that they rest on an ecological picture of the subcontinent (Zimmermann 1987). Each kind of land (deśa) has its characteristic animals, and the qualities of an animal’s flesh reflect those of its habitat: the meat of the antelope and the deer of the dry, open jāṅgala is light, wholesome and the best for general use, whereas that of the heavy, slow animals of the marshy ānūpa is heavy and productive of phlegm. The ideal landscape of Āyurveda was a dry savanna rich in game (Chapter 1). The scheme is dietetic, not zoological — it groups the lion with the crow because they feed alike — but it rests on careful attention to where and how animals live. The therapeutic uses of meats and of other animal products in Āyurveda are discussed in Volume 13 (Āyurveda).

    Health and Safety Note

    The medical texts prescribe the meat, fat, horn, bile and other products of many wild animals. These prescriptions are described here as historical evidence. Many of the animals concerned are now protected by law, and the hunting of them and the possession of or trade in their parts are criminal offences under the Wild Life (Protection) Act; illegal trade in animal parts for medicine is one of the main threats to species such as the tiger, rhinoceros and pangolin. Nothing in this chapter is a recommendation to use any animal product as medicine.

    16.4 Mṛgayā: Hunting as Royal Sport and Royal Vice¶

    16.4.1 The Arthaśāstra¶

    The literature of statecraft treated the hunt with a mixture of approval and suspicion. The Arthaśāstra (composite, c. 50 BCE–300 CE) provides for an enclosed game forest (mṛgavana) for the king’s recreation, stocked with animals whose claws and teeth have been removed or which have otherwise been rendered harmless, and for forests in which animals are given safety; killing or injuring animals in protected forests is fined (Chapter 11, Section 11.3) (Kangle 1965; Olivelle 2013).

    In its discussion of the vices (vyasana) of the king, the Arthaśāstra stages a debate on whether hunting or gambling is the worse. An earlier teacher holds that hunting is worse, because it exposes the king to robbers, enemies, wild beasts, forest fires, falls, losing his way, hunger and thirst, and so to the loss of life. The text’s own view is that gambling is worse, and it credits hunting with real benefits: exercise, the reduction of phlegm, bile, fat and sweat, practice in hitting moving and stationary targets, and knowledge of the behaviour of animals when angry or afraid (Kangle 1965; Olivelle 2013). Hunting is thus treated as a kind of military training, a theme taken up in Volume 20 (Yuddha and Dhanurveda).

    16.4.2 The Law Books and the Epics¶

    The Manusmṛti (c. 2nd century CE) is less indulgent. Among the ten vices of a king that arise from desire, it names hunting first, and it counts hunting with drinking, gambling and women as the four most ruinous of them (Olivelle 2005). Its argument that meat cannot be obtained without injury to living creatures applies to the hunt as to the slaughterhouse, though Manu still permits meat in many circumstances.

    The epics are full of hunting, and many of their decisive misfortunes begin with it. In the Rāmāyaṇa, King Daśaratha as a young man, hunting by night beside the Sarayū, shoots at a sound he takes for an elephant drinking and kills the son of a blind ascetic couple; the dying father’s curse, that Daśaratha too will die grieving for a son, is fulfilled when Rāma goes into exile. In the Mahābhārata, Pāṇḍu shoots a deer in the act of mating, which turns out to be a sage who had taken that form, and is cursed to die if he ever approaches his wives. In these stories the hunt is a royal privilege whose excess brings disaster.

    16.4.3 Hunting in Kāvya¶

    Kālidāsa’s Abhijñānaśākuntala opens with a hunt. King Duṣyanta, in his chariot, pursues a blackbuck which flees, turning its neck to look back at the chariot, until hermits of Kaṇva’s forest stop him: the deer belongs to the hermitage and must not be killed (Chapter 11). In the second act the king’s general praises hunting in terms that closely echo the Arthaśāstra — it takes off fat, makes the body light and fit, teaches how animals behave in fear and anger, and gives the archer practice at moving targets — while the jester, worn out by the chase, complains of its hardships.

    Classical Tamil poetry, by contrast, knows hunting mainly as the life of the hill people of the kuṟiñci landscape, who hunted boar and deer, gathered honey and guarded their millet fields against parrots and elephants (Chapter 2). Across south India, hero-stones (naṭukal, vīragal) commemorate men who died defending cattle from raiders or fighting tigers and boar, and the dynastic emblem of the Hoysaḷas of Karnataka showed their legendary founder Saḷa killing a tiger (or, in some versions, a lion).

    16.4.4 The Mānasollāsa¶

    The most detailed Sanskrit account of the royal hunt is in the Mānasollāsa of Someśvara III (Section 3.8; (Shrigondekar 1925)). Its fourth book, on the king’s entertainments (vinoda), treats hunting at length among the other pleasures of the court: the varieties of hunting, the keeping and training of hunting dogs and the methods used for particular kinds of game. It describes an ideal of courtly life rather than particular hunts. Falconry and the Śyainikaśāstra are discussed in Chapter 17 (Section 17.3).

    16.5 The Ethics of Killing: Aśoka, Buddhists and Jains¶

    16.5.1 Aśoka¶

    The edicts of Aśoka (r. c. 268–232 BCE), whose provisions on animals and trees are summarised in Chapter 11 (Section 11.4), are the earliest datable Indian texts to criticise hunting. The Eighth Rock Edict states that in the past kings went out on pleasure tours (vihārayātrā) in which there was hunting and other such amusements, but that Aśoka, ten years after his consecration, set out for Sambodhi — the place of the Buddha’s enlightenment — and since then has made tours of dhamma instead, meeting ascetics and brahmins and instructing the people of the countryside (Singh 2008). The First Rock Edict adds that where once many hundreds of thousands of animals were killed each day for the royal kitchen, only three are now killed — two peafowl and a deer, and the deer not always — and that even these will cease. The edicts present the giving up of the royal hunt as a mark of a reformed king; they did not forbid others to hunt.

    16.5.2 Buddhist Teaching¶

    The first of the five precepts of the Buddhist lay follower is to refrain from taking life (pāṇātipātā veramaṇī). The discourses list trade in living beings and in meat among the wrong livelihoods for a lay disciple (Aṅguttara Nikāya 5.177). The position on meat was more nuanced: monks, who lived on alms, were allowed to accept meat unless they had seen, heard or suspected that the animal had been killed specifically for them; the monastic code forbade them the flesh of certain animals, including humans, elephants, horses, dogs, snakes and the large carnivores — lions, tigers, leopards, bears and hyenas. Some later Mahāyāna texts, such as the Laṅkāvatāra Sūtra, condemn meat-eating outright.

    The Jātaka stories of the Buddha’s former births, many of them older folk tales, frequently cast the Bodhisattva as an animal who sacrifices himself for others (Cowell 1895). In the Nigrodhamiga Jātaka (no. 12) the Bodhisattva is a king of deer who offers his own life in place of a pregnant doe chosen for the royal kitchen, and so moves the king of Benares to grant safety to deer and then to all creatures; in the Mahākapi Jātaka (no. 407) a monkey king makes a bridge of his own body for his troop to escape the king’s archers; in the Chaddanta Jātaka (no. 514) a six-tusked elephant king allows a hunter to saw off his tusks. Several of these stories were carved at Bharhut and Sanchi and painted at Ajanta. They are moral fables, not natural history, but they show a sustained effort to imagine animals as moral subjects, and the hunter in them is usually the villain. The Pañcatantra, by contrast, a collection of animal fables for the instruction of princes, probably composed in the early centuries CE and later carried through Middle Persian into Arabic as Kalīla wa Dimna, uses its lion king and scheming jackal ministers as types of human character; it is evidence of attitudes to power rather than of zoological knowledge (Olivelle 1997).

    16.5.3 Jain Teaching¶

    Jainism made non-injury (ahiṃsā) the first and most comprehensive of its vows. Jain doctrine classifies living beings by their number of senses and distinguishes mobile (trasa) from immobile (sthāvara) beings (Tatia 1994). The mendicant renounces all injury; the layperson renounces at least the intentional killing of mobile beings, which rules out hunting, fishing and occupations that involve killing. Jain communities have maintained animal shelters (pāñjarāpoḷ, “pinjrapole”) for aged, injured and unwanted animals, especially in Gujarat and Rajasthan, and Jain teachers sought, with varying success, to persuade rulers to proclaim days on which no animal might be killed (amāri). Jain sources credit the twelfth-century Caulukya king Kumārapāla of Gujarat, a patron of the scholar Hemacandra, with such prohibitions, and record that the Śvetāmbara monk Hīravijaya obtained from Akbar orders forbidding slaughter on certain days, including the Jain festival of Paryuṣaṇa. The scope and enforcement of such orders are hard to judge.

    Scholarly Note: Ideals and Practice

    The ethic of non-injury was real and influential. It shaped the diet of large sections of Indian society, inspired royal proclamations from Aśoka to Akbar, and sustains today the animal shelters of Jain communities and the protective practices of groups such as the Bishnoi. But it did not end hunting. Kings who patronised Buddhist and Jain monks continued to hunt; the Arthaśāstra and the Mānasollāsa treat the hunt as a royal art; and the killing of animals that damaged crops or took livestock was accepted almost everywhere. The relation between ideal and practice in this field is a long negotiation, not a story of a lost conservationist golden age (Alsdorf 2010).

    16.6 Hunting and Gathering Peoples¶

    Alongside the royal hunt there was the hunting of those for whom it was a livelihood. Many communities of forest and hill — among them the Chenchus of the Nallamala hills, the Bhils and Gonds of central India, the Santals of the Chotanagpur plateau and the Irulas of Tamil Nadu — lived wholly or partly by hunting, trapping and gathering. Specialist hunting and trapping castes, such as the Pārdhīs of western and central India and the Baheliyas of the north, served villages and courts as catchers of birds and animals (Chapter 17). The Sanskrit texts rank the hunter (vyādha, lubdhaka) low, and under colonial rule many such groups were classified as “criminal tribes” under the Act of 1871.

    Royal and colonial hunters depended on their knowledge of tracks, calls and seasons: the trackers, beaters and trappers of the shikār, the capturers of elephants and cheetahs, and the informants of colonial naturalists came mostly from these communities and were rarely named (Rangarajan 2001). Some communal hunts survive in modified form, such as the annual ritual hunts (sendra) of the Santals, now contested by forest officials. In the 1970s the Irulas, traditional catchers of snakes and rats, formed a snake catchers’ cooperative that extracts venom for the manufacture of antivenom. Forest peoples also worship the animals they live among, as with tiger deities such as Vāghobā in Maharashtra, or seek protection from them, as from the Sundarbans goddess Bonbibi, invoked by Hindu and Muslim honey-gatherers and fishers against the tiger. These traditions are treated more fully in Volume 23 (Jana–Jāti).

    16.7 Sultanate and Mughal Hunting¶

    16.7.1 The Ring Hunt and the Royal Chase¶

    The Turkish and Mongol traditions brought by the Delhi Sultans and the Mughals placed the hunt at the centre of royal life. The historian Baranī presents the long hunting expeditions of Sultan Balban (r. 1266–1287) as exercises that kept his cavalry in readiness, much as the Arthaśāstra had treated hunting as training. The most spectacular form of the Central Asian hunt was the qamargha, a ring hunt in which thousands of beaters, sometimes whole armies, gradually closed a circle many miles across, driving the game into an ever smaller space where the ruler and his nobles killed it. Abū’l-Faẓl records that in 1578 Akbar, during a great qamargha near Bhera in the Punjab, was overcome by a spiritual experience and ordered the hunt stopped and the trapped animals released — an episode he presents as a sign of the emperor’s spiritual elevation. The Āʾīn-i Akbarī justifies Akbar’s hunting as more than pleasure, an occasion for learning about the condition of the country and its people, and describes a variety of methods used at court, among them hunting with trained cheetahs and with tame decoy antelopes (Abū’l-Faẓl 1873).

    Bābur, who came to India from Kabul in 1526, recorded the animals of his new country with a naturalist’s curiosity, among them the rhinoceros, which he hunted in the jungles near Peshawar — evidence of how far west the species still ranged in the early sixteenth century (Thackston 1996). His great-grandson Jahāngīr (r. 1605–1627) was the most passionate hunter of the dynasty and the most careful recorder of animals. His memoirs note the game he and his household killed, with tallies broken down by species; record with pride that the empress Nūr Jahān shot tigers from an elephant’s howdah; and contain detailed observations of animal behaviour and of rare animals brought to the court (Thackston 1999). Near Lahore, at Sheikhupura, he built a tower over the grave of a favourite antelope, Mansraj, and forbade the hunting of deer in the area; the Hiran Minar still stands. Mughal painting is a major source for the appearance of wild animals in the period (Chapter 17).

    Mughal and later princely courts also kept menageries of local and exotic animals, a tradition continued by the public zoological gardens of the nineteenth century, such as that at Alipore in Calcutta (1876).

    16.7.2 The Cheetah¶

    The cheetah (Acinonyx jubatus) once ranged across the open plains and scrublands of northern, western and central India and the Deccan, where it hunted blackbuck and gazelle. Its history in India has been reconstructed by Divyabhanusinh (1995). Trained cheetahs were used for coursing antelope from before the Mughal period, and the practice reached its height at the Mughal court. The animal, hooded, was carried on a cart or on horseback close to a herd of blackbuck, unhooded and released for a short, explosive chase; when it brought down its prey it was rewarded with a share of the meat. Akbar kept very large numbers of cheetahs, ranked in grades with their own keepers, and the Āʾīn-i Akbarī describes their management in detail (Abū’l-Faẓl 1873). Jahāngīr records as a remarkable event the birth of a litter of cheetah cubs in captivity in his own establishment, observing that this had not happened before even though his father had kept so many (Thackston 1999).

    That remark points to the heart of the problem. As with the elephant (Chapter 15), coursing cheetahs were not bred but captured from the wild, generally as adults that had already learned to hunt, so that every hunting establishment was a continuous drain on the wild population. Divyabhanusinh argues that this long-standing capture, combined with the shrinking of the grasslands and of the blackbuck herds on which the cheetah depended and with the bounties paid on it as a predator in colonial times, drove it to extinction. By the early twentieth century Indian princes who wanted to continue coursing had to import cheetahs from Africa. The last reliable records of wild cheetahs in India date from the late 1940s — a widely reproduced photograph shows three young cheetahs shot by the Maharaja of Koriya in 1947 — and the species was declared extinct in India in 1952. The Asiatic cheetah now survives only in Iran, in very small numbers (Divyabhanusinh 1995).

    16.8 Colonial Shikār and the War on “Vermin”¶

    British rule transformed the scale and meaning of hunting (Rangarajan 2001). The shikār — the hunting of tigers from elephant back or from platforms (machān) over a tethered bait, with lines of beaters driving the animal towards the guns — became a central ritual of colonial and princely power. Viceroys, governors and visiting royalty were entertained with great tiger shoots, such as the one held for King George V in the Nepal Terai in 1911; British officers hunted wild boar on horseback with spears (“pig-sticking”) as a test of nerve and horsemanship, contested in competitions such as the Kadir Cup near Meerut; and Indian princes competed in the size of their tiger tallies, some of which ran into hundreds.

    Alongside the sport went a systematic campaign of extermination. From the 1870s the colonial government paid rewards for killing tigers, leopards, wolves, bears, wild dogs and other animals classed as “vermin” or “dangerous animals”. Officials justified the bounties as protection for human life and livestock — wolves, tigers and leopards did kill large numbers of people and animals in some districts — and as a means of extending and securing cultivation. Mahesh Rangarajan’s study of the bounty records shows that tens of thousands of tigers and well over a hundred thousand leopards and wolves were killed for reward in the half-century after 1875 (Rangarajan 2001); his study of the Central Provinces traces how the campaign against “dangerous” animals was tied to the colonial drive to extend settled cultivation into forest country (Rangarajan 1996). At the same time, the Arms Act of 1878 restricted the ownership of guns by Indians, and some historians have argued that the combination of disarmed villagers and protected game reserves left cultivators more exposed to crop damage in some areas.

    The consequences can be traced species by species. The Asiatic lion, once found across much of north-western, northern and central India, was shot out of nearly all its range during the nineteenth century, surviving only in the Gir forest of Saurashtra. The Indian rhinoceros, which Bābur had still found near Peshawar, had by the late nineteenth century vanished from the Indus basin and most of the Ganga plain, surviving in the floodplains of the Brahmaputra, north Bengal and the Nepal Terai. The cheetah disappeared. The swamp deer (barasiṅghā), the wild buffalo, the gaur and the blackbuck lost much of their range to cultivation, drainage and guns. Clearance for cultivation, plantations and railways probably did more lasting damage than hunting, but the two worked together.

    Colonial law began to protect some animals while their numbers were falling. The Elephants’ Preservation Act of 1879 restricted the killing of wild elephants — valuable to the state as working animals, much as they had been to the kings of the Arthaśāstra — and the Wild Birds and Animals Protection Act of 1912 provided for closed seasons and protection of specified species. Forest departments regulated hunting through licences and game rules in reserved forests, which had the effect of reserving the animals for officials and sportsmen while excluding local hunters (Chapter 11, Section 11.9).

    16.9 The Beginnings of Conservation¶

    Modern wildlife conservation in India grew partly out of the hunting culture itself. The Bombay Natural History Society, founded in 1883, brought together hunters, naturalists and collectors; its Journal published notes from all over the subcontinent, and its Mammal Survey of India in the 1910s and 1920s documented the distribution of species. Many early conservationists were hunters alarmed at the decline of game.

    Princely states created and protected some of the most important wildlife areas. The Nawabs of Junagadh protected the last lions of Gir from about the beginning of the twentieth century, restricting their shooting to rare, closely controlled occasions; a well-known story credits the Viceroy Lord Curzon, who declined an invitation to shoot a lion around 1900, with encouraging this policy. Princely hunting preserves became the cores of several later national parks: Travancore created a game sanctuary around the Periyar lake in 1934, and Jaipur’s preserve at Ranthambhore later became a tiger reserve. Such preserves often protected animals at the expense of neighbouring cultivators. In Assam, the Kaziranga area was declared a reserved forest in 1908, largely to protect the rhinoceros, and later became a game sanctuary; it became a national park in 1974 and a World Heritage Site in 1985.

    In 1936 the United Provinces established India’s first national park in the sub-Himalayan forests of the Ramganga valley, named the Hailey National Park after the Governor, Sir Malcolm Hailey. Among those who helped to define its boundaries was Jim Corbett (1875–1955), born at Naini Tal, who had become famous for hunting man-eating tigers and leopards in Kumaon and Garhwal at the request of the government and the villagers who suffered from them. His books, beginning with Man-Eaters of Kumaon (1944), joined accounts of these hunts to a deep knowledge of the forest, and in later life he turned from shooting to photography and to warning of the tiger’s decline (Corbett 1944). After independence the park was renamed, first Ramganga and then, in the 1950s, Corbett National Park.

    16.10 Wildlife Protection in Independent India¶

    16.10.1 From the Board for Wildlife to Project Tiger¶

    The Indian Board for Wild Life was constituted in 1952 to advise the central and state governments, and new sanctuaries and national parks were declared in the following decades, including the Gir sanctuary in 1965. But the 1950s and 1960s were a period of rapid decline. The clearing of forest and grassland for agriculture, the spread of firearms, the trade in skins and the opening of former princely preserves all reduced wildlife populations. The General Assembly of the International Union for Conservation of Nature, meeting in New Delhi in 1969, drew attention to the plight of the tiger; tiger hunting was banned in India in 1970.

    The Wild Life (Protection) Act of 1972 provided for the first time a national legal framework. It prohibited the hunting of animals listed in its schedules, regulated trade in wild animals and their products, and set out procedures for declaring sanctuaries and national parks. Later amendments tightened it and, in 2002, created conservation and community reserves (Chapter 11). India joined the Convention on International Trade in Endangered Species (CITES) in 1976. In the early 1970s the tiger replaced the lion as India’s national animal.

    Project Tiger was launched on 1 April 1973, with nine tiger reserves — among them Corbett, Kanha, Manas, Ranthambhore, Similipal and the Sundarbans — under the direction of Kailash Sankhala, with the strong personal support of the Prime Minister, Indira Gandhi. A count in 1972 had put the number of tigers in India at fewer than two thousand. The project’s model was a core area free of human use within a buffer zone of regulated use. The pugmark census of its early decades was shown by the ecologist K. Ullas Karanth and others to be unreliable, and was replaced by camera trapping and statistical sampling. In 2005 it emerged that the Sariska Tiger Reserve in Rajasthan had lost all its tigers to poachers, a shock that led to the report of a Tiger Task Force and to the creation of the National Tiger Conservation Authority in 2006. The official all-India estimates since 2018, based on camera trapping, have put the number of tigers at around three thousand or more; the figures have been welcomed as evidence of recovery and also questioned on methodological grounds.

    Project Elephant followed in 1992 (Chapter 15, Section 15.8). The campaign against a hydroelectric dam in Kerala’s Silent Valley, in which the lion-tailed macaque became a symbol, ended with the declaration of a national park there in 1984.

    16.10.2 The Lions of Gir and the Question of Translocation¶

    The Asiatic lion (Panthera leo leo, formerly P. l. persica) has recovered under protection from a very small population in the early twentieth century to several hundred animals, according to the censuses of the Gujarat Forest Department, and lions now range well beyond the Gir protected area into the farmland and coasts of Saurashtra. Recovery has brought conflict over livestock and the resettlement of many families of the Maldhārīs, pastoralists who lived with their buffaloes and cattle in settlements inside Gir, from the 1970s onwards.

    Because the whole wild population lives in one region, conservation biologists have long argued that a second population should be established elsewhere, as an insurance against epidemic disease or natural disaster; an outbreak of canine distemper virus that killed a number of Gir lions in 2018 lent weight to the argument. From the 1990s the Kuno–Palpur sanctuary in Madhya Pradesh was prepared as a second home for the lion, and villages were relocated out of it. The Gujarat government, which regarded the lion as a symbol of the state, resisted the transfer. In 2013 the Supreme Court ordered that lions be translocated to Kuno, but the order was not carried out.

    Debate: The Cheetah at Kuno

    On 17 September 2022 eight cheetahs brought from Namibia were released into enclosures in the Kuno National Park, Madhya Pradesh, followed in 2023 by twelve from South Africa, under a government plan to establish the species in India seventy years after its extinction. Supporters argue that the cheetah can serve as a flagship for India’s neglected grasslands and open forests and restores a lost member of the fauna. Critics, including several Indian and international biologists, have questioned whether Kuno and the proposed additional sites are large enough and hold enough prey for a free-ranging population; whether African cheetahs, a different subspecies from the extinct Asiatic animal, can be called a “reintroduction”; whether conflict with leopards, people and livestock outside the park can be managed; and whether the project diverted attention and resources from the translocation of lions to Kuno ordered by the Supreme Court. Several imported adults and cubs born in India died in the first years, which supporters attributed to the expected difficulties of any introduction and critics to flaws in design. The outcome remains uncertain.

    16.11 Wild Animals and Farmers¶

    For most Indian farmers the most important fact about wild animals is the damage they do. Crop raiding is as old as agriculture: the Tamil poems of the hill country already picture watchers guarding millet against elephants, boar and parrots, and the watch-platform (machān), the night fire, the drum and the sling remain familiar in the fields. Today the most serious crop raiders are elephants, wild pigs, nilgai (Boselaphus tragocamelus, the “blue bull”, which many villagers will not kill because its name associates it with the cow), several species of deer, and rhesus and bonnet macaques. Elephants, which raid paddy, sugarcane, banana and stored grain and can destroy houses, kill people in India every year and are themselves killed by electrocution, poisoning and trains; conflict with farmers is now one of the main obstacles to their conservation (Sukumar 2003). Livestock predation by lions in Saurashtra, tigers and leopards in central India and the Himalaya, wolves in the Deccan grasslands and snow leopards in the high mountains imposes heavy costs on herders, who are often among the poorest people in their regions.

    Responses combine guarding, trenches, walls and electric fences, early-warning systems for elephants, and compensation schemes run by state forest departments, often criticised for low payments and long delays. Section 62 of the Wild Life (Protection) Act allows the central government to declare an animal vermin in a specified area for a specified period, and this power has been used in recent years at the request of states — in 2015–16, for example, by notifications of the Ministry of Environment, Forest and Climate Change for wild pigs and nilgai in parts of Bihar and for rhesus macaques in parts of Himachal Pradesh — each time provoking controversy. Research by Vidya Athreya and colleagues in the sugarcane country of western Maharashtra showed that leopards live and breed in dense rural landscapes far from any forest, mostly unseen (Athreya et al. 2013), and that capturing “problem” leopards and releasing them elsewhere tended to increase attacks on people rather than reduce them (Athreya et al. 2011). Such findings have encouraged a shift from removal towards coexistence.

    Some agricultural landscapes are themselves important habitats. The blackbuck, chinkara and nilgai of the north-western plains, the wolves and Indian foxes of the Deccan, and many birds live among fields and on the grazing commons that official classifications long labelled “wastelands”. Where communities such as the Bishnoi protect wild animals on religious grounds, blackbuck and chinkara graze close to the fields (Chapter 11, Section 11.8).

    16.12 Conservation and Displacement¶

    The protected-area model adopted in India, as in much of the world, rested on the idea that wildlife is best protected by removing people. Villages were relocated out of national parks and tiger reserves — from Kanha, Sariska, Kuno and many other areas — and the rights of those who remained to graze, collect forest produce or cultivate were curtailed. Pastoral groups such as the Maldhārīs of Gir and the Van Gujjars of the Rajaji area in the Uttarakhand foothills, and Adivasi communities in central and southern India, bore much of the cost. Relocation has sometimes been voluntary and fairly compensated, in other cases coercive and impoverishing (Rangarajan 2001; Gadgil and Guha 1992).

    The Forest Rights Act of 2006 (Chapter 11, Section 11.11) recognised the rights of forest dwellers within protected areas and laid down that “critical wildlife habitats” could be made inviolate only through a defined process with the informed consent of the people affected. The relationship between that Act and the Wild Life (Protection) Act remains contested in courts and in practice.

    Debate: Inviolate Areas or Shared Landscapes?

    One school of conservationists, many of them field biologists working on large mammals, argues that wide-ranging, conflict-prone animals need large areas free of human use, as the tiger’s recovery in some reserves shows. Another, represented by ecologists and historians such as Madhav Gadgil and Mahesh Rangarajan and by advocates of forest rights, argues that exclusion has been unjust to forest dwellers, has made them hostile to conservation, and ignores the long coexistence of people and wildlife in Indian landscapes; they emphasise community-managed reserves, the knowledge of local people and the protection of wildlife outside parks (Gadgil and Guha 1992; Rangarajan 2001). Most participants accept both that some areas need strict protection and that conservation cannot succeed without the support of those who live with wild animals; the disagreement is about the balance.

    These questions of the rights and knowledge of forest communities are treated more fully in Volume 23 (Jana–Jāti).

    Summary¶

    Wild animals have been part of the Indian agrarian world since the hunters of the rock shelters and the seal-carvers of the Indus cities. Vedic and classical texts named many wild mammals, and Āyurveda classified them by habitat and manner of feeding. Hunting (mṛgayā) was both a royal art, praised as training in the Arthaśāstra and by Kālidāsa and elaborated in the Mānasollāsa, and a royal vice in Manu and the epics, while Aśoka, the Buddhists and the Jains made the renunciation of killing a moral ideal that coexisted with the hunt rather than replacing it. The Mughals raised cheetah coursing to its height; capture, the loss of grasslands and colonial bounties drove the cheetah to extinction in India by about 1950. Colonial shikār and the war on “vermin” devastated tigers, leopards and wolves, and the lion and rhinoceros survived only in refuges. Conservation grew from naturalists’ societies, princely preserves and the first national park (1936), and after independence the Wild Life (Protection) Act of 1972 and Project Tiger built a network of protected areas — at heavy cost to farmers living with crop raiders and predators and to forest dwellers displaced from parks.
