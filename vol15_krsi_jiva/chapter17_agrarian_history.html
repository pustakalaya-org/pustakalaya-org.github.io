    Chapter 18: Agrarian Society and Change: Caste, Land Grants, the Indo-Islamic World and Colonial Rule¶

    18.1 Texts and Agrarian Society¶

    The treatises discussed in the earlier chapters of this volume — the Kṛṣi-Parāśara, the Vṛkṣāyurveda texts, the elephant and horse books — describe how fields, gardens and animals should be managed. They say much less about who did the work, on whose land, for whose benefit, and under what compulsion. Yet agricultural knowledge in India, as everywhere, was embedded in a social order: in rules about who might plough and who might own land, in the obligations of tenants and labourers, in revenue systems that decided how much of the harvest the cultivator kept, and in political changes that brought new crops, new irrigation devices and new markets. This chapter sketches that history from the early centuries CE to the mid-twentieth century. It does not attempt a complete agrarian history of South Asia, for which readers should turn to Ludden (1999), the two volumes of the Cambridge Economic History of India (Raychaudhuri and Habib 1982; Kumar 1983) and, for the Mughal period, Habib (1999). Its aim is to place the knowledge traditions of this volume within the society that produced and used them.

    18.2 Who Worked the Land: Varṇa, Caste and Agricultural Labour¶

    18.2.1 Agriculture in the Normative Texts¶

    In the normative literature agriculture belongs, together with cattle-keeping and trade, to vārttā, the livelihood assigned to the vaiśya. The Arthaśāstra defines vārttā in exactly these terms and treats it as one of the four branches of knowledge a king must master (Kangle 1965; Olivelle 2013). In practice, already in the Mauryan and post-Mauryan periods much of the actual cultivation was in the hands of śūdras. The Arthaśāstra, in its chapter on settling the countryside, recommends founding villages consisting mostly of śūdra cultivators, and its crown farms under the superintendent of agriculture (sītādhyakṣa) were worked by slaves, hired labourers and convicts as well as by sharecroppers (Kangle 1965). Sharma (1980) traced how the śūdra, originally a servile category, came over the first millennium CE to be identified above all with the peasantry.

    The Pali canon gives a complementary picture. Its gahapati, the landed householder, appears as a substantial farmer who employs dāsas (slaves) and kammakaras (hired workers) on his fields (Chakravarti 1987). The well-known story of the brahmin Kasibhāradvāja, who was supervising a ploughing with hundreds of ploughs when the Buddha came for alms, shows a large landholder directing the labour of others; the Buddha’s reply turns ploughing into a metaphor for spiritual practice (Sutta Nipāta 1.4, vv. 76–82; Section 2.10).

    The Manusmṛti’s advice that brahmins and kṣatriyas should avoid the plough (Section 2.9) had lasting effects. In much of India it became a taboo against brahmins personally handling the plough. It did not prevent brahmins from holding land; it meant that brahmin and other high-caste landholders had their land ploughed by tenants and labourers of lower status. The prestige of not ploughing, and the stigma attached to manual labour in the fields, were an enduring part of the agrarian order.

    18.2.2 Peasants, Labourers and Agrestic Servitude¶

    From the early medieval period onwards, caste and agrarian position were closely linked, though never identical. In most regions a set of locally dominant cultivating castes — for example the Veḷḷāḷar of the Tamil country, the Reḍḍi and Kamma of Andhra, the Kuṇbī of Maharashtra, the Jāṭ of the north-west — held or controlled land, often alongside brahmin and temple holdings. Beneath them were tenants and labourers, many of whom belonged to castes that were treated as untouchable. In the wet-rice regions of the far south, the agricultural labour of the paddy fields was performed largely by groups such as the Pallar and Paṟaiyar in Tamil Nadu and the Pulayar and Cherumar in Kerala, who were held in hereditary agrestic servitude: they were attached to land and to masters, could be transferred with the land, and in Malabar were bought and sold. Colonial legislation (the Indian Slavery Act of 1843) withdrew legal recognition from slavery, but forms of bonded and attached labour persisted long afterwards.

    Detailed studies of particular regions show how such relationships worked and changed. Breman (1974) studied the hāli system of south Gujarat, in which Dubla farm servants were bound to Anāvil brahmin landowners, and showed how a relationship described by landowners in the language of patronage became, under commercial pressure, one of exploitation without protection. Prakash (1990) examined the kamiyā labourers of south Bihar and the way colonial officials came to define them as “bonded” labour. In the north Indian village of Karimpur, William Wiser described the jajmānī system, in which service castes (carpenters, blacksmiths, potters, barbers, washermen, leather-workers and others) received shares of the harvest from landholding patrons in return for customary services (Wiser 1936). Wiser presented these exchanges as a stable reciprocity; later scholarship has emphasised the inequality and coercion within them.

    The handling of dead cattle, of hides and of leather — indispensable to an agrarian economy that ran on bullocks and leather buckets — was assigned to castes such as the Cāmār and Mādiga and was a central marker of their exclusion. B. R. Ambedkar argued that the stigma attached to beef-eating and to the handling of dead cattle was a key element in the origin of untouchability (Ambedkar 1948); the history of cattle and caste is discussed further in Chapter 13. In irrigated regions, rights to water were also structured by caste: Mosse (2003) shows how the management of south Indian tanks encoded caste hierarchy in the allocation of water, the duties of tank servants and the honours of temple festivals (see Chapter 8).

    18.2.3 Women’s Work and Women’s Knowledge¶

    The agricultural treatises were written by men and address the male householder. Yet women’s labour has been central to Indian agriculture: in transplanting and weeding rice, in harvesting and threshing, in seed selection and storage, in the kitchen garden, in dairying and the care of animals, and in processing grain. Many of the work songs of the paddy fields are women’s songs. Much of the practical knowledge of seeds and of food processing discussed in Chapters 9 and 14 was carried by women. Their rights over land, however, were limited: under most customary and textual regimes women inherited land rarely, and only in the late twentieth and early twenty-first centuries did Indian law begin to give daughters equal rights in agricultural property (the Hindu Succession (Amendment) Act of 2005 made daughters coparceners). Agarwal (1994) has argued that independent land rights for women are among the most important determinants of their welfare and status in rural South Asia.

    Scholarly Note: Reading the Agricultural Texts Socially

    When the Kṛṣi-Parāśara advises the farmer to inspect his cattle shed or to supervise the sowing, it addresses a householder who commands the labour of others. Its knowledge is real, but it is managerial knowledge. The knowledge of the ploughman, the transplanter and the herder — much of which never reached writing — has to be recovered from ethnography, oral tradition and the observations of outsiders. Both kinds of knowledge belong in a history of Indian agriculture.

    18.3 Land Grants and Agrarian Expansion, c. 300–1200 CE¶

    The most abundant documents of Indian agrarian history for the first millennium and a half CE are inscriptions recording grants of land, mostly on copper plates. Kings and local rulers granted villages or plots to brahmins (brahmadeya, agrahāra), to temples (devadāna) and to Buddhist and Jain monasteries, often with rights to the revenue and sometimes with exemption from the entry of royal officials. The grants specify boundaries — trees, tanks, streams, anthills, fields of named cultivators — and so preserve a precise if fragmentary picture of the rural landscape (Singh 2008).

    Historians have interpreted the spread of land grants in different ways. One influential view, associated with R. S. Sharma and with D. D. Kosambi’s earlier work, saw in them the fragmentation of royal authority and the subjection of the peasantry (“Indian feudalism”); others have stressed instead the integration of new regions and peoples into state society, the extension of cultivation into forest and frontier land, and the role of brahmin settlements and temples in agricultural colonisation (Kosambi 1956; Singh 2008). What is not in doubt is that this period saw a great expansion of settled, plough-based and especially rice-based agriculture — in Bengal and Odisha, in the Deccan, and in the river deltas of the south — often at the expense of forest and of forest peoples, many of whom were absorbed as peasant castes.

    In the Tamil country, the inscriptions of the Pallava and Cōḻa periods show villages governed by assemblies: the ūr of non-brahmin landholders and the sabhā of brahmin settlements, grouped into localities (nāṭu). The famous inscriptions of Uttaramērūr (10th century) set out rules for the committees of a brahmin village assembly, including one for the tank. Temples were great landholders and creditors, financing irrigation and receiving produce (Singh 2008). In the Vijayanagara period (14th–16th centuries), as Morrison (1995) has shown for the region around the capital, the state, temples and local elites invested in reservoirs, canals and terraced fields, intensifying agriculture in a semi-arid landscape.

    18.4 Agriculture under the Delhi Sultanate¶

    The establishment of the Delhi Sultanate in the early thirteenth century brought northern India into close connection with the Persianate world of Central and West Asia. Its agrarian significance lay in three areas: revenue, irrigation and technology (Raychaudhuri and Habib 1982; Habib 2008).

    The Sultanate assigned the revenue of territories (iqṭāʿ) to military commanders in return for service. ʿAlāʾ al-Dīn Khaljī (r. 1296–1316), according to the historian Baranī, demanded half of the produce as land revenue in the Doab around Delhi, assessed by measurement, and sought to reduce the share taken by village intermediaries. Muḥammad bin Tughluq (r. 1325–1351) raised the revenue demand in the Doab, with disastrous results in a period of drought, and later attempted to extend cultivation through a special department and state loans. Fīrūz Shāh Tughluq (r. 1351–1388) built canals from the Yamuna and the Sutlej and laid out gardens around Delhi (Section 8.11).

    The period also saw the spread of new techniques and products, above all the geared “Persian wheel”, whose right-angled gearing let animals drive the older pot-wheel efficiently (Section 8.9; (Habib 2008)). Sericulture expanded, especially in Bengal; sugar refining and the distillation of spirits developed; and new fruits and garden plants arrived with the Persianate court culture.

    18.5 The Mughal Agrarian System¶

    18.5.1 Revenue, Measurement and the Crops of an Empire¶

    The Mughal empire (1526–1857, effectively to the early eighteenth century as a major power) was above all an agrarian state. Its revenue system, as reformed under Akbar in 1580 by Rāja Ṭoḍar Mal and others, assessed the land revenue on the basis of measured land and of cash rates fixed for each crop, derived from averages of past yields and prices (the zabṭ or dahsāla system). The great administrative survey of the empire, the Āʾīn-i Akbarī of Abū’l-Faẓl (completed in the 1590s), records these crop rates for the provinces season by season, together with prices, wages and lists of fruits, vegetables and flowers (Abū’l-Faẓl 1873). Read as a source of agricultural history, it shows a diverse agriculture: many varieties of rice, wheat, barley and millets; pulses and oilseeds; cotton, sugarcane, indigo, opium poppy and hemp; vegetables and spices. It also shows what was not yet there: the crops of the Americas — maize, chillies, tobacco, groundnut — were absent or marginal at the end of the sixteenth century and spread in the seventeenth and later centuries (tobacco reached Akbar’s court only at the very end of his reign) (Habib 1999).

    Irfan Habib’s study of the Mughal agrarian system remains the fundamental work (Habib 1999). It distinguishes the resident peasant (khud-kāsht) from the non-resident cultivator (pāhī-kāsht), describes the zamīndārs with hereditary rights over the land revenue of villages, and argues that the high revenue demand, levied largely in cash, pushed peasants towards cash crops and markets and, in bad years, into flight and revolt. The famine of 1630–1632 in Gujarat and the Deccan, described by Dutch and English observers and by Mughal chroniclers, showed how drought, war and the revenue demand could combine with terrible effect. Habib’s interpretation has been debated — some historians see more regional variation and more peasant prosperity — but the documentary picture of a commercialised agriculture embedded in a revenue state is generally accepted (Raychaudhuri and Habib 1982).

    18.5.2 Cash Crops and Regional Specialisation¶

    By the seventeenth century several regions were known for specialised production for distant markets: indigo from Bayana near Agra and from Sarkhej near Ahmedabad; cotton and cotton textiles from Gujarat, the Coromandel coast and Bengal; raw silk from Bengal; sugar from Bengal and the Gangetic plain; pepper from Malabar. European trading companies bought these products in large quantities, and their records, together with those of Indian merchants, show how closely the peasant producer was tied to merchant credit (Raychaudhuri and Habib 1982). Trade and crafts are treated in Volumes 17 and 19.

    18.5.3 The Village Order in the Deccan¶

    Records from the Deccan of the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries, under the Deccan sultanates and the Marathas, give an unusually clear picture of the internal organisation of the village. Hereditary rights and offices were called watan. The village headman (pāṭīl) and the accountant (kulkarṇī) held their offices and associated lands by inheritance, collected the revenue and represented the village to the state. Cultivators with permanent hereditary rights in their fields (mirāsdārs) were distinguished from tenants and outsiders who cultivated on temporary terms (upari). A group of village servants and artisans — traditionally reckoned as twelve, the bārā balutedār, including the carpenter, the blacksmith, the potter, the barber, the washerman, the leather-worker and the Mahār, who served as watchman, messenger and remover of dead cattle — received fixed shares of the harvest (balutā) from each cultivating household in return for their customary services. The balutā system is the Deccan counterpart of the north Indian jajmānī relations described above: it bound the village together in a web of reciprocal obligations, but on profoundly unequal terms, and it fixed the Mahār and other Dalit servants in the most degraded tasks. B. R. Ambedkar, himself born into a Mahār family, made the abolition of the Mahār’s hereditary watan service one of his early political causes in the 1920s and 1930s.

    18.6 Gardens and Horticulture in the Indo-Islamic World¶

    Gardens were not new to India. Sanskrit literature knows the royal pleasure garden (udyāna, upavana) and its pavilions, ponds and flowering trees, and the arbori-horticultural chapters of the Bṛhatsaṃhitā, Surapāla and the Upavanavinoda were written for such gardens (Chapter 4). Someśvara’s Mānasollāsa (1131) includes garden pleasures among the enjoyments of a king (Shrigondekar 1925). But the Persianate garden brought by the Sultans and Mughals had a distinctive form and ideology, and it transformed the landscape of northern India (Moynihan 1979; Wescoat and Wolschke-Bulmahn 1996).

    18.6.1 The Chahār Bāgh¶

    The classic Persianate garden is the chahār bāgh, the “fourfold garden”: a walled rectangle divided into four by raised walkways and water channels meeting at a central pool or pavilion, often further subdivided. Running water, fountains and cascades were essential; so were shade trees, fruit trees and flowers planted in the sunken beds. The form carried associations with the gardens of paradise in Islamic tradition, whose four rivers it was sometimes understood to evoke, as well as with royal power over land and water (Moynihan 1979).

    Bābur (r. 1526–1530), the founder of the Mughal dynasty, was a passionate maker of gardens. His memoirs, the Bāburnāma, complain that Hindustan lacked running water and walled gardens, and describe how he laid out gardens at Agra soon after his conquest, using water-wheels to raise water from the Yamuna (Thackston 1996). The same memoirs contain careful descriptions of the animals, birds, trees and fruits of India — the mango, the banana, the jackfruit, the rhinoceros, the parrots — written with a naturalist’s eye and a newcomer’s frankness. They are among the most important pre-modern descriptions of Indian natural history.

    18.6.2 Mughal Gardens and the Science of Water¶

    Under Bābur’s successors the garden became one of the chief arts of the empire. In Kashmir, the gardens of the Dal lake — the Shālīmār Bāgh begun under Jahāngīr and the Nishāt Bāgh built by his brother-in-law Āṣaf Khān — exploited the mountain slope to create terraces with cascades and channels fed by springs. In Lahore, Shāh Jahān’s Shālīmār garden (1640s) was supplied by a canal. The tomb gardens of Humāyūn in Delhi and of Mumtāz Maḥal (the Taj Mahal) in Agra applied the chahār bāgh plan on a monumental scale. Behind these works lay real hydraulic skill: the lifting of water by wheels, the regulation of channels, the construction of tanks and the management of gradients (Wescoat and Wolschke-Bulmahn 1996). Garden-making also stimulated horticulture — the cultivation and grafting of fruit trees, the introduction of Central Asian melons and grapes, and the planting of avenues and orchards. The pineapple, brought by the Portuguese from the Americas, was known at the Mughal court by Jahāngīr’s time.

    Jahāngīr’s observations of plants and animals, and the natural-history painting of his court, are discussed in Chapters 16 and 17.

    Two Garden Traditions

    The Sanskrit Vṛkṣāyurveda tradition and the Persianate garden tradition met in India. The first emphasised the care, nourishment and treatment of individual trees and plants; the second, the geometry of the garden as a whole and the control of running water. Gardeners (mālī) and water engineers working for Mughal, Rajput and Deccani patrons drew on both, and the gardens of Rajput palaces such as Amber and Udaipur combine the two.

    18.7 Persian Writing on Fields, Crops and Gardens¶

    From the thirteenth to the nineteenth century Persian was the language of government over much of India, and a large body of Persian writing bears on agriculture: revenue manuals and village accounts, the great survey of the Āʾīn-i Akbarī, memoirs and chronicles, regional histories and topographies, and a few manuals of agriculture and gardening. Little of it is agricultural instruction in the manner of the Kṛṣi-Parāśara or the Vṛkṣāyurveda texts. Its value lies mostly elsewhere: in administrative records that measured, counted and priced, and so preserved a picture of what was grown, where, and with what yield, more detailed than anything in the Sanskrit sources. Its limits are those of the revenue office. Like the Sanskrit treatises, it sees agriculture from above, and the knowledge of the cultivator appears in it only indirectly.

    18.7.1 The Āʾīn-i Akbarī as an agricultural record¶

    Akbar’s revenue reforms required uniform measures, and Abū’l-Faẓl’s account of them in the Āʾīn-i Akbarī is in effect a description of how land and harvests were to be known (Abū’l-Faẓl 1873; Habib 1999). Length was measured in the imperial yard (gaz-i Ilāhī); fields were measured with the surveyor’s jarīb, which, the Āʾīn records, was changed in the 1570s from a hempen rope, which shrank and stretched with the damp, to lengths of bamboo joined by iron rings; and the unit of area, the bīgha, was fixed at sixty by sixty gaz.

    The Āʾīn classifies land not by soil but by the regularity of its cultivation. Polaj is land cultivated for every harvest and never left fallow; parautī is land left out of cultivation for a time so that it may recover its strength; chachar has lain fallow for three or four years; and banjar has been uncultivated for five years or more. Polaj, and parautī when it was cropped, paid the full revenue; chachar and banjar were assessed at reduced rates that rose year by year to the full rate, an arrangement meant to encourage the reclamation of waste. The scheme is a revenue classification, but it rests on the cultivator’s own distinction between land in continuous use and land resting under fallow (Chapter 10), and the word banjar is still the ordinary term for uncultivated land in the revenue records of northern India.

    For land of the polaj class the Āʾīn gives, crop by crop, the yield per bīgha of good, middling and bad land. The average of the three was taken as the standard yield, and one-third of it as the state’s share. Converted into money at local prices, these shares produced the cash rates (dastūr) per bīgha for each crop in each assessment circle, and the ten-year averages of 1580 became the dahsāla rates (Section 18.5; the fiscal system is discussed in Volume 19). The crops covered run from wheat, barley, gram and other pulses, rice, sorghum and the millets, oilseeds and cotton to the high-value crops — sugarcane, indigo, opium poppy, betel leaf, melons and vegetables — each with its own rate. As a by-product of taxation, the Āʾīn thus preserves the most detailed crop and yield statistics of any pre-modern Indian state.

    Modern historians have used these figures in different ways. Shireen Moosvi built from them a statistical reconstruction of the output, revenue and income of the empire around 1595 (Moosvi 1987), and Irfan Habib mapped the distribution of crops, manufactures and resources in his Atlas of the Mughal Empire (Habib 1982). Both Moosvi and Habib (1999) concluded, with due caution about the figures, that yields per unit of land in the late sixteenth century were not very different from those recorded around 1900, while the area under cultivation was considerably smaller. The Āʾīn’s accounts of the twelve provinces note the characteristic produce of each, from the rice of Bengal to the saffron fields of Kashmir (Section 10.6). Its chapters on the imperial household describe the fruit-house, with the melons, grapes and other fruits brought from Kabul, Badakhshan and Samarqand and those now grown in India by gardeners from Iran and Central Asia; they list the fruits of India and their prices; the flowers of India, with their colours and seasons; and the perfumery, whose rose and other scents are discussed in Chapter 5. Other chapters regulate the imperial stables of elephants, horses, camels, oxen and mules, with the daily rations of each class of animal (Chapter 15). Taken together they are a record of the plants and animals of a sixteenth-century court as precise, in its way, as Bābur’s memoirs.

    18.7.2 Manuals of agriculture and gardening¶

    Persian writing on agriculture belongs to the wider Islamicate tradition of books on filāḥa (Persian falāḥat), “husbandry”. In Arabic that tradition goes back to the tenth-century Nabataean Agriculture of Ibn Waḥshiyya and includes the agronomists of al-Andalus, among them Ibn al-ʿAwwām of Seville (late twelfth century). In Persian, the Ilkhanid vizier Rashīd al-Dīn (d. 1318) wrote a work on agriculture and arboriculture, the Āthār wa aḥyāʾ, that took account of plants from India and China. Closest to India in time and milieu is the Irshād al-zirāʿa (“Guide to agriculture”), written in Herat in 1515 by Qāsim b. Yūsuf Abū Naṣrī Haravī. It describes the cultivation of grains, vegetables, flowers and fruit trees around Herat, the seasons of the agricultural year, grafting and the layout of the walled garden, and it shows how closely the Timurid chahār bāgh was tied to practical horticulture (Subtelny 1997). This was the world in which Bābur grew up, and his successors brought gardeners from Iran and Central Asia to India.

    Manuals were also written in India. The best known is a short Persian treatise on cultivation, the Nuskha dar fann-i falāḥat (“Treatise on the art of agriculture”), which deals with fruit trees, flowers and vegetables and is ascribed to Dārā Shukoh (1615–1659), the eldest son of Shāh Jahān. An English translation by Razia Akbar was published by the Asian Agri-History Foundation (Akbar 2000), which also published Surapāla and the Kṛṣi-Parāśara (Section 1.7); the text’s sources, date and authorship still await critical study. Persian versions of Indian works on animals — the Sālotar books on horses derived from the Śālihotra tradition (Chapter 15) and works on hawks and hunting — show the same movement of knowledge between Sanskrit and Persian that is better known in medicine and astronomy. Earlier still, al-Bīrūnī’s Arabic account of India (c. 1030) had drawn extensively on Varāhamihira’s Bṛhatsaṃhitā, the source of so much Indian rain lore (Chapter 3).

    18.7.3 Revenue papers and the language of the fields¶

    Below the level of the empire, revenue was assessed and recorded in village and district papers kept in Persian and in the regional languages. The best-studied series come from the Kachhwāhā state of Amber (later Jaipur) in eastern Rajasthan, whose arhsaṭṭā records, in Rajasthani, give for many villages the area under each crop and the revenue due from it from the later seventeenth century. S. P. Gupta and others have used them to reconstruct cropping patterns, the stratification of the peasantry, and the spread of new crops, among them tobacco and maize, in the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries (Gupta 1986). Comparable records survive from the Deccan in Marathi, written in the Modi script and full of Persian terms (Section 18.5).

    The administrative vocabulary of these records has outlived the states that created it. Kharīf and rabī for the two cropping seasons (Section 9.12), zamīndār and kāshtkār for landholder and cultivator, khasra for the field register, jamabandī for the record of rights and rents, girdāwarī for the seasonal inspection of crops and jarīb for the measuring chain are all Persian or Arabic words that remain in everyday use in the land records of northern India, alongside Indic terms that the Āʾīn itself used, such as banjar for waste. Colonial land administration inherited this apparatus and the village accountants (paṭwārī) who kept it, and built its own agricultural statistics upon it (Section 19.8).

    18.7.4 Observers and chroniclers¶

    Persian poets and historians also wrote about Indian agriculture as observers. Amīr Khusrau (1253–1325), in his long poem Nuh Sipihr (1318), praised India’s flowers and fruits above those of Persia and Central Asia. The Moroccan traveller Ibn Baṭṭūṭa, who served the Delhi Sultan Muḥammad bin Tughluq in the 1330s and 1340s, wrote in Arabic of the fertility of the land, which gave two harvests a year. Bābur’s memoirs survey the plants and animals of Hindustan with a newcomer’s frankness (Section 18.6), and Jahāngīr’s record his own observations as a naturalist (Chapters 16 and 17). Later topographical histories, such as Sujān Rāy Bhaṇḍārī’s Khulāṣat al-tawārīkh (1695), describe the provinces of the empire and their produce. These works are uneven as evidence, but together with the revenue records they make the Persian archive the richest single source for the agriculture of India between the fourteenth and the eighteenth centuries (Habib 1999).

    18.8 Colonial Agrarian Change, 1757–1947¶

    18.8.1 New Revenue Settlements¶

    British rule, beginning with the East India Company’s acquisition of the revenue administration (dīwānī) of Bengal in 1765, reorganised the relationship between the state, landholders and cultivators (Ludden 1999; Kumar 1983). Three main systems emerged:

    • The Permanent Settlement of Bengal, Bihar and Odisha (1793) fixed the revenue in perpetuity and recognised the zamīndārs as proprietors of the land, leaving cultivators as their tenants; tenant protection came only gradually through later tenancy legislation.

    • The Ryotwari settlements of Madras and Bombay (from the early nineteenth century, associated with Thomas Munro) assessed revenue directly on individual cultivators (ryots), at rates that were for long very high.

    • The Mahalwari settlements of the North-Western Provinces and the Punjab (from 1822) assessed revenue on village or estate communities.

    All three turned land into a saleable commodity, and debt and sale of land to moneylenders became a recurring source of rural conflict, as in the Deccan riots of 1875.

    18.8.2 Commercial Crops and Their Costs¶

    Colonial rule intensified the production of crops for export. Indigo was grown in Bengal and Bihar under a system that compelled peasants to cultivate it on terms dictated by European planters; the Indigo Revolt of 1859–1860 in Bengal, and Gandhi’s Champaran campaign of 1917 in Bihar, were responses to it. Opium was grown under a government monopoly in Bihar and around Benares for export to China. The American Civil War (1861–1865) created a cotton boom in the Deccan, followed by collapse. Jute in eastern Bengal, tea in Assam and the hills (worked by indentured labourers recruited from central and eastern India), coffee in the south and wheat for export from the newly irrigated Punjab were further examples (Kumar 1983; Ludden 1999).

    18.8.3 Famine¶

    The late eighteenth and nineteenth centuries were punctuated by catastrophic famines: Bengal in 1770, the Great Famine of 1876–1878 in the south and west, and the famines of 1896–1897 and 1899–1900. Davis (2001) has argued that the late-Victorian famines resulted from the interaction of El Niño droughts with colonial policies that prioritised revenue, exports and market principles over relief; Roy (2011) gives more weight to the droughts themselves and to the weakness of transport and credit. After the Famine Commission of 1880, the government adopted Famine Codes that organised relief works and food distribution, and famine mortality declined — until the Bengal famine of 1943, in which between two and three million people died in most estimates. Amartya Sen’s analysis of that famine, which showed that it occurred without a catastrophic decline in food availability and was driven by the collapse of the purchasing power (“entitlements”) of rural labourers, artisans and fishermen, has become a classic of economic thought (Sen 1981). In every famine the landless — agricultural labourers, many of them Dalits, and village artisans — died in disproportionate numbers.

    18.8.4 Colonial Agricultural Science and Indian Practice¶

    The colonial state at first regarded Indian agriculture as backward and in need of improvement. Its more perceptive observers, above all Voelcker (Section 7.5.2), found Indian cultivators skilled and placed the obstacles in shortages of manure, water, credit and fuel. The departments, research institutes and colleges that grew out of this concern, from the Famine Commission of 1880 to the high-yielding varieties of the 1960s, are the subject of Chapter 19; colonial botany, veterinary science and forestry are treated in Chapters 6, 13 and 11 (Arnold 2000).

    18.8.5 Plantations and Indentured Labour¶

    A different agrarian world grew up on the plantations. Tea was planted in Assam from the late 1830s and later in Darjeeling, the Dooars and the Nilgiris; coffee in Kodagu, Mysore and the Nilgiris; and indigo, before its decline, in Bengal and Bihar. Plantations needed large, disciplined labour forces in thinly populated regions, and planters recruited them from the Adivasi and low-caste populations of Chotanagpur, the Santal Parganas and the Central Provinces, often through contractors and under penal contracts that made leaving the estate a criminal offence. Mortality on the journey and in the early years on the estates was very high. The plantation economy thus brought into being a new class of wage labourers, uprooted from their own agrarian communities, whose descendants still form a large part of the population of the tea districts (Kumar 1983; Ludden 1999).

    18.8.6 Peasant Movements¶

    Colonial agrarian policy provoked resistance from cultivators, often in alliance with, and sometimes against, the nationalist movement (Ludden 1999). Among the best-known episodes are:

    • the Indigo Revolt in Bengal (1859–1860), in which cultivators refused to grow indigo for European planters;

    • the Deccan riots of 1875, directed against moneylenders who had acquired peasants’ land, which led to the Deccan Agriculturists’ Relief Act of 1879;

    • the Champaran (1917) and Kheda (1918) campaigns led by Gandhi, against compulsory indigo cultivation in Bihar and against revenue collection after crop failure in Gujarat;

    • the Eka and Kisan Sabha movements of tenants in Awadh in the early 1920s, and the rebellion of Muslim Mappila cultivators in Malabar in 1921, in which agrarian grievances against landlords were entangled with religious conflict;

    • the Bardoli satyagraha of 1928 in Gujarat against an increase in land revenue;

    • the founding of the All India Kisan Sabha in 1936, which gave peasant organisation a national form;

    • the Tebhāgā movement of sharecroppers in Bengal (1946–1947), who demanded two-thirds of the crop instead of half, and the armed peasant uprising in Telangana (1946–1951) against the landlords of the Nizam’s state.

    The colonial state also legislated to protect cultivators from losing land to moneylenders — the Punjab Land Alienation Act of 1900, for instance, restricted the transfer of land from “agricultural tribes” to others — in ways that were shaped as much by political calculation as by concern for the peasantry. These struggles placed land reform at the centre of the agenda of independent India.

    18.9 After Independence¶

    The independent Indian state inherited a stagnant agriculture, deep inequality in land and a recent memory of famine. In the early 1950s most states passed laws abolishing zamīndārī intermediaries; tenancy reform and ceilings on landholding followed, with very uneven implementation. Vinoba Bhave’s Bhūdān (“land gift”) movement, begun in 1951 in Telangana, sought voluntary donations of land for the landless. Large irrigation projects, community development programmes and agricultural universities were established. In the mid-1960s, after two severe droughts and dependence on food imports, India adopted the package of high-yielding varieties, fertiliser and irrigation known as the Green Revolution. Its scientific and institutional origins are described in Chapter 19, and its achievements and costs in Chapter 20.

    Summary¶

    The knowledge traditions of Indian agriculture were embedded in a society in which the right to own land, the duty to labour on it and the stigma of particular kinds of work were distributed by caste and gender. Normative texts assigned agriculture to the vaiśya but discouraged high castes from ploughing; much of the actual cultivation was done by śūdra peasants and by labourers held in various forms of servitude, many from castes treated as untouchable, and by women whose work was rarely recorded. Land grants of the first millennium CE document the expansion of settled agriculture. The Sultanate and Mughal states brought new revenue systems, canals, water-wheels, gardens and fruits, and left a Persian archive of measured fields and crop rates richer than any earlier record. Colonial rule turned land into a commodity, promoted export crops and presided over devastating famines.
